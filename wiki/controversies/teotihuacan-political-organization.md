## Description

The form of government at [[teotihuacan]] is one of the most actively debated questions in Mesoamerican archaeology. Unlike Classic Maya polities — where named rulers are depicted on stone monuments with hieroglyphic titles and dates — Teotihuacan leaves no identified royal portraits, no deciphered royal names, and no confirmed royal tomb. Yet the city was the largest in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica north of the Andes, organized with extraordinary spatial regularity over an area of approximately 20 square km. The question is: what kind of political authority produced this? The evidence is compatible with several quite different models.

The controversy also bears directly on periodization questions: many scholars see a mid-history shift in political form around 300–350 CE, associated with the desecration of the Feathered Serpent Pyramid (FSP). This putative shift adds temporal complexity to the debate.

## Position A: Corporate/Collective Governance

Blanton et al. (1996) proposed a "corporate" model for Teotihuacan, in which power was shared among a collective of elites rather than concentrated in a single ruler or dynasty. Evidence cited:

- **No unmistakable representations of individual rulers** in any durable medium — stone statuary, mural painting, or decorated ceramics — either early or late. As Cowgill notes: "There seem to be no unmistakable representations of rulers or other exalted humans in any durable medium" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 7).
- **No identified royal tomb**: despite extensive excavation, no burial has been identified as that of a Teotihuacan ruler with the certainty that exists for, say, Tikal or Copán.
- **Iconographic emphasis on categories, not individuals**: Teotihuacan imagery shows multiplicity and replication — identical costumed figures in processions — rather than individuated rulers. "This contrasts markedly with the Maya emphasis on variety and individuality" (Ch. 8).
- **Apartment compound investment**: Far more effort in the middle period went into substantial housing for the general population rather than into audacious new monuments, suggesting "a shift to less power for individual heads of the state, more sharing of power among high-ranking elites" (Ch. 7).
- **Cowgill's Venice analogy**: He suggests accession to rule may have been "by election by an elite council from among a relatively large number of elite eligibles, rather than from among only a few persons with close genealogical ties to the previous ruler. It may have worked something like the Renaissance Republic of Venice" (Ch. 7).

## Position B: Autocratic/Dynastic Rule (Early Phase)

Evidence for powerful individual rulers, especially in the city's early history (pre-350 CE):

- **Scale of construction**: The Sun Pyramid (~65 m high, among the largest structures in the ancient Americas), the Moon Pyramid, and the Ciudadela-FSP complex required mobilization of labor and resources on a scale that "suggest a very strong central authority" (Ch. 7). The Sun Pyramid was apparently built rapidly in the early period.
- **Human sacrifice at the FSP**: Approximately 200 individuals were sacrificed in multiple burial episodes during the FSP's construction, with victims showing evidence of being bound and killed ritually. The scale and organization of this sacrificial program implies "powerful individual rulers" (Ch. 10).
- **The Big Fist and the Colossus of Coatlinchán**: A gigantic carved stone fist in the Teotihuacan site museum suggests a colossal sculpture possibly representing a powerful ruler. The Colossus of Coatlinchán — over 7 m tall and weighing about 180 tons, found unfinished in a quarry near Texcoco — may have been intended as a ruler's self-portrait: "Could it be that the colossus was planned as a self-portrait by a very powerful autocrat who overreached and provoked rebellion and a new regime of more collective rule, perhaps at the time the FSP was desecrated?" (Ch. 7).
- **Comparison with Shang China**: Cowgill notes that late Shang rulers whose power is attested by inscriptions and massive sacrificial burials were also not represented in durable personal portraits — "the apparent low profile of Teotihuacan rulers is not unambiguous evidence that their power was restricted" (Ch. 7).
- **Analogy with institutional incentives**: In societies that emphasize ruler portraiture (like Classic Maya), this "celebration of rulers may have been a strategy to bolster the authority of individuals whose actual power was limited. Perhaps Teotihuacan's early rulers were too powerful to need to rely on self-portraiture" (Ch. 7).

## Position C: Mid-History Shift (Compound Model)

A widely shared view holds that Teotihuacan's political organization underwent a significant transformation around 300–350 CE, marked by the desecration of the FSP:

- **FSP desecration**: The Feathered Serpent Pyramid was systematically defaced and its iconographic program deliberately damaged around 300–350 CE. This is interpreted by many as evidence of a political revolution or regime change — the ending of one form of rule and the beginning of another.
- **Post-desecration construction patterns**: After the desecration, the city built no new audacious civic-ceremonial monuments. Instead, resources flowed into the apartment compound system. This shift is consistent with a transition from autocratic to more collective governance.
- **Colossus evidence**: If the Colossus of Coatlinchán was abandoned unfinished because of political upheaval — as Cowgill speculates — it would point to the overthrow of an autocratic project and its replacement by a different political ethos.
- **Possible mural representation of rule**: A figure in the stone frieze in the Avenue of the Dead Complex and a mural figure in the Atetelco compound have been proposed as representations of rulers, both probably post-250 CE, suggesting that rule continued but took a different iconographic form (Ch. 7).

## Key Evidence: The Absence Problem

A central methodological difficulty is that negative evidence — the absence of ruler representations and royal tombs — is ambiguous. It could indicate:
1. Collective governance genuinely lacking individual rulers.
2. Individual rulers who chose a different ideological self-presentation.
3. Representations or tombs that existed but have not yet been found or recognized.

A stone fist of colossal scale in the Teotihuacan museum suggests option 3 may apply: "A proportional body would have been several meters tall. There is no evidence of any such monument at Teotihuacan, but disembodied hands are not uncommon in Teotihuacan murals" (Ch. 7). The possibility that a destroyed ruler portrait has simply not been found cannot be excluded.

## Late-Period Complication: Intermediate Elites

Linda Manzanilla's work at Teopancazco introduces a further complication for the late period. She argues that "the growing power of 'intermediate' elites … may have significantly impeded the ability of the state to function effectively" (Ch. 9) — persons with status and wealth independent of the state, who increasingly siphoned off surpluses formerly accruing to central authority. This model is distinct from both corporate and autocratic models: it suggests a trajectory from (relatively) effective central authority toward factional fragmentation in which intermediate elites competed with the state rather than operating within it. Robertson's and Sempowski's data on growing compound-level wealth differentials in the Xolalpan and Metepec phases are consonant with Manzanilla's interpretation.

## Ciudadela, ranked sacrifice, and mural anonymity (Cowgill CHNPA 2000)

CHNPA Ch. 6 supplies load-bearing detail for both early autocracy and later collective-style representation:

**Palaces and marketplace.** The Feathered Serpent Pyramid is flanked by North and South
“Palaces,” widely thought residences of state heads. The Great Compound west of the
Ciudadela (same planning megacomplex) may have been a major marketplace. Millon
characterizes the Ciudadela as the political and symbolic focal point; construction shortly
after the Sun Pyramid suggests a possible shift in the ideological basis of rulership
without major discontinuity.

**Mass burials.** More than 130 persons found, total victims probably ~200 at construction;
most bound; more likely high-status Teotihuacanos in good standing (ranked costume/goods)
than stripped prisoners — implying strong, even despotic, central authority whether
accompanying a ruler or dedicated to a deity.

**Ranked costume categories publicly symbolize hierarchy:** (1) early-teen probable females
with few goods, often sets of 8; (2) slightly older males with points, pyrite-mirror disks,
shell “trophy” jaws — probable officers/bodyguards, sets of 18 (+2 to 20); (3) greenstone
ornaments including rattlesnake-rattle nose pendants, center group of 20; (4) robust male
with exceptional “Tlaloc”-type nose pendant in a looted pit; (5) other looted large pits may
have held state heads but looting prevents certainty. Early Teotihuacan was not egalitarian,
even in theory; what was unusual was **suppression of individuality** within levels.

**Later murals (A.D. 300–600/750):** humans subordinated only to deities, never other humans,
yet in elaborate non-everyday costume — hierarchy presupposed; individuality suppressed via
multiplicity/replication of identical figures (except late Techinantitla different glyphs).
Contrast with Maya naturalism and later eclectic [[cacaxtla]]/[[xochicalco]].

**Millon vs. Cowgill on the ~A.D. 300 turn.** Millon (1992) argues elite reaction to
autocratic excesses (climaxing in Feathered Serpent mass sacrifice) produced a new ethic of
collective leadership and institutionalized restraints on personal power ~A.D. 300, lasting
centuries, with possible late autocratic trend. Cowgill agrees leadership became less
enterprising/overbearing but suggests the change may have been **less institutional and more
about individual ruler personalities**.

**Standing interpretive tensions Cowgill leaves open:** (1) strong early autocracy (Ciudadela
sacrifices) vs. later collective/impersonal representation without clear rulers; (2) war
important but conquest never commemorated; (3) possible locus shift of active political
management from Ciudadela to Avenue of the Dead macrocomplex; (4) relation between
impersonal art and concrete social hierarchy “puzzling”; (5) dates may be off ± century.

**Shared factual ground (framed per side):** no deciphered royal names; no confirmed royal
tomb; mass dedicatory sacrifice at FSP (~200, military attire); apartment-compound corporate
residential units; multiethnic barrios; deliberate late burning of civic core. Disagreement is
over whether anonymity + compound corporate groups imply collective governance, whether early
monumental programs + sacrifice imply autocrats who later lost power, and where active
political management sat (Ciudadela vs Avenue of the Dead complex).

## Current Status

The controversy remains open. A rough consensus holds that:
- Early Teotihuacan (before ~350 CE) probably had strong, possibly autocratic, rulers responsible for the largest civic-ceremonial construction programs.
- The FSP desecration (~300–350 CE) probably marks a political transition toward more collective or council-based rule.
- Late Teotihuacan (Xolalpan–Metepec) operated under a more corporate or oligarchic system, with growing intermediate elite competition undermining state coherence.

But each element of this narrative is contested. Better definition of ceramic phases, discovery of elite tombs with clearer contexts, and more extensive excavation of potential palace complexes (like the Xalla) would substantially advance resolution. **Resolution status remains open** after EW2001 integration.
