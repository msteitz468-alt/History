# Philosophy versus Theology in Islam (the al-Ghazālī–Ibn Rushd debate)

The central confrontation in medieval Islamic thought between **falsafa** (the Hellenizing philosophy of
[[al-farabi|al-Fārābī]] and [[ibn-sina|Avicenna]]) and **kalam** (theology), crystallized in the exchange
between [[al-ghazali|al-Ghazālī]] and [[ibn-rushd|Ibn Rushd]].

## The positions

- **al-Ghazālī** ([[ghazali-incoherence-of-philosophers|*Incoherence of the Philosophers*]], c. 1095):
  the philosophers' metaphysics rests on unproven assumptions and contradicts revelation; three doctrines
  are outright unbelief ([[the-three-condemnations|takfīr]]) — the [[eternity-of-the-world|world's
  eternity]], [[god-knowledge-of-particulars|God's ignorance of particulars]], and the denial of
  [[bodily-resurrection-islam|bodily resurrection]] — and the supposed necessity of
  [[ghazali-on-causality|causation]] is really the customary act of God ([[occasionalism-islamic]]).
- **Ibn Rushd** ([[tahafut-al-tahafut|*Incoherence of the Incoherence*]], c. 1180): al-Ghazālī's
  refutations are dialectical, not demonstrative; the philosophers' core positions stand; and much of what
  al-Ghazālī attacked was Avicenna's Neoplatonic distortion, not genuine Aristotelianism. In his *Faṣl
  al-Maqāl* he argues that philosophy and revelation, rightly interpreted, cannot conflict.

## Significance and status

The debate framed the limits of reason and revelation in Islam for centuries and, through the Latin
translation of Averroes' reply, shaped [[arabic-philosophy-latin-reception|Latin scholasticism]]. Its
**resolution is contested**: a long-standing narrative held that al-Ghazālī's victory throttled Islamic
philosophy (challenged by [[decline-narrative-critique|recent revisionism]]), while the philosophical
tradition in fact continued in the [[post-avicennan-philosophy|post-Avicennan]] and
[[illuminationism|Illuminationist]] schools.
