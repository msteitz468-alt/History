## The Question

Did the adoption of agriculture make people *less* healthy than the foragers who
preceded them? The question matters because it undercuts the old progressivist
assumption (Childe's [[neolithic-revolution]]) that farming was an obvious
improvement — and it reframes the whole problem of [[why-did-agriculture-begin]].
This is the subject of Ch. 4 of CWH Vol. II (Charlotte Roberts), the
bioarchaeology of health and diet.

## The evidence

Bioarchaeology reads health from skeletons and teeth — markers including
**dental caries**, enamel hypoplasia (childhood stress), **cribra orbitalia /
porotic hyperostosis** (anaemia/iron deficiency), reduced stature, Harris lines,
infectious-disease lesions, trauma, and joint degeneration. The foundational
work was the Cohen & Armelagos conference volume *Paleopathology at the Origins
of Agriculture* (1982/1984), extended globally by Cohen & Crane-Kramer (2007).

**Why farming might harm health.** Sedentism brought refuse, vermin, contaminated
water, poor sanitation, and crowding (raising infectious-disease and zoonotic
loads); narrow cereal-based diets brought deficiencies (iron, niacin, vitamins
B/C) and a "mismatch" with the diet humans evolved on; heavy repetitive labour
(grinding, hoeing) and conflict left their marks; rising fertility increased
stress on women.

## Positions

**1. Health declined (dominant).** Cohen & Crane-Kramer: "evidence of health
decline through time is far more common than evidence of stability or
improvement." This is the standard reading and aligns with the volume's overall
"agriculture was not easy street" framing.

**2. Decline is real but variable and not universal.** The pattern is *not*
consistent across time and space. Counter-examples abound: dental caries did
*not* rise from Natufian foragers to Neolithic farmers in the Levant; rice
agriculture appears far less cariogenic than other cereals (no caries increase
in SE Asia; no Jomon→Yayoi difference in Japan). Cohen & Crane-Kramer themselves
warn "nothing more than broad generalisations should be expected." Roberts
stresses each site must be assessed individually; some studies show
*improvements*.

**3. The osteological paradox (methodological caution).** Wood et al. (1992)
argued that skeletal lesions can be *ambiguous*: a population with many
stress-marker lesions may indicate people who survived stress long enough to
form bone reactions (relatively robust), while those with few lesions may have
died too fast to react (frailer). "Health" cannot be read off bones naively —
hidden heterogeneity in frailty and selective mortality confound the inference.

## Resolution Status

**Partially resolved.** There is broad agreement that (a) the naïve "farming =
progress = better life" story is dead, and (b) on balance health and dietary
quality declined across the transition — the "first epidemiological transition."
But the decline was *variable, region-specific, and crop-specific*, and the
osteological paradox means specific skeletal claims must be made carefully.
Biomolecular methods (aDNA, isotopes, dental calculus) are expected to sharpen
the picture.
