# The Population of the Pre-Columbian Americas

A **standing controversy** of the wiki, sharpened by Noble David Cook's chapter
in [[bentley-cwh-v6-2015|CWH VI]]. How many people lived in the Americas in 1492
— and therefore how catastrophic the post-contact
[[columbian-exchange-1492|demographic collapse]] was — is one of the most
disputed quantitative questions in world history. The stakes are not merely
numerical: estimates govern how we judge the scale of the catastrophe, the
productivity and complexity of pre-contact American societies, and the moral
weight of conquest.

## The Dispute

The documentary record is sparse, late, and ideologically freighted (the Spanish
destroyed Indigenous records; chroniclers had reasons to inflate or deflate).
Estimates therefore depend heavily on **method** — back-projection from later
censuses, carrying-capacity models, depopulation-ratio assumptions, archaeology,
and tribute records — each carrying large, compounding uncertainties. Cook
illustrates the range vividly for the single island of **Hispaniola**: estimates
of the Taíno population at contact run from Verlinden's low of **60,000** to
Sherburne F. Cook and Woodrow Borah's "impossible" **7,975,000**, with measured
scholars (Livi Bacci 200,000–300,000; Frank Moya Pons 377,000–600,000) clustering
far lower than the high-counters.

## Positions

**1. Low counts** (Ángel Rosenblat, Alfred Kroeber, and earlier twentieth-century
estimates). Hemispheric totals on the order of 8–15 million, with modest regional
densities. Critics charge these understate complex agricultural societies and
rely on post-collapse observation.

**2. High counts** (the "Berkeley School" — Borah and Sherburne F. Cook; Henry
Dobyns). Tens of millions in Mesoamerica and the Andes alone; Dobyns proposed
hemispheric figures of 90–112 million. These rest on high depopulation ratios
(up to 20:1 or more) applied to early tribute counts, and on the assumption of
catastrophic, often "virgin-soil," epidemics preceding direct observation.
Critics find the multipliers and disease assumptions unfalsifiable.

**3. Measured middle** (Massimo Livi Bacci, Frank Moya Pons, and much recent
work). Intermediate regional figures, with explicit skepticism about both extreme
back-projection and the evidence for the earliest epidemics. Livi Bacci, notably,
found little contemporary evidence of epidemic disease in the very first
generation on Hispaniola, even as witnesses attest to other illness — implying
that starvation, violence, and forced labor, not only pathogens, drove the
initial collapse.

## Linked Questions

- **How much was disease versus violence and exploitation?** The high-count case
  leans on disease as the prime mover; skeptics (Livi Bacci) emphasize the early
  weight of starvation and brutal labor regimes.
- **When did smallpox actually arrive?** The first well-documented Caribbean
  epidemic is December 1518, but it may have come earlier (Cook notes a possible
  chain of infection from young carriers on the 1493 fleet) — bearing directly on
  whether pre-observation epidemics can be assumed.

## Status

**Open.** No consensus exists on hemispheric totals; the credible range still
spans an order of magnitude. There is wider (not universal) agreement that
population fell catastrophically after contact — by up to ~90 percent in the
worst-hit regions over the sixteenth century — and that both pathogens and
colonial violence were responsible. Method, not new documents, remains the crux.
