# The Three Chapters Controversy

The **Three Chapters Controversy** was a major theological and political dispute in the sixth-century Christian Church, initiated by the Byzantine emperor [[justinian|Justinian I]]'s attempt to reconcile the Monophysites of Egypt and Syria with the Chalcedonian state church. The controversy resulted in deep divisions between the Eastern imperial court and the Western Latin church, leading to the abduction of Pope Vigilius, the convocation of the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553), and a long-lasting schism in northern Italy.

---

## The Dispute

The core of the dispute was christological and ecclesiastical: how to reconcile the definitions of the **Council of Chalcedon (451)**—which declared Christ to have two natures (divine and human)—with the **Monophysites** (and Miaphysites), who rejected Chalcedon as Nestorian heresy and insisted that Christ had a single divine-human nature.

To appease the Monophysites without explicitly repudiating Chalcedon, Justinian issued an imperial edict in 543/4 condemning the **"Three Chapters"**:
1.  The person and writings of **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (d. 428).
2.  The writings of **Theodoret of Cyrrhus** (d. 458) against Cyril of Alexandria.
3.  The letter of **Ibas of Edessa** (d. 457) to Maris the Persian.

Because the Council of Chalcedon had formally restored Theodoret and Ibas to communion, Western theologians (particularly in Italy, Gaul, and North Africa) viewed Justinian's condemnation of these writings as a direct assault on the validity of Chalcedon. 

---

## Positions

### 1. The Imperial Court (Justinian and the Eastern Bishops)
Justinian and his court theologians argued that condemning the Three Chapters was necessary to purge the Church of Nestorian remnants and reconcile the millions of Monophysite subjects in Syria and Egypt, whose alienation threatened the empire’s unity. They argued that because the condemned texts were heterodox, the condemnation did not invalidate the Council of Chalcedon but clarified its true meaning. Empress Theodora strongly supported this policy, sheltering Monophysite clergy.

### 2. The Western Latin Church (North Africa, Italy, and Gaul)
Led by Western theologians like Facundus of Hermiane, the Western church rejected the imperial edict. They argued that:
*   A secular ruler had no authority to dictate Christian doctrine or condemn deceased church fathers who had died in peace with the Church.
*   Condemning individuals and texts accepted at Chalcedon undermined the authority of the council itself.
*   They refused to sign the condemnation, viewing it as an imperial capitulation to Monophysite heresy.

---

## The Capture and Coercion of Pope Vigilius

In 545, Justinian ordered his guards to abduct **Pope Vigilius** from Rome. Vigilius was brought to Constantinople, where he was subjected to years of intense imperial pressure. He initially refused to condemn the Three Chapters, but eventually yielded, issuing a document (*Judicatum*, 548) condemning the chapters while trying to preserve Chalcedon's authority. 

Faced with an uproar and excommunication by Western bishops, Vigilius retracted his assent and sought sanctuary in a church across the Bosporus. In 553, Justinian convened the **Fifth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople II)** without the Pope's presence. The council formally condemned the Three Chapters. Vigilius, sick and broken in spirit, finally submitted to the council's decisions before dying on his journey back to Rome in 555.

---

## Resolution and Legacy

The controversy failed to achieve its objectives:
*   **Failed Reconciliation:** The condemnation did not win back the Monophysites of Egypt and Syria, who soon established independent hierarchy under Jacob Baradaeus (the Jacobite Church).
*   **Schism in Italy:** The submission of the papacy to imperial dictate outraged northern Italian bishops. The metropolitan sees of Milan and **Aquileia** broke communion with Rome. While Milan returned to communion in the late 6th century, the **Schism of Aquileia** persisted until the end of the seventh century (c. 698), with Aquileia styling itself an independent patriarchate.
*   **Papal Weakening:** The controversy illustrated the extreme subordination of the bishop of Rome to Byzantine imperial power (Caesaropapism) under the Justinianic reconquest, which was only broken after the Lombard invasion forced the papacy to assert political autonomy.
