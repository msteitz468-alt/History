## The Question

When agriculture appears in a new region, is it because **farmers physically
moved there** (demic diffusion), or because resident **foragers adopted
domesticates and techniques** from neighbours (cultural/indigenous diffusion)?
This is one of the central structural debates of Neolithic studies, fought most
intensely over Europe but recurring for every region farming reached from
elsewhere (the Pacific, southern Africa, Japan, South Asia).

## Positions

**1. Demic diffusion.** Farming spread because farming *populations* expanded
and migrated, displacing or absorbing foragers. Classic statement:
Ammerman & Cavalli-Sforza's "wave of advance" model, supported by the
southeast→northwest genetic gradient in Europe matching the gradient of
Neolithic radiocarbon dates. Revived after 2005 by **ancient DNA** showing
genuine population discontinuity between Mesolithic foragers and the first
farmers (e.g. LBK haplogroup N1a, near-absent today).

**2. Cultural diffusion.** Farming spread as *ideas and materials*, adopted by
indigenous foragers who were the demographic ancestors of later populations.
Supported (1990s–2000s) by modern **mtDNA** suggesting only ~10–22% of European
maternal lineages arrived with the Neolithic, the rest being of Mesolithic/
Palaeolithic ancestry.

**3. Leapfrog colonization + assimilation (the synthesis).** Small pioneer
farming groups "leapfrogged" to favourable locations (river valleys, coasts),
then progressively **assimilated** surrounding foragers into the farming gene
pool. This is the model the archaeogenetics chapter (and Whittle's European
chapter, Cycle 6) regards as best fitting the data: real migration *and* real
indigenous contribution. Simulation shows the LBK had to absorb a substantial
Mesolithic component to grow at the observed rate; "2,000 years of parallel
societies" of foragers and farmers coexisted in central Europe (Bollongino et
al. 2013).

## Why it stayed unresolved so long

- **Different evidence streams said different things** at different times:
  classical markers → demic; modern mtDNA → cultural/continuity; aDNA →
  discontinuity/demic. Each technological wave flipped the consensus.
- **Modern genetics can't see the Neolithic cleanly** — later migrations overwrite
  the signal; a modern ancestry percentage is not a Neolithic demographic ratio.
- **Region-specificity.** The answer genuinely differs by place: strongly demic
  into temperate Europe; far more indigenous/cultural in the Pacific (see
  [[austronesian-dispersal]]) and in parts of East/South Asia where foragers
  incorporated crops into existing systems.

## Resolution Status

**Partially resolved.** The field has largely abandoned the demic-vs-cultural
*binary* in favour of region-specific mixtures dominated by the leapfrog-plus-
assimilation model, with ancient DNA as the decisive arbiter where it exists
(currently mainly Europe). The *proportions* in any given region remain debated.
