## The Dispute

What did the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucid kingdom]] achieve historically? Specifically, did
its [[seleucid-colonization|colonisation]] genuinely **hellenize** the Near East and
produce a fusion of Greek and native — or was it a thin, fragile veneer that left the
underlying "ancient oriental" society essentially unchanged? The question matters because
the Seleucid kingdom is the principal test case for the depth and durability of Greek
cultural expansion in Asia. CAH VII.1 ch. 6 (D. Musti) explicitly stages his discussion
against the previous edition's contributor, M. I. Rostovtzeff.

## Position 1 — Rostovtzeff's Pessimism

**M. I. Rostovtzeff** (in the first-edition CAH) judged the Seleucid achievement
**meagre**: the two desirable goals of city-foundation and hellenization were "only
scantily achieved." Local religions showed "impressive staying power"; Greek high culture
produced only a handful of names (the poet Meleager of Gadara, the historian Posidonius —
whom Rostovtzeff thought "much less Syrian" than his birthplace implied). On this view the
Seleucid empire's role in cultural history was merely as a **"vehicle of transmission"** —
a conduit through which Greek forms passed to Sasanian Persia, Christian Syria and
Armenia, pre-Islamic Arabia, and Gandhara, without itself becoming a creative
Graeco-oriental civilisation.

## Position 2 — Musti's Revision

Musti accepts that the pessimistic picture "cannot be fundamentally disputed," but
**fills it out and complicates it**:

- **Hellenization was socially specific.** It was usually a policy directed at the
  **aristocracies**, where fragile agreements could be struck; the classic case of its
  *failure* — running up against popular and religious resistance — is Antiochus IV's
  hellenizing of **Judaea** (the Maccabean revolt). But in already-hellenized western
  Asia Minor, Seleucid policy could reach down to the lower classes (Antiochus III's
  "popular card" against Rome). Hellenization "did not always and everywhere mean a break
  with the popular base."
- **Better evidence than Rostovtzeff had.** Two areas now look different: (1) the Seleucid
  **temple policy** was **more respectful** of temples, cities, and temple-lands than the
  old "ruthless secularization" thesis (Ramsay) held — the Seleucids appear as
  *conservers* of ancient temple-states; (2) the **spread of Greek (and Macedonian)
  culture** among the colonists themselves was "larger and more potent than was previously
  thought," as archaeology and epigraphy accumulate (e.g. **[[ai-khanoum|Ai Khanum]]**).
  This does not prove deep hellenization of the *natives*, but the cultural vitality of
  the Graeco-Macedonian settlements was real and widespread.

## The Deeper Characterization — an "Asiatic" Society

Musti argues that **hellenization is anyway not the right yardstick** for judging the
kingdom's success. Structurally the Seleucid state remained a society of the **"Asiatic"
or "ancient oriental" type** — a monarch who is "lord of the land" over an immense
*dependent (but not enslaved) peasantry* (the [[seleucid-empire|*laoi*]]). The genuinely
new forms — urbanisation, trade, and a *limited* development of slavery — grew up
alongside, but never displaced, this base; the kingdom never reached a fully slave-based
system (Syria instead became the great **slave reservoir for the western Mediterranean**).
The causes of decline, Musti holds, lie chiefly in this **internal structure** and in the
attraction it exerted on an expanding Rome — not merely in external aggression and
dynastic accident (Rostovtzeff's emphasis).

## Resolution Status

**Partially resolved.** No one disputes the staying-power of local cultures or the
thinness of Greek penetration into the native masses; the broad "transmission" role is
agreed. But the *degree* of hellenization, the depth of Greek–native fusion, the temple
policy, and the weight of internal vs. external causes of decline remain live questions,
shifting as archaeological and epigraphic evidence (especially from the colonies)
accumulates. The trend since Rostovtzeff is toward crediting the Graeco-Macedonian
*settlements* with more cultural vitality while keeping a sober view of the *natives'*
hellenization. Compare the parallel [[ptolemaic-economy|debate over Ptolemaic Egypt]] and
Préaux's "no mixed civilisation" thesis.
