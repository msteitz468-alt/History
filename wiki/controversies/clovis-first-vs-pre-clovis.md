## The Dispute

Were the makers of Clovis fluted points the *first* humans in the Americas, or
did an earlier population precede them? The question bundles three linked
problems: **timing** of entry, **route** (interior ice-free corridor vs. Pacific
coast), and the **archaeological status** of sites claimed to pre-date Clovis.

## Positions

### Position 1: Clovis-First (the classic model, 1930s–1990s)
- Clovis (~13,500–12,800 BP) is the founding culture; its tight ~600-year
  date span and continent-wide spread record the actual peopling event
- Entry was overland via the **ice-free corridor** between the Laurentide and
  Cordilleran ice sheets after the LGM
- Pre-Clovis claims fail on stratigraphic disturbance, contaminated dates, or
  non-diagnostic "artifacts" that may be geofacts
- Strength: Clovis is genuinely the first *widespread, unambiguous* material
  culture; the burden of proof on earlier claims is legitimately high

### Position 2: Pre-Clovis (now the majority view)
- **Monte Verde, Chile** (~14,650–15,600 BP), broadly accepted since the 1990s,
  is >1,500 km south of Beringia with a non-Clovis toolkit — humans must have
  entered well before Clovis
- **Paisley Cave, Oregon**: a human coprolite ~14,000 BP — the earliest widely
  accepted *direct* evidence of occupation
- North American contenders: Meadowcroft (PA), Buttermilk Creek/Debra L.
  Friedkin (TX, ~15,000 BP, below a Clovis level), Topper (GA)
- A **Pacific coastal route** would have been open before the interior corridor
  and fits early southern sites; most such shoreline sites are now submerged by
  post-glacial sea-level rise, explaining their scarcity
- The earliest Alaskan assemblages (**Nenana complex**) show no clear Clovis
  affinity, suggesting Clovis fluting developed *within* the Americas rather
  than arriving ready-made

## Key Evidence

| Evidence | Bears on |
|---|---|
| Monte Verde (~14,650–15,600 BP), non-Clovis toolkit | Strong pre-Clovis: entry well before 14,500 BP |
| Paisley Cave coprolite (~14,000 BP) | Earliest accepted direct human evidence; non-diagnostic |
| Buttermilk Creek lithics below Clovis (~15,000 BP) | Pre-Clovis if stratigraphy holds |
| Pedra Furada / Toca da Tira Peia (Brazil, 20,000+ BP claims) | Disputed; OSL not radiocarbon; most remain skeptical |
| Nenana complex (Alaska) lacks Clovis affinity | Clovis may be an in-continent development |
| Submerged Pleistocene coastlines | Coastal-route sites largely unrecoverable |
| <10 early skeletons continent-wide | Any single find could reshape the picture |

## Resolution Status

**Partially resolved.** The strict Clovis-first model is now rejected by most
archaeologists — Monte Verde settled that humans were in the Americas before
Clovis. What remains genuinely open is *how much* earlier, by *which route*,
and *which* of the many pre-Clovis claims are valid. Because pre-Clovis deposits
typically lack radiocarbon-suitable material and rely on the less-favored OSL
method, and because early populations were small, dispersed, and left buried,
taphonomically degraded sites, "true firsts" are extremely hard to pin down.
Waguespack expects the Clovis horizon to be pushed back further with new finds.

## Implications

This dispute sets the final benchmark for the [[paleolithic-global-dispersal]]:
the Americas were the last habitable continent reached, so dating their
colonization constrains the *end* of the global peopling process. It also bears
directly on the [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]] debate — a longer
pre-Clovis human presence weakens a simple "Clovis blitzkrieg" overkill model.

See [[colonization-americas-14500bp]], [[clovis-culture]], and the structurally
parallel [[australasian-colonization-timing]] (same early-vs-late, coastal-route
shape, on the other end of the dispersal).

## Monte Verde and the fall of the consensus (Mann)

Charles Mann's [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]] (Ch. 5, "Pleistocene Wars") dramatizes the moment the
Clovis-first orthodoxy broke. The decisive site was **Monte Verde**, a waterlogged riverbank in southern
**Chile** excavated by **Tom Dillehay** and Mario Pino (1977–85): a preserved village of hide tents,
firmly dated to **at least 12,800 years ago**, with more tentative hints of habitation **>32,000** years
ago. Because Monte Verde lies **10,000 miles south of the Bering Strait** — far beyond where people could
have walked in the few centuries the [[clovis-culture|Clovis]] model allowed, and probably *before* the
"ice-free corridor" opened (whose very existence new research questioned) — it implied arrival thousands
of years earlier, perhaps by a coastal/maritime route. The dispute was "forceful, even rancorous"
("People refused to shake my hand at meetings," Dillehay told Mann; "it was like I was killing their
children"). In **1997** a panel of prominent skeptics, **C. Vance Haynes** among them, visited the site
and publicly conceded it: "Monte Verde is real... a whole new ball game." But Mann records the
**continuing pushback** — **Stuart Fiedel** attacked Dillehay's final report as so poorly documented that
the provenance (and thus the age) of the crucial undatable **stone tools** could not be verified, and
Haynes himself then called for "further testing." The orthodoxy fell, but no firm new consensus on dates
or routes replaced it.

*Sources: Waguespack, CWH Vol. I, Ch. 19 — see [[christian-cwh-v1-2015]]; Mann, [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]].*

## CHNPA II.1 — Zeitlin & Zeitlin Mesoamerican framing (Ch. 2)

*Source: Robert N. Zeitlin & Judith Francis Zeitlin, CHNPA Vol. II Part 1, Ch. 2.*
Adds Mesoamerican candidate sites and the chapter's historiographic framing of Clovis-first
standards. For the process narrative of Mesoamerican peopling and Archaic aftermath see
[[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]].

### Clovis-first barrier and burden of proof

Until recently (relative to the chapter's writing), occupation of the Americas was held not
confirmable earlier than ~**12,000 years ago**, when Clovis fluted points appear widely in
North America. Clovis well dated not much earlier than **9500 b.c.** Clovis-first advocates
demanded pre-Clovis skeletal remains, diagnostic pre-Clovis assemblages, or human-altered
environments, preferably multi-technique concordant dating; they denied prejudice but pointed
to uncertainties at each purported early site. Early-entry researchers accused conservatives
of defending Clovis as New World "mother culture" with inordinately high standards.

### Monte Verde acceptance (~12,500 B.P.)

Dillehay's Monte Verde (southern Chile) excavations produced wood, bone, ivory, cordage,
lithics, and a human footprint; eleven associated radiocarbon samples centered ~**12,500 B.P.**
In **1997** a committee of nine specialists (including some Clovis-first proponents) inspected
the site and unanimously accepted genuineness and pre-Clovis age. An earlier, less-well-preserved
level dated **≥33,000 B.P.** remains under investigation. Aligns with Mann's Monte Verde
narrative above.

### No verified New World skeletons >11,000 ya (as of chapter)

Claims before 35,000–40,000 ya remain highly speculative; no recorded New World hominid
skeletons near that age. Of thirteen oldest known North American fossil humans (1985 sample),
seven re-dated by AMS — none over 11,000 ya. On-Your-Knees Cave (Alaska) ~9,800 ya; Kennewick
(Washington) ~9,300 ya (revised AMS 8750 B.P. noted in bibliography).

### Mesoamerican pre-Clovis candidates (none universally accepted)

Within the 14,000–35,000 ya bracket, Mesoamerican candidates include:

| Site | Claimed evidence (CHNPA summary) | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Valsequillo** (Puebla: Hueyatlaco, El Horno, etc.) | Lithics with Pleistocene fauna; Irwin-Williams preferred ~21,850±850 b.p.; experimental ~250,000 BP dates largely disparaged | Contested |
| **Tlapacoya** (Lake Chalco) | 17 dates 33,000–14,000 b.p.; accepted hearth 21,700±506 b.p. with tools and extinct fauna | Contemporaneity of artifacts and dates criticized |
| **Rancho La Amapola / El Cedral** (SLP) | Hearth 31,850±600 b.p. | Only three definite artifacts |
| **El Bosque** (Nicaragua) | Dates ~18,100–>35,000 b.p. | Tool authenticity and dates doubted; MacNeish saw "chopper" tradition >30,000 ya |
| **Loltún Cave** (Yucatán Puuc) | Crude stone/bone tools with horse, mastodon; only lowland candidate | No 14C; typology/fauna only (MacNeish 40,000–15,000 ya) |

By 12,000 ya Paleoindian settlement was unequivocally widespread; Clovis (including
Clovis-like points into Belize, Guatemala, Costa Rica) remains the secure basemark against
which earlier claims are measured. Dental (Sinodont) and mtDNA evidence for Asian origin and
multi-wave entry (possibly from ~40,000 ya) is summarized on
[[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]].

(Snow/Gonlin/Siegel 2020).

## CHNPA I.1 — Dean R. Snow (ch. 3): detection problems and collapsed claims

Snow’s Cambridge chapter (mid-1990s synthesis) is a strong statement of **methodological conservatism** on pre-Clovis claims. Dates and verdicts below are **as Snow states** (B.C./A.D.); later consensus shifted after Monte Verde’s 1997 panel acceptance (see Mann and Zeitlin sections above).

### Detection barriers

- First arrivals “were not numerous”; much of the landscape they occupied was later **inundated by the sea**.
- Tiny isolated finds are inconsistent with the **minimum social requirements of hunting bands** (need for multi-band networks of ~500 for long-term survival).
- **Radiocarbon caveats for pre-8000 B.C.**: bristlecone calibration covers most post-Pleistocene but not earlier; 14C half-life ~5,730 years yields large standard error at Upper Paleolithic ages; different organic materials yield different dates; technical errors “have tended to increase rather than decrease age determinations” — pre-peopling 14C dates “must be examined with considerable skepticism.”

### Window and verdict (Snow)

“Well-dated sites prove that Paleo-Indians were widespread in America by **10,000 B.C.**, but all evidence for an earlier presence is controversial.” Warming and retreating glaciers set the stage by 12,000 B.C.; rising water created Bering Strait by 10,000 B.C. — “an optimal window of opportunity” (though Bering Strait may not have been a true barrier; Eskimos and Australians cited).

### Collapsed or discarded claims (Snow’s list)

Old Crow (Yukon) bone tools once dated 20,000–41,000 B.C. (flesher 25,000 B.C.) were widely accepted, including by Snow himself; revised bone dating now less than 2,000 years old. Discarded or seriously questioned: Lewisville (TX), Old Crow, Calico and Laguna Beach (CA), Pikimachay (Peru), Sandia Cave (NM), Tule Springs (NV), Koch (MO), Taber (Alberta), and others.

### Remaining North American candidates (Snow)

| Site | Snow’s reading |
|---|---|
| **Meadowcroft** (PA) | Possible coal contamination for dates older than 15,000 B.C.; best remaining dates **8900–12,275 B.C.** |
| **Wilson Butte Cave** (ID) | Dates 12,500 and 13,000 B.C. on bone not clearly associated with artifacts |
| Latin American cluster | Taima-taima/Muaco, Pedra Furada, Monte Verde (principal ~11,000 B.C.; lower hearth claim 32,000 B.C.), Pikimachay — all “problematic” as of chapter |

Snow’s threshold: “for the moment, at least, it appears that all of the really convincing evidence for early Indians is less than **14,000 years old (12,000 B.C.).**” Frederick West, C. Vance Haynes, and Paul S. Martin “may well turn out to have been right all along.”

### Historiographic place

Snow’s chapter belongs to the late Clovis-first / high-burden-of-proof school. The wiki’s broader resolution status (**partially-resolved** against strict Clovis-first after Monte Verde 1997) supersedes his verdict on *whether* pre-Clovis exists, while his **detection-method and collapsed-site inventory** remain load-bearing for why the debate was so bitter and why many early claims failed. Dental/blood-group three-wave chronology and Siberian analogs are summarized on [[paleoindian-and-archaic-north-america]]. Companion concept page: [[clovis-first-controversy]].

*Source: [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-1]], ch. 3 (Snow).*

## From Evans & Webster 2001 — Mesoamerican Paleoindian (Thomas R. Hester)

Hester’s encyclopedia Paleoindian Period entry is a **Mesoamerica-focused** high-burden
assessment of Pre-Clovis claims, not a New World-wide verdict:

- Despite claims (notably by R. S. MacNeish) and localities such as **Loltun Cave**,
  **Valsequillo**, **El Bosque**, and **Tlapacoya**, there is currently **little solid
  evidence for Pre-Clovis occupations in Mesoamerica**; data are insufficient though such sites
  may eventually be found. Acceptance of **Monte Verde** (Chile) could push New World
  Paleoindian chronology to c. 18,000–20,000 ya.
- **Tlapacoya:** Lorenzo & Mirambell found fossil bones, stone tools (2,500 lithics including
  imported obsidian), possible cooking areas RC 21,000–24,000 ya on ancient Lake Chalco—
  artifact status accepted but association with early dates disputed.
- **Rancho La Amapola** (San Luis Potosí): hearthlike feature >31,000 ya; few lithics/worked
  horse bone associations debated.
- **Santa Isabel Iztapan:** two mammoth kills with non-Clovis points (including “Lerma” and
  rectangular-base types) and tools including contested obsidian prismatic blade; Tepexpan
  “Man” (actually adult female) may be contemporary with those finds (~11,000 ya by fluorine).
- Most Mesoamerican **Clovis points are surface finds**; none has been found in stratigraphic
  context, and none is associated with mammoth kills. Conservative first-people placement for
  the region: **11,500 ya** via Clovis distribution across Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, and into
  Central America.

This leaves Hester (2001 knowledge cutoff) aligned with **cautious Pre-Clovis skepticism for
Mesoamerica specifically**, while acknowledging continental Monte Verde implications—consistent
with the wiki’s **partially-resolved** global status (pre-Clovis accepted at selected sites)
without forcing Mesoamerican Pre-Clovis acceptance from weak local data. See
[[paleoindian-and-archaic-mesoamerica]].

*Source: [[evans-webster-archaeology-ancient-mexico-central-america-2001|Evans & Webster 2001]],
Paleoindian Period (Hester).*

## Fagan & Durrani (2020) — Monte Verde as accepted pre-Clovis; Clovis origins "a complete mystery"

Fagan & Durrani (2020) write from a position that treats Monte Verde, Chile as an accepted pre-Clovis site rather than a contested one, and flag that "current DNA evidence is suggesting a first settlement as early as 23,000 years ago" against a still-favored consensus date of roughly 15,000 BP for entry south of the ice sheets. On Clovis itself, they date the fluted-point tradition (named for Clovis, New Mexico) to roughly 11,200–11,000 BC (given elsewhere in their summary as 11,200–10,900 BC), likely rooted in Alaskan forager traditions (Hidden Mammoth, Mesa sites) using bifacial spear points as early as 11,800 BC, with no direct link demonstrated to the Siberian Dyuktai tradition. They are explicit that the popular image of Clovis peoples as specialized "big-game hunters" is misleading: Clovis populations exploited grasslands, woodlands, tundra, deserts, and coasts, and plant foods and fish/sea-mammal resources were locally as important as, or more important than, megafauna. Their toolkit included fluted points on foreshafts designed to snap off in the wound (paralleling Cro-Magnon foreshaft spear technology) and atlatls (spear-throwers), not bows. By 10,000 BC, foragers occupied "every corner of the Americas," with total population estimated at only a few tens of thousands. Notably, Fagan & Durrani decline to resolve Clovis origins, calling them explicitly "a complete mystery" — an epistemic-humility position consistent with, but more cautious than, the wiki's "partially-resolved" status above.
