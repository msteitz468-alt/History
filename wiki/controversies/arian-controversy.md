# The Arian Controversy

The great fourth-century dispute over the **relationship of the Son to the Father** within the Christian
Trinity — the defining theological and political conflict of the post-Constantinian church, from the
condemnation of Arius (c. 318) to its settlement at the Council of Constantinople (381). Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 19 (**Henry Chadwick**). Although a doctrinal dispute, it was
inseparable from imperial politics, since (as [[late-roman-church-institution|the church became a public
institution]]) the emperor was the arbiter of the faith.

## The question at issue

The Alexandrian presbyter **Arius**, excommunicated by his bishop **Alexander**, taught that the **Son is
subordinate to and posterior to the Father** — "true God" being the Father alone, the Son holding the
divine title "by grace, almost by courtesy," begotten of the Father's will to mediate between the
transcendent Absolute and the created world. The Son could in principle have erred; "the difference
between Father and Son is as important a truth as their affinity." Against this, **Alexander** and his
zealous deacon and successor **[[athanasius|Athanasius]]** insisted on the Son's **full divinity** — the
denial of which threatened the very possibility of redemption.

## Positions

- **Nicene / homoousian** (Athanasius, the West): the Son is ***homoousios*** — "identical in essence" —
  with the Father, derived from the Father's *ousia*. The **Council of [[council-of-nicaea-325|Nicaea]]
  (325)**, steered by Constantine, won almost all 220 bishops to this creed and anathematized Arius.
- **Arian / subordinationist** (Arius, **Eusebius of Nicomedia**, the "Eusebians"): the Son belongs to the
  created order, is of a distinct *hypostasis*, and is subordinate.
- **Homoian** ("the Son is *like* the Father"): the compromise formula favoured by
  [[constantius-ii|Constantius II]] in his "search for a creed" (Sirmium, 357–9), deliberately avoiding the
  contested terms *ousia* and *hypostasis*.
- **The East–West linguistic divide**: Chadwick stresses a genuine **conceptual difference** —
  the **Origenist Greek East** insisted on **three *hypostaseis*** (to avoid Sabellian collapse), locating
  their unity in agreement of will; the **Latin West** (and the over-zealous **Marcellus of Ancyra**)
  insisted on **one *hypostasis***, which the East heard as Sabellianism. Much of the bitterness was
  **terminological** — *hypostasis* meaning different things in Greek and Latin.

## The course of the conflict

Chadwick traces the dispute as much **political as theological**. Nicaea did not settle it: under
[[constantius-ii|Constantius II]] the Homoian/anti-Nicene party dominated, repeatedly **deposing
[[athanasius|Athanasius]]** (exiled to Trier 335, again 339, restored 346 by imperial fiat). The
**council of Antioch (341)** rejected both Marcellus and the charge of Arianism; the would-be ecumenical
**council of Sardica (342)** collapsed amid mutual excommunications and the East–West quarrel over **Rome's
right to hear appeals** from Greek synods. Constantius' victory at **Mursa (351)** brought the Homoian
**Valens of Mursa** to prominence and the imposition of Homoian creeds. The tide turned only after
Constantius' death: under [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]], the **Council of Constantinople (381)** reaffirmed
Nicaea, **sealed the defeat of Arianism** within the empire, and fixed the Nicene–Constantinopolitan creed
as orthodoxy.

## Significance and resolution

Within the empire the controversy was **resolved by consensus** in favour of Nicene orthodoxy at
Constantinople (381) — but **Homoian ("Arian") Christianity survived among the Germanic peoples**
(converted via **Ulfilas'** Gothic mission), so that the Visigoths, Vandals and others entered the empire
as "Arians," making the creed a marker of barbarian–Roman religious difference for centuries. Chadwick's
chapter shows the dispute as the **forge of Christian orthodoxy** — the process by which the church, now a
public institution under imperial patronage, hammered out its central doctrine through councils whose
authority rested jointly on episcopal consensus and imperial ratification, at the cost of deep and lasting
**East–West** mistrust over both doctrine and the authority of Rome.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] narrates the controversy from “the school of Plato, to the decline and fall of the empire” — as politics of court, post, and capital blood as much as theology. **Positions below are Gibbon’s**, not wiki adjudication.

- **Scope:** more diffuse than Donatism; from Constantine to Clovis/Theodoric, temporal interests of Romans and Barbarians “deeply involved in Arian disputes.”
- **Constantine’s ecclesiastical levity:** “while he protected Arius, and persecuted Athanasius, he still considered the council of Nice as the bulwark of the Christian faith” and glory of his reign; baptized by an Arian bishop.
- **Constantius’s synodal traffic:** Ammianus — plain Christianity confounded by superstition; posts “almost ruined by their hasty and repeated journeys”; eunuchs, women, bishops against Homoousion.
- **Athanasius:** immortal name inseparable from Catholic Trinity; 46-year administration; five expulsions; “superiority of character and abilities” that would have qualified him better than Constantine’s sons to govern a monarchy.
- **First Christian prince resisted by religious principle:** Constantius, victor over Gallus, Silvanus, Vetranio, Magnentius, “experienced the strength of those principles, which, in the cause of religion, could resist the most absolute of sovereigns.”
- **Constantinople’s first blood ecclesiastical:** Paul vs Macedonius; Hermogenes dragged to death; ~3,150 dead at Macedonius’s installation — “The first blood which stained the new capital was spilt in this ecclesiastical contest.”
- **Valentinian’s western truce vs Valens’s eastern Arianism**; **Julian’s recall of banished bishops** to exploit mutual hatred (see [[concepts/arianism]]).
