# Were There States in North America North of Mexico?

## The Dispute

Did **state-level political organization** exist in Native North America north of Mesoamerica
before European contact — or were the most elaborate societies (Chaco, Hohokam, Cahokia)
necessarily "intermediate," falling short of states? In [[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] this
is the central historiographical conflict of the North American chapters (Begun & Brashler,
Ch 20; Lekson, Ch 21), and a genuine interpretive dispute about how to classify
[[chaco-canyon|Chaco Canyon]] and its peers.

## Positions

**1. No states north of Mexico — "the good gray intermediate."**
The long-dominant view holds that Native societies north of Mesoamerica were, and always had
been, **simple, egalitarian, and communal** — at most "chiefdoms" or "intermediate" societies.
A panel of distinguished archaeologists concluded of the ancient Pueblo Southwest that "we
can't even pretend [they] formed states." *Strength:* fits the ethnographically known modern
Pueblos, whose institutions are egalitarian and ritual-based. *Weakness (per Lekson):* this
view is the inheritance of eighteenth–nineteenth-century **colonial and racist biases** that
painted North American natives as history-less "savages," and it relies on **projecting modern
Pueblo ethnography** ("Pueblo Space") backward onto a very different past.

**2. Secondary states existed — Chaco as *altepetl* (Lekson).**
Lekson argues that Chaco was a **secondary state**, a local translation of the Mesoamerican
**altepetl** (a multi-noble-family city-state), with stratified palaces vs. commoner
farmsteads, a capital city, an 80,000 km² regional system, and dense **Mesoamerican
connections** (turquoise exports; copper, macaw, and cacao imports). North America, on this
view, was "awash with states and empires" and must be read on **continental scales** — a New
World history. *Strength:* the altepetl is a real, contemporary, demonstrably connected Native
form, not an invented "uniquity" or an ethnographic anachronism. *Weakness:* Chaco's spatial
scale dwarfs any real altepetl, and material culture, ideology, and domestic life differed
sharply; critics see emulation or a distinctive ritual polity rather than a transplanted state.

## CWH IV's Framing

The two chapters lean toward **upgrading** North American complexity. Begun & Brashler treat
the Southwest's elites and hierarchy as newly recognized realities; Lekson presses the
**altepetl** thesis hard, explicitly to "re-situate the region on a continent awash with states
and empires." Per the Contradiction Protocol, the orthodox "intermediate" position is recorded
here with its arguments and is not adjudicated.

## Resolution Status

**Open.** Lekson concedes the **range of interpretations of Chaco is "staggering" and
unreconciled** — village, pilgrimage center, chiefdom, regional capital, or altepetl. No
consensus exists on whether the label "state" applies north of Mexico; the dispute turns on
definitions of the state, the weight given to Mesoamerican connections, and the proper role of
Pueblo ethnography in interpreting the deep past.
