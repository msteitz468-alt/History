# Postmodernism and Historical Knowledge

Is objective knowledge of the past possible, or is history only one discourse among
many? The defining methodological dispute of 1980s–90s Anglo-American historiography,
superseding the Carr–Elton debate of the 1960s. Concept background:
[[postmodernism-and-history]], [[historical-objectivity]]. Recorded here primarily
through Evans (1997), an engaged partisan of the middle position — his
characterizations of opponents should be read as such.

## Position 1 — Radical skepticism

Historical knowledge in the traditional sense is impossible; sources are texts
referring to other texts; interpretations are chosen on moral/aesthetic/political,
not evidential, grounds.

- **Hayden White** (strong early form): "There is... no single correct view of any
  event or process... the only grounds for preferring one over another are moral or
  aesthetic ones"; contradictory stories can be told "without violating rules of
  evidence or critical standards."
- **Keith Jenkins**: the primary/secondary source distinction "fetishizes documents";
  "when we study history we are not studying the past but what historians have
  constructed about the past"; professional history expresses dominant ideology.
- **Frank Ankersmit**: "Content is a derivative of style"; historiography as
  "continuous playing with the memory" of the past; "Autumn... has come to Western
  historiography."
- Best arguments: archives are products of survival-chance and archivists' elite-biased
  selection; narrative form is imposed, not found; historians' theory-choice is
  underdetermined by evidence; the discipline's "objectivity" has historically served
  nationalism and empire (see [[rankean-historicism]]).

## Position 2 — Empiricist rejection

The challenge is destructive nonsense; the documentary record, approached without
preconceptions, yields truth.

- **Geoffrey Elton**: postmodernism as "menacing," "the intellectual equivalent of
  crack," total relativism "the ultimate heresy"; good history is "definitive."
- **Arthur Marwick**: a "menace to serious historical study." **Lawrence Stone**:
  history risked "becoming an endangered species." **Raphael Samuel** (from the
  socialist left): history-as-invention "completely unacceptable."
- Best arguments: the discipline's shared verification method (footnoting, authorship
  and wording checks) demonstrably works across all theoretical schools; scholarly
  frauds and errors are in practice detected and corrected (see [[abraham-affair]]).

## Position 3 — The moderate middle (Evans; Appleby, Hunt and Jacob; LaCapra; later White)

Facts precede interpretation conceptually; interpretation precedes evidence
(Evans's formula). Documents constrain but do not dictate; research is a "dialogue"
with disconcerting voices from the past (LaCapra). Objectivity ≠ neutrality (Haskell).
Knowledge is "partial and provisional... but nevertheless true"; conclusions "tenable
though always less than final." Postmodernism's contributions (reflexivity, language,
new subjects, narrative revival) are accepted; its strong epistemology rejected as
self-refuting (Boghossian).

## Key Episodes

- **The Stalybridge Wakes gingerbread-seller** — Carr's exemplary "fact" (a seller
  kicked to death in 1850, a fact only once historians cited it). Evans's archival
  check found contemporary newspapers reporting the Wakes fully with **no violent
  incident** — undermining both Carr's constructivism and Kitson Clark's use of the
  anecdote, and illustrating the fact/evidence distinction working as a discipline.
- **The de Man affair (1987)** — deconstruction's leading American exponent revealed
  as a wartime contributor to a Nazi-controlled paper; his defenders' simultaneous
  appeal to infinite interpretability and to "setting the record straight" widely read
  as discrediting; LaCapra: the debate showed "deconstruction can be used to justify
  or rewrite anything."
- **White's retreat** — in Friedländer's *Probing the Limits of Representation*, White
  conceded the facts of the Holocaust "closed off" certain emplotments, effectively
  abandoning the strong thesis. Holocaust denial functioned throughout as the
  limit case establishing that fact-level truth is non-negotiable (Lipstadt; Evans:
  "Auschwitz was not a discourse").
- **The Abraham affair** (see [[abraham-affair]]) — cited by both sides: by
  postmodernists as evidence that evidentiary standards are politically policed, by
  empiricists as evidence that interpretations "really can be tested and confirmed or
  falsified by an appeal to the evidence" (Evans).

## Resolution Status

Partially resolved by attrition and absorption: the strong skeptical claims lost their
leading advocate (White's retreat) and failed the denial limit case; the discipline
absorbed the linguistic turn's methods while retaining verification norms.
Postmodernist history settled into a sub-specialism (cultural/intellectual history) —
the trajectory Evans predicted. The philosophical question is not closed; Jenkins,
Munslow and others rejected Evans's account (2000 afterword exchange, not yet in the
source base).
