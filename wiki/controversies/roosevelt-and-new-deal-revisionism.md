# Roosevelt and New Deal Revisionism

Was Franklin D. Roosevelt a great president whose leadership carried the United
States through its two gravest twentieth-century crises — or is that reputation
substantially a manufactured image concealing a record of economic failure,
constitutional aggrandizement, and diplomatic defeat? This is one of the most
durable interpretive disputes in American historiography. It is **open as public
discourse** — the revisionist literature has never stopped selling or being
written — while **largely closed within the academy**, where scholarly surveys
consistently rank [[roosevelt-franklin-d|Roosevelt]] among the top three
presidents. Both facts are recorded here as facts about the debate, not as a
verdict on it.

## Position 1 — The revisionist critique (Flynn and the Old Right)

The founding statement is John T. Flynn, *[[flynn-roosevelt-myth-1948|The
Roosevelt Myth]]* (1948), a national bestseller by a former *New Republic*
economics columnist who had broken with the administration over the NRA. In its
strongest form the case runs:

- **The image was manufactured.** The public Roosevelt — "that noble, selfless,
  hard-headed, wise and farseeing combination of philosopher, philanthropist and
  warrior" — "has been fabricated out of pure propaganda," amplified by wartime
  emotion; the task of history is to restore "the Franklin D. Roosevelt of the
  years 1932 to 1945 in his normal dimensions" (Flynn 1948, foreword and closing
  chapter).
- **The New Deal did not end the Depression.** Unemployment remained near ten
  million on the eve of rearmament; the 1937–38 collapse ("the forgotten
  depression") showed the recovery rested on deficit spending rather than
  restored private investment; only war spending ended the slump. The
  administration proceeded by improvisation — Flynn's Books One and Two are
  titled "Trial—and Error" and "Confusion" — not by design.
- **Power was permanently centralized.** The [[court-packing-fight-1937|court-
  packing attempt]], the third term, executive agencies, and wartime controls
  effected a lasting constitutional shift toward the presidency, entrenched by
  a spending politics that built a permanent client electorate.
- **Wartime diplomacy was capitulation.** The Atlantic Charter's principles
  were serially abandoned; at Teheran and [[yalta-conference-1945|Yalta]],
  Roosevelt — ailing, over-confident in personal charm, and poorly staffed —
  conceded Eastern Europe and much of Northeast Asia to Stalin, betraying
  "every promise" made to Poland, the Baltic states, and China. Strong-man
  realists (Churchill, Stalin) held him to his pledges; the peoples who trusted
  him were the ones betrayed.
- **The 1944 statement — the New Deal as structural fascism.** Four years
  before *The Roosevelt Myth*, Flynn's *[[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|As We
  Go Marching]]* (1944) made the structural version of the case: America had
  assembled four of fascism's five elements (debt-supported spending,
  corporativist planning on the NRA model, militarism, imperialism), and a
  public-debt-supported autarky ultimately cannot be run without the fifth,
  the totalitarian state (see [[as-we-go-marching-thesis]]). Its
  documentation: the discarded 1932 platform (paralleled to Mussolini's and
  Hitler's abandoned programs), the deficit tables 1933–38 and the 1938
  "balanced budget" claim, the Hansen/NRPB permanent-deficit doctrine, and
  the six-point drift of power to the executive (lump-sum appropriations —
  the RFC's $34bn disbursed without appropriation; 4,000+ executive orders;
  delegated legislation). Notably Flynn's 1944 FDR is an opportunist, not a
  doctrinal fascist — "confused, selfish, and utterly political leadership,"
  a systemic drift rather than a design — a more structural claim than the
  personal indictment of 1948.
- **The 1940 statement — the character thesis.** The earliest of the three
  Flynn books, *[[flynn-country-squire-1940|Country Squire in the White
  House]]* (May 1940, written against the third term while Flynn still
  identified as a liberal "well left of center"), grounds the whole critique
  in character rather than structure: FDR as an amiable Hudson Valley squire
  without economic convictions — "not a thinker," a man of "impressions and
  leanings," carried upward by political "tides" rather than ability — whose
  New Deal was therefore improvisation governed by politics. Its distinctive
  contributions to Position 1: the ghostwriting/attribution-stripping case
  (the famous phrases and speeches assigned to Proskauer, Moley, Howe,
  Corcoran); the claim that the NRA originated in Chamber of Commerce
  planning, not the Brain Trust; the "White House, Inc." charge that the
  Roosevelt family earned ~$2.5M during the Depression from the name; the
  argument that FDR resisted rather than championed Social Security; and the
  May-1940 prediction — eighteen months before Pearl Harbor — that "the only
  thing that can save his regime is to take the country off into a war
  hysteria." Notably it *rejects* the dictator charge made by FDR's
  conservative enemies ("Roosevelt is no dictator"), arguing instead that he
  "has the stuff that paves the way for dictators" — a milder claim than the
  structural-fascism argument Flynn built four years later.
- Later scholars and writers in this line: the "revisionist" diplomatic
  historians of Pearl Harbor and the war's origins (Charles A. Beard, *President
  Roosevelt and the Coming of the War, 1941*, 1948; Charles C. Tansill, *Back
  Door to War*, 1952), and later Old Right/libertarian historiography (e.g.
  Murray Rothbard's circle; Burton Folsom, *New Deal or Raw Deal?*, 2008; Amity
  Shlaes, *The Forgotten Man*, 2007, on the economic-failure prong).

## Position 2 — The mainstream assessment

The dominant academic line from the 1950s onward — Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr.
(*The Age of Roosevelt*, 1957–60), William Leuchtenburg (*Franklin D. Roosevelt
and the New Deal*, 1963), Frank Freidel, James MacGregor Burns, and in the
current generation Jean Edward Smith (*[[smith-fdr-2007|FDR]]*, 2007) — holds:

- Roosevelt inherited a collapsing banking system and 25% unemployment and
  restored functioning government and public confidence within months; whatever
  the New Deal's economic incompleteness, it preserved constitutional democracy
  in a decade when comparable crises produced dictatorships elsewhere.
- The New Deal's institutional legacy — Social Security, deposit insurance,
  securities regulation, labor rights — was durable structural reform, not
  improvisation; "trial and error" was pragmatic experimentalism, a virtue in
  an unprecedented crisis.
- On the war: rearmament, Lend-Lease, and coalition management were
  far-sighted; at Yalta the disposition of Eastern Europe reflected the Red
  Army's actual position, not negotiating failure — there was little any
  diplomacy could have conceded that Stalin did not already hold.
- Scholarly presidential-ranking surveys have placed FDR in the top three
  (usually with Washington and Lincoln) in effectively every poll since 1948 —
  the same year Flynn's book led the bestseller lists, a measure of the
  academy/public split.

## Position 3 — The New Left critique

From the 1960s (Barton Bernstein, Howard Zinn, Paul Conkin) a mirror-image
revisionism: the New Deal is faulted not for going too far but for not going
far enough — it rescued and stabilized corporate capitalism, delivered its
gains disproportionately to organized and white constituencies, and foreclosed
more thoroughgoing redistribution. Included here because it shares with
Position 1 the rejection of the heroic narrative while inverting the valence.

## Shared factual ground, framed per side

- Unemployment never fell below ~14% before 1941; full employment came with
  war mobilization. (Position 1: proof of failure; Position 2: proof of the
  Depression's unprecedented depth and of fiscal stimulus arriving only with
  the war.)
- The 1937–38 downturn was among the sharpest contractions in US history.
  (Position 1: the New Deal's own doing; Position 2: caused by premature
  fiscal-monetary tightening — an argument *for* the spending the revisionists
  condemn.)
- The court-packing bill failed in Congress, but the Court's jurisprudence
  shifted decisively in 1937 and Roosevelt eventually appointed eight
  justices. (Position 1: intimidation succeeded where legislation failed;
  Position 2: constitutional adaptation to industrial society, achieved within
  the system.)
- Yalta's territorial outcomes closely tracked the military positions of
  spring 1945. (Position 1: the positions themselves resulted from earlier
  strategic and diplomatic choices; Position 2: therefore Yalta ratified
  reality rather than creating it.)
- Roosevelt's health in 1944–45 was far worse than the public knew, and was
  actively concealed. (Position 1: a disqualifying deception bearing directly
  on Yalta; Position 2: real, but with limited demonstrable effect on
  outcomes.)
- Pre-Pearl Harbor public assurances did not match private policy. Even
  pro-Roosevelt scholars conceded this: Thomas A. Bailey wrote that "Roosevelt
  repeatedly deceived the American people during the period before Pearl
  Harbor," and Schlesinger added that "he had no choice but to trick them into
  acting for what he conceived to be their best interests." (Position 1: the
  concession is the case — Flynn expressly confines his charge to the honesty
  of the statements, declining to argue intervention itself was wrong;
  Position 2: statesmanship under isolationist constraint, vindicated by
  events.)
- The choice of North Africa (TORCH) over a 1943 cross-Channel invasion
  postponed the invasion of Europe by a year. (Position 1, Flynn's strongest
  counterfactual: the postponement handed Eastern Europe to the Red Army —
  "the one chance of avoiding all those terrible conditions in Eastern Europe
  ... was thrown away"; Position 2: a 1943 invasion against an unattrited
  Wehrmacht risked catastrophic failure, and the decision was Churchill's
  preference accepted for coalition reasons.)

## Historiographical status

Flynn's book was reviewed with hostility or silence in the academy yet sold
enormously; that reception gap has persisted for seventy-five years, with the
revisionist case sustained largely outside university history departments
(journalism, libertarian and conservative institutions) and periodically
renewed (Pearl Harbor revisionism in the 1950s, New Deal economic revisionism
after 2007). The dispute is recorded as **open as discourse; closed within the
academy** — both attributed facts.
