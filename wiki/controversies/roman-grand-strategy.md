# Did Rome Have a "Grand Strategy"? (the Luttwak Controversy)

A genuine, still-open dispute over whether the Roman Empire pursued a deliberate, system-wide
**military strategy** of fixed, defensive frontiers — or whether "grand strategy" and "scientific
frontier" are modern anachronisms imposed on a fundamentally **expansionist, open-frontier**
reality. Raised in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 8 (**C. R. Whittaker**); see
[[roman-frontiers]].

## Position 1 — Grand strategy and scientific frontiers (Luttwak)

**Edward Luttwak**, *The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire* (1976), argued that Rome pursued a
**coherent, evolving strategic system**: from Julio-Claudian client-state "hegemonic" control, to
the "scientific," **preclusive defence** of fixed linear frontiers (Hadrian's Wall, the German
*limes*, the Euphrates line) in the High Empire, to "defence in depth" in the late empire. On this
reading the great wall systems were **strategic barriers** sited to maximize military advantage,
the river lines were **defensive frontiers**, and the role of client kings diminished as the
system matured. The view drew on Victorian and French imperial conceptions of the "scientific
frontier" (the term coined on British India's North-West Frontier) and on the idea of natural,
defensible boundaries.

## Position 2 — Open frontiers, no strategic system (Mann, Isaac, Whittaker)

The revisionist majority — **J. C. Mann**, **Benjamin Isaac** (*The Limits of Empire*, 1990), and
**C. R. Whittaker** (*Frontiers of the Roman Empire*) — reject this:

- **Rome had no instrument of strategy**: "no Institute of Strategic Studies," only books of
  *stratagemata* (tactics, not strategy); rudimentary, inaccurate maps; emperors not systematically
  advised by their generals. Decisions were driven by an **ideology of limitless conquest**
  (*imperium sine fine*) and the **glory** of individual emperors, not by strategic calculation.
- **Frontiers were zones, not lines** (after Lucien Febvre and the *Annales* distinction between a
  military *front* and a territorial *frontier*, which merged only with the nineteenth-century
  nation-state). Roman power demonstrably extended **far beyond** the walls and roads; the *limes*
  was originally a **road for movement**, and the visible works were **non-uniform** and often
  **badly sited militarily**, built for administrative control, surveillance, and the regulation of
  (especially nomadic) movement and exchange — **open, not preclusive**.
- Where frontiers stopped is explained **ecologically/economically** (Owen Lattimore's China
  model): the margin of profitable agriculture, a "zone of choice," not a strategic stopping-line.
- **Client kings remained essential** instruments of control beyond the provinces throughout the
  period, against Luttwak's "diminishing role" thesis.

## State of the debate

CAH XI gives Whittaker's revisionist position the floor and treats Luttwak's model as largely
**superseded** among specialists — yet the dispute is **open**, not resolved. It turns on
genuinely **interpretive** questions (can coordinated frontier dispositions exist without a
documented "strategy"? does emperor-level decision-making over war and peace amount to strategy in
effect?) and is constrained by **uneven archaeology** — far richer in the West (especially Britain)
than in the strategically decisive East, where each season's excavation reveals deeper Roman
penetration. The Augustan "frontier policy" question (whether the deathbed *consilium* to keep the
empire "within limits" was permanent policy or temporary prudence) is a connected, equally
unsettled strand.
