# Burr Conspiracy and Trial (1806–1807)

The most dramatic domestic episode of Jefferson's second term: Aaron Burr's alleged plot to detach western territory or launch a filibuster against Spanish possessions, the administration's response, and the high-profile trials in Richmond that ended in acquittal and constitutional precedent.

## Narrative

After his term as Vice President and the fatal duel with Alexander Hamilton, Aaron Burr traveled west in 1805–1806, meeting with prominent figures and apparently developing plans involving the Mexican provinces or possible separation of western territories. General James Wilkinson, governor of Louisiana Territory and a longtime correspondent of Burr's (with his own Spanish connections), played a central and ambiguous role.

By late 1806 reports reaching Jefferson and the cabinet convinced the President that Burr was organizing an expedition that threatened the Union or at least violated American neutrality toward Spain. Jefferson issued a proclamation on 27 November 1806 warning citizens against participation in "a military enterprise" against Spanish dominions and authorizing the use of force to suppress it. Federal authorities moved against Burr's boats and associates on the Ohio and Mississippi.

Burr was arrested in the Mississippi Territory, escaped briefly, and was eventually taken into custody. Key associates (Erick Bollman, Samuel Swartwout) were sent to Washington. The Supreme Court (in Ex parte Bollman and Ex parte Swartwout) discharged some on habeas grounds while the cases proceeded. Burr himself was brought to Richmond for trial before Chief Justice John Marshall, sitting as circuit judge.

The treason trial (summer 1807) became a national spectacle. The prosecution, led by government attorneys including William Wirt, relied heavily on Wilkinson's testimony and documentary evidence. Marshall ruled that treason required an overt act of levying war, proved by two witnesses to the same act; mere conspiracy or preparation was insufficient. Burr was acquitted of treason. A subsequent misdemeanor trial also ended in acquittal. Marshall's handling of the subpoena to the President and other procedural rulings further inflamed administration supporters.

Jefferson had publicly stated in his message to Congress that Burr's guilt was "beyond question." The acquittals and the narrow construction of treason were widely interpreted as rebukes to the executive.

## Causal Analysis

Burr's motives combined personal ambition, financial desperation, and resentment after his political downfall. The loose, fluid situation on the western frontier, combined with widespread anti-Spanish sentiment and rumors of filibustering, created fertile ground for conspiracy talk. Wilkinson's double game (feeding information to both Burr and the Spanish, then denouncing the plot when it suited him) was a major complicating factor.

Jefferson, convinced that the security of the Union was at stake, acted vigorously once the reports crystallized. He relied on Wilkinson despite private doubts. The administration's public assertions of guilt reflected genuine belief but also political calculation.

## Consequence Analysis

The legal outcome strengthened the judiciary's independence and established a demanding standard for treason prosecutions that has endured. It dramatized the separation of powers and the limits on executive influence over the courts.

Politically the episode damaged Jefferson's aura of success in the second term. Critics charged persecution and gullibility. Supporters viewed the courts (especially Marshall) as protecting a dangerous man. The Union held; no major western secession occurred. Burr was ruined for respectable public life in the United States.

The affair also highlighted the continuing fragility of the new western acquisitions and the problems of governance and loyalty in the borderlands.

## Actors

- **Primary**: Thomas Jefferson (decision to prosecute and public statements), Aaron Burr (central figure), James Wilkinson (key informant and witness), John Marshall (presiding judge who shaped the legal rules).
- **Secondary**: Theodosia Burr Alston, Jonathan Dayton, Harman Blennerhassett, western contacts, government prosecutors, defense counsel (including Luther Martin).

## Historiography

Malone (Vol. 5) gives extensive attention to Jefferson's relations with the principals and his response as the responsible head of government. He notes Jefferson's gullibility regarding Wilkinson and the error of the public pronouncement of guilt, while arguing that the President could not have done less once convinced the Union was endangered. The legal historians and biographers of Marshall and Burr have focused on the trial as a landmark in American constitutional law. Modern scholarship continues to debate the exact nature and seriousness of Burr's plans.
