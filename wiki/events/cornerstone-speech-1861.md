# Cornerstone Speech (21 March 1861)

Alexander H. Stephens' address to the Savannah Atheneum in which the Confederate Vice President declared that the new Southern government was founded on the "great truth" that "the negro is not equal to the white man" and that "slavery—subordination to the superior race—is his natural and normal condition." Stephens contrasted this "cornerstone" with the "shaky foundation" of Thomas Jefferson's Declaration of Independence and predicted that the Confederacy would become "the model nation of history."

## Narrative

Delivered less than three weeks after the formation of the Confederate government, the two-hour speech was immediately telegraphed and reprinted across the United States and Europe. It supplied the single most effective piece of verbatim self-incrimination for the Confederacy's enemies. Abolitionists and anti-Confederate publicists treated Stephens' own words as authoritative proof that the rebellion existed to entrench and expand racial slavery on a national scale.

The speech's velocity and scope were extraordinary: London and Paris dailies carried it; selections were read in the British Parliament and French Corps Législatif; an American missionary in the Ottoman Empire quoted it to local audiences. It damaged Confederate diplomacy from the outset.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The secession crisis and the need for the new Confederate leadership to define its constitutional and moral basis in explicit opposition to the old Union.

**produced:** The central text of the international stigmatization campaign. Charles Sumner, Frederick Douglass, and European liberals repeatedly cited it to demonstrate that the Confederacy could not be treated as a normal state.

## Consequence Analysis

The speech turned what Confederates presented as a nationalist project into, for much of the world, a "new power of a new species" (Sumner) whose explicit foundation on racial hierarchy justified exceptional diplomatic isolation. It remained the touchstone for arguments against the Lost Cause well into the 21st century (Carol Moseley Braun's 1993 Senate speech, monument removal campaigns).

## Historiography

Lost Cause writers and some later historians minimized the speech as extemporaneous or unrepresentative of "true" Confederate aims. Bonner restores it as the clearest public articulation of the regime's ideological core at the moment of birth and the most potent single weapon in the global pariah campaign.
