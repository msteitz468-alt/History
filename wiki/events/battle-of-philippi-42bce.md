# The Battle of Philippi (42 BCE)

The two engagements in Macedonia in which the **[[second-triumvirate|triumvirs]] Antony
and Octavian** destroyed the army of **Brutus and Cassius** — the death-blow of the
Republican cause and the decisive proof that the Republic, in Pelling's phrase, "was
already dead." Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 1 (Pelling).

## Narrative

The campaign was the triumvirs' **war of vengeance** for the
**[[julius-caesar-assassination-44bce|murdered Caesar]]** — solemnized by Caesar's
**consecration as a god** at the start of 42, which let Octavian style himself *divi
filius*. The **"Liberators"** had spent 43–42 raising armies and squeezing the wealthy
East: Cassius sacked **Rhodes**, Brutus stormed **Lycian Xanthus** (mass suicide),
extorting ten years' tribute from Asia. Pelling judges this eastward delay a **fatal
strategic error** — it surrendered the friendly states and supplies of Greece to the
triumvirs and forfeited the chance to isolate their advance force.

The armies met at **Philippi** (autumn 42). In the **first battle** (early Oct.),
**Brutus' wing routed three of Octavian's legions** and took his camp (Octavian
himself, sick, hid in a marsh), but **Antony broke Cassius**, who — despairing in the
dust and ignorant of Brutus' success — **killed himself**. After three weeks, with
his supply lines threatened and his army restless, Brutus was forced into the **second
battle (23 Oct.)**: his lines broke, the carnage was great, and **Brutus too took his
own life.** "With him died the republican cause."

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **assassination of Caesar** and the triumvirs' war of vengeance, set
against the Liberators' control of the East. contributed_to: by **Cassius' strategic
delay** in Asia Minor, which ceded Greece, supplies, and the initiative.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **annihilation of the Republican leadership** and the new **division of
the Roman world** — Antony taking the **East** and the bulk of the legions, Octavian
the hateful task of settling veterans in Italy and the war against
[[second-triumvirate|Sextus Pompeius]].
enabled: **Antony's military supremacy** ("the world saw Antony as the victor of
Philippi"), which made him for years the senior triumvir — and the eventual collision
of the two that ran to **Actium** (31).

## Historiography

No contemporary narrative survives (Cicero is dead): the sources are the later Appian,
Cassius Dio, and Plutarch's *Brutus* and *Antony*, heavily shaped by Octavian's later
propaganda and "a heavy element of fiction." Pelling stresses that the triumviral
period is for us "black and white" — the everyday political life of Rome under the
triumvirs is almost invisible.

**OHCW (Stockton; Griffin).** Stockton: leaving Lepidus in Italy, Antony and Octavian crushed Brutus and Cassius at Philippi (Oct. 42); Octavian then faced settling ~100,000 veterans on Italian land — misery of the dispossessed mirrored in Virgil’s First Eclogue; Perusine War then Treaty of Brundisium (Oct. 40) with Antony–Octavia marriage. Griffin: of Virgil’s fifty-one years, sixteen were civil-war years; Philippi proscriptions said to kill ≥150 senators and 2,000 equites; large Italian land expropriations — “even the survival of Rome seemed to be in doubt,” central for the poetry. Stockton’s continuum: from Caesar’s murder to Actium, “the rule of law was set aside and justice was merely ‘the interest of the stronger’” for thirteen years.
