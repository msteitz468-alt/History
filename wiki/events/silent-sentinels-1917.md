# Silent Sentinels (1917–1919)

Nonviolent White House picketing campaign of the National Woman's Party — banners quoting Wilson's own democracy rhetoric ("Mr. President, how long must women wait for liberty?") — beginning 10 January 1917 under [[paul-alice|Alice Paul]] and [[burns-lucy|Lucy Burns]]. Cox's Part III is largely the political and carceral history of this confrontation.

## Narrative

Pickets of the National Woman's Party began standing silently at the White House gates on **10 January 1917** under [[paul-alice|Alice Paul]] and [[burns-lucy|Lucy Burns]], carrying banners that quoted Wilson's own democracy rhetoric. After U.S. entry into [[first-world-war-1914|World War I]], the confrontation escalated.

**Arrests and Occoquan (1917).** Russian-banner protests (from 20 June) and then "Kaiser Wilson" banners (Burns's formulation, 10 August: twenty million American women "not self-governing") triggered arrests for "obstructing traffic." Bastille Day (~14 July) saw VIP pickets choose **two months at Occoquan Workhouse** over fines. After a July pardon (Edith Wilson's diary: "those devils in the workhouse"), picketing resumed; Navy-led riots and longer sentences followed. Fall waves brought six- and seven-month terms, solitary confinement, hunger strikes.

**Force-feeding and Night of Terror.** Paul (weight ~60 lbs) and others were force-fed in District Jail / psych-ward classification; Occoquan conditions included worms in food, yearly blanket washing, open latrines, beatings. On transfer night ("Night of Terror," Nov. 1917), prisoners were thrown into cells; Burns was handcuffed by the wrists above her head to cell-door bars overnight; Dora Lewis smashed head-first into an iron bedframe; Dorothy Day's arms were twisted and she was banged over an iron bench. Quantico Marines sealed the prison. Jailers housed white suffragists with Black women prisoners to humiliate them — an attempt that sometimes produced solidarity instead (Mary Church Terrell and daughter had picketed earlier that winter).

**Habeas and release.** Judge Waddill (Alexandria) ordered release from illegal Occoquan confinement; by the night before Thanksgiving 1917 no suffrage prisoners remained. NWP suspended demos hoping for a State of the Union recommendation that did not come. [[rankin-jeannette|Jeannette Rankin]] visited Occoquan and introduced a resolution on conditions that Rules chair Pou bottled up.

**Wilson's line.** Through Tumulty he insisted there was "no real harshness," that prisoners submitted to artificial feeding "without resistance," and that there was "an extraordinary amount of lying about the thing" — while Cox documents administration green lights (Brownlow) and wartime propaganda apparatus (CPI, *Official Bulletin*, Burleson) turned against the pickets. Dudley Field Malone broke with Wilson over Occoquan, resigned, and was never spoken to again.

(Primary narrative synthesis: Cox 2024, Ranges 04–05; NWP memoirs / *Jailed for Freedom* tradition.)
