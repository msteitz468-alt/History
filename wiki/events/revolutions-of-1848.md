# The Revolutions of 1848

The **Revolutions of 1848** ("the Springtime of Peoples") were the wave of liberal and
national uprisings that swept continental Europe in 1848–49 — France, the German states, the
Habsburg Empire, and Italy — briefly toppling the Restoration order before a conservative
recovery reversed most of their gains. Deep-read from
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v10|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. X]] (C. H. Pouthas).

## Narrative

NCMH X stresses that the revolutions were **"simultaneous… yet isolated"**: inspired by a
common ideology and vocabulary but with **no international plot** — "the same programme
concealed dissimilar situations." A pre-revolutionary atmosphere had built up — the **famine of
1847** and an industrial crisis of unemployment, Pius IX's reforms exciting Italy, the French
reform "banquets," German party congresses — but at the start of 1848 "no one believed
revolution was imminent."

The **February Revolution in Paris** (overthrowing Louis Philippe, proclaiming the Second
Republic) was the spark. The wave spread within weeks: constitutions at **Turin, Florence,
Rome, and Naples**; parliamentary ministries and National Guards across **western Germany**;
and — decisively — revolution in **Vienna itself (13–15 March)**, where the fall of
**Metternich** "entailed the immediate collapse of Austrian hegemony in Italy and the end of
absolute monarchy in Prussia." In the Habsburg lands the nationalities rose: **Kossuth's
Hungary** won a liberal "Statute" and the abolition of feudal dues; **Prague/Bohemia** gained a
charter; an all-German parliament was convened at **Frankfurt**. Strikingly, most of this came
"without violence," through the unanimity of public opinion and the "unreasoning fear" of
governments taken by surprise.

The unity dissolved in **1849**. Divisions between liberals and radicals, between the
bourgeoisie and the workers (the June Days in Paris), and above all between rival nationalities
(Germans vs. Czechs and Magyars vs. their own minorities) let the monarchies recover. Austrian
armies (Radetzky in Italy, Windischgrätz and Russian help in Hungary) crushed the risings; the
Frankfurt Parliament's offer of a German crown was refused by the Prussian king; the Roman and
Venetian republics fell. By late 1849 the old order was largely restored.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **1846–47 famine and economic crisis** (hunger riots, mass unemployment).
- `caused_by:` accumulated **liberal and national grievances** against the Restoration
  (censorship, the confessional/police state, denial of representation and of national unity).
- `contributed_to:` the **Paris February Revolution** as the detonator, and the **surprise and
  weakness** of unprepared governments.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **fall of Metternich** and the temporary collapse of the 1815 order — and the
  abolition of serfdom/feudal dues in the Habsburg lands (a lasting gain).
- `produced:` a sweeping **conservative recovery (1849)** — most constitutions revoked, leaders
  exiled — making 1848 "the turning-point at which modern history failed to turn" (A. J. P.
  Taylor).
- `enabled:` the **lessons of failure**: that liberalism and nationalism could not triumph by
  idealism and barricades alone, but needed *Realpolitik*, armies, and a great power — the
  insight that drove [[italian-unification|Italian]] and [[german-unification|German]]
  unification through Cavour and Bismarck.

## Actors

- **Liberal and radical bourgeois intellectuals** and **urban crowds** (NCMH X stresses they
  were idealistic, inexperienced, and "not revolutions of the masses"); the **nationalities**
  (Germans, Italians, Hungarians under **Kossuth**, Czechs); against the **conservative
  monarchies** (the Habsburgs, Prussia) and **Metternich**. See [[nationalism]], [[liberalism]].

## Historiography

NCMH X (Pouthas) emphasizes the revolutions' ideological derivation from French political
thought and their leaders' inexperience. The classic verdict (Namier, Taylor) treats 1848 as a
liberal failure; later social historians stress the peasant and worker dimensions and the
genuine, lasting abolition of feudal obligations in central Europe.
