# Battle of the Allia

## Narrative

According to Roman tradition, a Roman army met an invading force of Senone Gauls under Brennus at the Allia river, roughly eleven miles north of Rome, around 390 BCE. The Romans, deployed on an extended and thinly held line to avoid being outflanked, broke almost immediately under the Gallic charge; the rout was so complete and demoralizing that survivors fled past Rome itself to the safety of Veii rather than defending the city, leaving Rome open to the Gauls, who entered and sacked it within days.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** the sudden appearance of a large, unfamiliar Gallic force in Roman territory with no time for the customary systematic Roman military preparation; contributed_to by tactical mismanagement in the Roman deployment, which historians such as Livy attribute to overconfidence or poor generalship.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced:** the immediate and undefended [[gallic-sack-of-rome-390bce|sack of Rome]]; a lasting cultural trauma in Roman memory, with "dies Alliensis" (18 July) marked thereafter as a black day (dies religiosus) on which no public business was conducted.

## Actors

(Roman tradition does not preserve a reliably historical named Roman commander for the battle; Brennus is named as the Gallic leader in later, semi-legendary accounts.)

## Historiography

As with much early Roman history, the battle is known only through later literary tradition (principally Livy, writing over three centuries afterward), and modern historians treat the specific tactical details, casualty figures, and even the precise date with considerable caution, while treating the basic fact of a severe Roman defeat opening the way to the sack of Rome as reasonably well established.
