## Narrative

### Background

After subduing Kyushu (1587), [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] addressed the only remaining independent power in Japan: the Hōjō family of Odawara, who controlled the eight provinces of the Kantō. The Hōjō had long refused to come to the capital and acknowledge Hideyoshi's supremacy, and a border incident involving Sanada clan territory — over which Hideyoshi sent an ultimatum — gave the formal pretext for war.

[[tokugawa-ieyasu]] forwarded the ultimatum to Odawara and urged the Hōjō to submit, noting "with a reproach for disregarding his advice, and a recommendation to apologize even at this late hour." Hōjō Ujimasa, in an extraordinary miscalculation, treated the threat as bluff: "With a strange obtuseness he failed to realize how the situation had changed in the last six years."

Before the campaign, Ieyasu sent his heir Nagamaru to Hideyoshi's Juraku mansion in Kyoto as a hostage. Hideyoshi's wife personally redressed the boy's hair and gave him fashionable court clothes, commenting that his manners were beautiful though "a bit countrified." This ceremony marked Nagamaru's coming-of-age and the conferral of the name Hidetada (taking the first character of Hideyoshi's name).

### Campaign

Hideyoshi mobilized an army of approximately 150,000 men. Ieyasu was assigned the heaviest burden — seven men per hundred koku from his fiefs, versus six for northern provinces and four for the Chugoku — partly because he was geographically closest to the Hōjō and partly to demonstrate his loyalty beyond question, since he was the father-in-law of Hōjō Ujinao.

The march was organized for maximum spectacle. Hideyoshi left Kyoto in scarlet-laced armor with a gold quiver, on a horse in golden-tasselled mail, preceded by "all his high officers equally splendidly attired" and great court nobles. Stands were erected along the highway as far as Fushimi. Ieyasu met Hideyoshi at Sumpu to receive him formally, then rushed back to his forward positions.

Hideyoshi's fleet — under Chosokabe Motochika, Wakizaka, Kato Yoshiaki, and Kuki Yoshitaka — instituted a naval blockade. The great host encircled Odawara. Oda Nobuo and others attacked the subsidiary fortress of Nirayama (Odawara's strongest external defense), but "they never succeeded in taking it" despite constructing a wooden tower. The 3,640-man garrison sallied forth and inflicted casualties on Fukushima's detachment.

Hideyoshi settled in for a siege of unusual comfort. Commanders built "quite handsome residences with Shoin reception-rooms and tearooms surrounded by landscape gardens," men cultivated vegetable patches, tradespeople set up shops, and Hideyoshi wrote to his consort asking her to send his favorite Lady Yodo to keep him company. Several thousand ships handled the supply, defeating the Hōjō calculation that the great army would run out of provisions.

Ieyasu took charge of the eastern sector, bringing miners from Kai to undermine the fortifications. A section of wall collapsed in a storm; Ii Naomasa and Matsudaira Yasushige forded the river and set fire to buildings. A fierce fight followed — 300 Tokugawa casualties, 400 Hōjō.

### Capitulation

By the sixth month the siege had not achieved a breach but Hōjō's confidence had been badly shaken. His chief councilor Matsuda Norihide was reported by his own son to be plotting to admit the besiegers. Hōjō Ujinao and his brother Ujifusa went out to Ieyasu to negotiate: Ujinao proposed his own suicide in exchange for the garrison's safety. After consultation with Hideyoshi, the terms were modified: Ujinao to be pardoned, Ujimasa and his brother Ujiteru and the two senior councilors Matsuda and Daidōji to commit seppuku. "On the tenth the Great Lord (Ieyasu) took up his quarters in the castle and inspected it."

Ujimasa and Ujiteru, under guard of 1,500 men, wrote their death verses and committed seppuku on the eleventh; Ujinori as second cut off their heads. Ujinao was exiled to Mount Koya, subsequently given 10,000 koku in Kawachi; he died of smallpox at twenty-nine. Ujinori was given 3,000 koku.

All the great Kantō fiefs were confiscated. Hideyoshi offered them to Ieyasu.

## Field Orders

Ieyasu issued detailed field orders for the campaign, notable for their emphasis on discipline, order of march, treatment of civilians and tradespeople, and strict prohibition on unauthorized seizure of persons: "Anyone who strikes camp without orders will be punished. May all the Gods of Japan both great and small pay attention!"

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[ieyasu-submission-to-hideyoshi-1586]]` — Ieyasu's submission placed him in Hideyoshi's coalition as a commander, making the Odawara campaign the logical next stage.
- `caused_by:` Hōjō's refusal to come to the capital or acknowledge Hideyoshi's supremacy — a decision rooted in their tradition of conservative independence that had served them well in the Sengoku period but was fatally miscalibrated for the new political reality.
- `contributed_to:` Ieyasu's position as father-in-law of Ujinao made him the natural conduit for the capitulation negotiations.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[hojo-clan]]` — The extinction of the Hōjō as an independent power, ending a dynasty that had controlled the Kantō since the early sixteenth century.
- `produced: [[ieyasu-moves-to-edo-1590]]` — Hideyoshi offered Ieyasu the eight Kantō provinces in exchange for his five ancestral provinces, completing the strategic encirclement of Ieyasu behind the Hakone barrier.
- `produced: [[ieyasu-moves-to-edo-1590]]` — The transfer made Edo, not Odawara, Ieyasu's new capital.

## Actors

- [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] — commander of the besieging coalition; achieved Japan's final unification
- [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] — commanded the eastern sector; led the capitulation negotiations
- [[hojo-clan]] — defenders; Ujimasa and Ujiteru died; Ujinao survived
- Hōjō Ujimasa — the senior Hōjō, whose refusal to submit sealed his family's fate; died by seppuku
- Hōjō Ujinao — Ieyasu's son-in-law; spared at the surrender
- Ii Naomasa — led the river assault on Odawara's eastern sector

## Historiography

Multiple sources are cited by Sadler: the Kwan-hasshu-kosen-roku for the offer of the Kantō to Ieyasu (with the famous "pair of pissers on the Kantō" anecdote), the Yuki Hōkyo diary for the siege camp's social life, and the Mikawa Fudoki. The campaign is treated in Tokugawa historiography as the moment that demonstrated both Hideyoshi's total dominance and the seeds of its undoing — the strategic placement of Ieyasu in the Kantō, ostensibly to reward and contain him, created the power base from which Ieyasu would eventually succeed. Sadler, following Tokutomi, notes the Tokugawa Jikki's cynical reading: Hideyoshi expected the unfamiliar Kantō to keep Ieyasu busy and might even weaken him enough for Hideyoshi to intervene again.
