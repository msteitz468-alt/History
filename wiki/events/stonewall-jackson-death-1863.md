## Narrative

On the night of May 2, 1863, following his devastating flank attack on the Union Eleventh Corps at the [[events/battle-of-chancellorsville-1863|Battle of Chancellorsville]], Confederate Lieutenant General Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson rode forward with his staff to scout further advance in the darkness. Returning toward Confederate lines, his party was mistaken for Union cavalry and fired on by his own men of the 18th North Carolina Infantry. Jackson was hit three times, and his left arm was amputated the next day. He was moved to Guinea Station to recover but developed pneumonia and died there on May 10, 1863, reportedly with the last words "Let us cross over the river, and rest under the shade of the trees."

## Causal Analysis

The wounding was caused_by the chaos and darkness of the aftermath of Jackson's own successful flanking maneuver, compounded by inadequate coordination between his forward staff and the pickets of his own army; his death a week later was caused_by post-amputation pneumonia rather than the wounds themselves.

## Consequence Analysis

Jackson's death removed the corps commander [[actors/robert-e-lee|Lee]] had relied on most heavily for aggressive, independent execution of complex maneuvers, forcing a reorganization of the Army of Northern Virginia from two corps into three ahead of the Gettysburg campaign — a restructuring many historians (though not all) judge weakened the army's command cohesion at Gettysburg. The loss became, in postwar Southern memory, one of the "Lost Cause" narrative's central turning points.

## Historiography

The death is among the most heavily documented individual episodes of the American Civil War, owing to numerous staff-officer memoirs and its place in postwar Lost Cause historiography, which tended to elevate it into a quasi-providential turning point; more recent scholarship treats it as significant but weighs it alongside broader structural factors (manpower, logistics, Union command improvement) in explaining the Confederacy's declining fortunes after mid-1863.
