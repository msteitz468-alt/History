# First European Encounters with Australia (c. 1600–1803)

## Narrative

"The history of Aboriginal people in Australia spans tens of thousands of years; the
history of newcomers to their shores is much shorter." (Konishi and Nugent, Ch 2,
[[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]])

The two centuries from 1606 to the early 1800s constitute the period of initial and
sporadic European contact with Australia — largely coastal, largely brief, marked by
systematic misunderstanding of Aboriginal societies, and culminating in the conditions
that led to British colonisation in 1788 and Flinders' circumnavigation of 1801–03.
These encounters were also concurrent with the more sustained and economically-driven
Macassan trepang trade with northern Aboriginal communities (see [[macassan-australian-contact]]).

### Prelude: European Maritime Expansion toward the Southern Hemisphere

Portuguese navigator Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope in 1488; Vasco de
Gama reached India in 1498; Ferdinand Magellan proved the Pacific connected to the
Atlantic by 1520. Spanish expeditions sought the fabled *terra australis* throughout the
16th century, reaching the Solomon Islands in 1567 and Torres Strait in 1605 (Luis Vaez
de Torres, second-in-command to Pedro Fernandes de Queirós). The Dutch established the
VOC in 1602 to consolidate commercial interests in Asia's spice trade.

### Dutch Encounters (1606–1690s)

**1606 — Janszoon / *Duyfken***: The VOC sent the *Duyfken*, commanded by **Willem
Janszoon**, to investigate "rumours of trade opportunities and gold" southeast of the
Spice Islands. Crossing Torres Strait and coasting Cape York's western shore, Janszoon's
crew became "the first Europeans to land in Australia" — unwittingly, having expected
to find gold (*Isla del Oro*). They found instead "a charmless, barren wasteland…little
oily trees." At the Wenlock River, sailors were attacked; one was killed. Janszoon's
verdict: inhabited by "wild, cruel, black and barbarous men." This was the first
documented violent encounter between Europeans and Aboriginal Australians.

At Cape Keerweer, an Aboriginal oral tradition recorded by Jack Spear Karntin (born
c. 1905) recalls the arrival: "They (the Aboriginals) did not yet understand things, no,
not our colour…If they had behaved properly, (the Aboriginals) would not have killed
them."

**1616 — Hartog / *Eendracht***: **Dirk Hartog** became the first European to land on
the western coast after the **Brouwer Route** (1611) brought VOC vessels east via the
"roaring forties," sailing past Shark Bay and north along what he named Eendracht's Land.
Without a chronometer (not invented until the mid-18th century), VOC vessels frequently
miscalculated longitude and struck the western coast; several were wrecked on the
Abrolhos Islands throughout the 17th century.

**1623 — Carstenszoon**: Jan Carstenszoon, captain of the *Arnhem* and *Pera*, visited
Cape York and introduced a practice of **kidnapping Aboriginal people** — seizing one
"round the waist, while at the same time a quartermaster put a noose round his neck."
The captives were taken to Ambon "in irons." Aboriginal people learned, per
Carstenszoon, to treat newcomers as "enemies."

**1642–1644 — Tasman**: Abel Janszoon Tasman charted Van Diemen's Land (naming it after
the governor-general of Batavia), heard human voices "resembling a trumpet," saw notched
trees and concluded the inhabitants "must be very big." He had no face-to-face contact
with Tasmanians. On his 1644 Gulf of Carpentaria voyage he saw Aboriginal people but
"found nothing profitable" — "poor naked people walking along the beaches, without rice
or many fruits, very poor and bad-tempered." After Tasman's voyages, Dutch maps named
the continent *Nova Hollandia* (New Holland).

**1688 and 1699 — Dampier**: English buccaneer **William Dampier** made the most
extensive 17th-century Aboriginal contact. At King Sound in January 1688 he judged
Aboriginal people "the miserablest People in the World" — they had "no houses," appeared
not to "worship any thing," did not till soil or pasture livestock, and lacked iron. He
described their physical appearance in derogatory racial terms. His published account
*A New Voyage Round the World* (1697) was widely read and "informed the expectations of
later voyagers to Australian waters." Returning in 1699 on HMS *Roebuck* at La Grange
Bay, Dampier attempted to capture Aboriginal people to find fresh water; a violent
confrontation ended with him shooting a man.

### James Cook and the *Endeavour* Voyage (1770)

**1770**: Cook's three Pacific expeditions (1768–1779) epitomised Enlightenment
scientific voyaging. The *Endeavour* reached the east coast in late April 1770, spending
c. four months charting from Point Hicks to Torres Strait.

At **Botany Bay**, initial contact was strained: two local men "opposed" the landing;
Cook fired, wounding one. For seven days the *Endeavour* remained and "no connections
were formed." Cook observed only material traces (shelters, canoes, weapons) — "we could
know but little of their customs as we never were able to form any connections with them."
He found the canoes "the worst I think I ever saw." The absence of trade troubled Cook:
in Enlightenment thought, "traffic" indicated capacity for commerce, a marker of
civilisation. In fact, Aboriginal gift-exchange was "a highly complex and ritualised
affair," and the reluctance to accept the outsiders' things "could well indicate caution
about entering into relations with strangers, especially when such relations would
necessarily involve reciprocity."

At **Endeavour River** (Cooktown area), the *Endeavour* remained seven weeks for reef
repairs. Cook adopted a different strategy: keeping his distance and letting local men
approach first. The Polynesian Ra'iatean man **Tupaia**, who had joined the voyage at
Tahiti, played a crucial mediating role — convincing local men to lay down arms,
enabling portrait-sketching and word-list compilation. But a dispute over turtles led the
Aboriginal men to set fire to grass near the camp: "fire was deployed as a tool of
defiance and deterrence."

Cook's accounts represented "a radical revision of the ideas and impressions based
primarily on Dampier's damning descriptions." Influenced by Rousseau, Cook characterised
the east-coast Aboriginal people as "far more happier than we Europeans" — "being wholy
unacquainted not only with the superfluous but the necessary Conveniencies so much sought
after in Europe, they are happy in not knowing the use of them."

Before departing, Cook claimed possession at **Possession Island** for King George III —
contrary to Admiralty instructions specifying that territory be claimed only with
Indigenous consent. "Most historians and legal scholars agree that British claims to
Indigenous people's territory lies less with Cook's ceremony of possession in 1770, and
more with the subsequent and bloody history of British occupation, conquest and settlement,
in which the land was taken by force from its original owners."

Cook is remembered in Aboriginal oral tradition as "the embodiment of a greedy land
grabber." Percy Mumbulla (c. 1905–1991) recalled the encounter at Bateman's Bay:
"When they were pullin' away to go back to the ship, them wild Kurris were runnin' out
of the scrub. They'd stripped right off again. They was throwin' the clothes an' biscuits
back at Captain Cook."

### French Expeditions (1772–1803)

Ten French expeditions arrived in Australia 1770s–1830s, with greater emphasis on
studying Aboriginal peoples and cultures than the Dutch or initial British ventures.

**1772 — Marion-Dufresne**: First face-to-face European contact with Tasmanians (Marion
Bay, Van Diemen's Land). Sent men ashore naked to emerge "like natural man." Initial
welcome was warm, but a misunderstanding over fire use — Marion-Dufresne lit a firebrand
to show peaceful intentions, which the Aboriginal people read as "a declaration of war" —
triggered stone-throwing; French fired, killing at least one man.

**1792–1793 — d'Entrecasteaux**: The most positive French accounts; d'Entrecasteaux
embraced Rousseau's philosophy and instructed the expedition to "investigate the customs
and habits of the natives." His crew initially concluded from burnt human remains that
Tasmanians were cannibals, then realised they cremated their dead. On returning in 1793
they had extended, largely friendly encounters; the French believed they had found
Rousseau's noble savage — "how much…civilised people could learn from this school
of nature!"

**1800–1803 — Baudin**: Nicolas Baudin's scientific expedition aboard the *Géographe*
and *Naturaliste* charted west and south coasts; spent five months at Port Jackson and
nearly three months in Van Diemen's Land. Relations with Aboriginal people were mixed;
later encounters turned hostile as French became increasingly intrusive. François Péron,
expedition chronicler, speculated that the Aboriginal men must have considered the French
"treacherous strangers."

### Matthew Flinders and Circumnavigation (1801–1803)

**Matthew Flinders** circumnavigated Australia in HMS *Investigator* 1801–03, the
first complete chart of the continent's coastline. He introduced it as "Australia" in
his account *A Voyage to Terra Australis* (1814), noting the name was "more agreeable
to the ear" and more "in tune with the names of the other great portions of the earth."

Flinders was accompanied by **Bungaree (or Bongaree)**, a Guringai man from Broken Bay
— "the first Aboriginal man to circumnavigate Australia." Bungaree served as intermediary
with other Aboriginal peoples, though Flinders discovered that Aboriginal languages were
"wholly different" from group to group. At Sandy Cape (Hervey Bay, Queensland), Bungaree
stripped off clothes and laid down weapons to approach a retreating group; they eventually
accepted his advance, enabling peaceful exchange. Flinders also encountered 6 Macassan
*praus* in the Gulf of Carpentaria, whose captain Pobassoo reported 60 vessels working
the coast — entirely unaware of the British colony at Port Jackson.

## Causal Analysis

The fundamental European misunderstanding of Aboriginal societies derived from multiple
structural causes:

- **Duration and mode of contact**: Encounters were brief, occasional and fleeting.
  "Few voyagers made return trips to places they had previously visited or stayed for
  very long in one place." Knowledge did not accumulate systematically.
- **Linguistic barrier**: Aboriginal people spoke approximately 250 distinct language
  groups; Europeans had no common language and rarely acquired even basic vocabulary.
- **Interpretive frameworks**: Europeans filtered Aboriginal society through European
  categories — commerce as a marker of civilisation, land cultivation as evidence of
  ownership, material goods as a proxy for social development. All of these frameworks
  systematically misread Aboriginal social complexity.
- **Visual bias**: European explorers almost exclusively encountered Aboriginal men;
  women and domestic social life were nearly invisible to them.
- **Self-reinforcing prejudice**: Dampier's negative account circulated widely and
  "informed the expectations of later voyagers."

The diversity of Aboriginal peoples across the continent meant that experience from
one region "was not readily applicable" elsewhere.

## Consequence Analysis

The two centuries of encounters were "not without consequences":

1. **Cartography**: European knowledge of the Australian coastline was built from Dutch
   surveys (north, west) + Cook (east) + Flinders (complete circumnavigation).
2. **Naming**: *Nova Hollandia* → *Terra Australis* → *Australia* (Flinders).
3. **Legal framework for dispossession**: Explorer accounts constructed Aboriginal
   people as lacking laws, religion and agriculture — providing the ideological
   scaffolding for [[terra-nullius]] and subsequent dispossession.
4. **Colonial selection**: Cook's and Banks' accounts led to Botany Bay being selected
   as the penal colony site; the First Fleet arrived 18 January 1788.
5. **Aboriginal historical memory**: "Today these same accounts are being interpreted
   in new ways as Aboriginal people mine them for details about ancestors, country,
   material culture and practices, allowing them to reconnect with ways of life and
   being before the rupture brought about by colonisation."

## Actors

**Aboriginal perspectives**: Aboriginal peoples across the continent experienced these
encounters as intrusions of "uninvited strangers" into country where "protocols governing
relations with newcomers were already highly developed." They employed withdrawal,
observation from distance, controlled movement of strangers within their territory, fire
as a deterrent, and selective trade. They consistently showed little interest in European
material goods — a disinclination that baffled European visitors but reflected complex
protocols around reciprocity and stranger relations.

The approximately **250 language groups** and 500 clans that populated the continent were
entirely invisible in their complexity to European visitors, who "did not realise that
the Aboriginal men…they encountered belonged to any of approximately 250 language groups
comprising 500 clans." The diversity meant that Aboriginal people "were already well
accustomed to dealing with outsiders" — protocols that European explorers failed to
recognise or understand.

## Historiography

Konishi and Nugent (Ch 2, [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013]]) situate this history at
the intersection of two historiographical traditions: (1) the conventional expansion-of-
Europe narrative emphasising cartographic and commercial progress toward British
colonisation; and (2) more recent interpretations reconstituting "the two-sided nature
of meetings between newcomers and the Indigenous people they encountered." Their chapter
explicitly frames the history "less as an expansion of Europe than as an incursion of
European and Asian newcomers into Indigenous people's country."

The absence of Aboriginal written sources is a core methodological challenge. Indigenous
perspectives survive in oral traditions, dances, songs, rock art and ritual. Cook-related
stories across Australia are "usually an amalgamation and condensation of various contact
experiences and events" — a means of accounting for change, not a verbatim historical
record of specific events.

Key contested questions: the legal basis of Cook's 1770 possession ceremony; whether
Dampier's descriptions simply reflected ignorance or embedded racial hierarchy; the degree
to which early explorer accounts caused (rather than merely reflected) the colonial
dispossession that followed.
