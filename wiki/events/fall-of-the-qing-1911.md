# The Fall of the Qing and the 1911 Revolution (Xinhai Revolution)

The revolution that toppled the [[qing-dynasty|Qing dynasty]] and ended **more than two thousand
years of imperial rule** in China, founding the **Republic of China**. From Jonathan Spence's
[[spence-search-for-modern-china|*The Search for Modern China*]].

## Background: a dynasty out of time

After the [[opium-wars|Opium Wars]], the [[taiping-rebellion|Taiping]], and defeat in the
[[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Sino-Japanese War (1895)]], the Qing attempted belated
**reforms** — abolishing the examinations (1905), building a modern **"New Army"** and railways,
and creating provincial assemblies and constitutional plans. But reform came too slowly and bred
its own opposition: a modernizing officer corps and a provincial gentry increasingly alienated
from the Manchu court, and a growing **anti-Manchu nationalism** voiced by exiled revolutionaries
— above all **Sun Yat-sen** and his **Revolutionary Alliance (Tongmenghui)**. The court's attempt
to centralize control (recentralizing the New Army under Manchu officers, nationalizing the
railways) deepened the disaffection.

## The Wuchang uprising and the cascade

The revolution began almost by accident. On **10 October 1911** ("Double Ten"), the **Wuchang
Eighth Engineer Battalion** of the New Army mutinied; within days the Wuhan cities fell to the
rebels. The court recalled the strongman **Yuan Shikai** — commander of the Beiyang army — but
he played a **canny double game**, pressuring both the Manchus and the revolutionaries. As
province after province **declared independence** from the Qing through the autumn, the dynasty's
authority collapsed; Spence stresses the **mass support** the Revolutionary Alliance turned out
to command.

## The Republic and the abdication

Sun Yat-sen — who read of the uprising in a Denver newspaper while fundraising in America —
returned to be elected **provisional president** of the Republic of China at Nanjing (1 January
1912). To avoid civil war, the revolutionaries struck a deal: **Yuan Shikai** would secure the
emperor's abdication in return for the presidency. On **12 February 1912** the boy emperor
**Puyi** abdicated, ending the Qing — and the imperial system founded by the
[[qin-shi-huang|First Emperor]] in 221 BC. Sun stepped aside, and **Yuan Shikai** became
president of the new republic.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the Qing's **failure to reform** fast enough to satisfy the new social forces it
  had created (the New Army, the provincial assemblies, students).
- `caused_by:` **anti-Manchu nationalism** and Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary organization.
- `contributed_to:` the **railway-nationalization crisis** and the recentralizing measures that
  alienated provincial elites.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **end of two millennia of imperial rule** and the founding of the **Republic
  of China**.
- `produced:` the rule of **Yuan Shikai** (who soon tried to make himself emperor, 1915–16) and,
  after his death (1916), the fragmentation into **warlordism**.
- `enabled:` the long **revolutionary century** — the [[chinese-communist-revolution|May Fourth
  movement, the CCP, and the eventual Communist revolution]] — as the "search for modern China"
  continued without a stable settlement.

## Significance

1911 is one of the great **transitions** of world history: the abolition of the world's oldest
continuous political institution. Yet Spence stresses it **resolved little** — the republic was
weak, fragmented, and soon prey to warlords; the deeper "search for modern China" had only
entered a new phase.
