## Narrative

Out of Africa 1 refers to the first dispersal of members of the genus *Homo*
out of Africa into Eurasia, beginning approximately 1.8 million years ago.
It was not a single migration event but a series of pulses and retreats shaped
by climate, technology, and ecological opportunity.

The earliest firm evidence outside Africa comes from **Dmanisi, Georgia**
(1.77 million BP) — skulls and stone tools associated with early forms of
*Homo ergaster* or early *Homo erectus* with brains about half the size of
modern humans. These populations had stone tools similar to the African Lower
Paleolithic (Oldowan), which allowed butchering of large animals and access to
meat and marrow, giving them the dietary basis to survive in colder regions.

Other early Eurasian evidence (Dennell, CWH Vol. I, Ch. 17):
- **Java, Indonesia**: *Homo erectus* at Sangiran ~1.5–1.6 million BP; Mojokerto
  <1.49 million BP
- **North China**: Nihewan Basin artefacts ~1.6–1.66 million BP (Majuangou);
  Lantian *H. erectus* cranium >1.2 million BP; Yuanmou possibly ~1.7 million BP;
  Zhoukoudian 0.4–0.78 million BP
- **Atapuerca, Spain**: a mandible ~1.3 million BP; a tooth from Barranco León
  (Orce Basin) possibly ~1.4 million BP — the westernmost early evidence
- **'Ubeidiya, Israel**: ~1.0–1.5 million BP (Acheulean)
- **Attirampakkam, South India**: ~1.0–1.5 million BP (Acheulean, much earlier
  than previously thought)

By ~1.5 million BP hominins were distributed across the entire Eurasian landmass
south of ~40°N latitude. More northerly environments required technologies (fire,
clothing) not yet firmly attested for these populations.

**The Acheulean second dispersal**: A distinct dispersal (or set of dispersals)
involved the expansion of Acheulean handaxe/cleaver technology from Africa into
Asia. The earliest African Acheulean dates ~1.7 million BP; in Asia the earliest
is 'Ubeidiya (Israel) ~1.0–1.4 million BP, with recent evidence pushing South
Indian (Attirampakkam) and Caucasus (Armenia) Acheulean back beyond 1 million BP.
A further possible dispersal is marked at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov (Israel), with
African-type handaxes ~700,000–800,000 BP. Acheulean reached Europe only
~500,000–600,000 BP, associated with *Homo heidelbergensis* (an immigrant into
Europe from Southwest Asia).

The dispersal was almost certainly episodic: advances during warmer periods
when uninhabitable regions opened up; retreats and abandonments during cold
spells. There may have been multiple reverse migrations back toward Africa.
The evidence is thin enough that a single new find could transform our
understanding of the timing and geography.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[oldowan-acheulean-technology|Oldowan and Acheulean stone tool technologies]]` — access to
meat allowed survival in environments where plant resources were seasonal or
scarce. This was the enabling technology.

`contributed_to: brain size increase and dietary shift in early Homo` —
larger brains required more energy; meat provided it; the feedback loop
encouraged expansion into richer prey environments.

Climate also shaped the timing: warmer phases expanded the range of viable
environments and opened corridors through the Levant into Eurasia; colder
phases contracted it.

Dennell (Ch. 17) links the earliest dispersals to the mastery of stone tools for
defleshing carcasses and extracting marrow (~2.6 million BP), which enabled
hominins to "delay consumption" and exploit large-mammal carcasses — carnivory
and tool-making as the impetus for leaving Africa. The "expensive tissue
hypothesis" (Aiello & Wheeler) connects increased meat-eating to gut shortening
and brain enlargement; the "social brain hypothesis" (Dunbar) connects larger
brains to larger social groups, better communication, resource-mapping, and
exchange networks — a "social brain" that "widened the potential scope for
hominins to disperse into new types of environments." Note: the early Dmanisi
hominins were highly sexually dimorphic, which some read as implying a harem-type
social organization.

## Consequence Analysis

- Multiple geographically isolated *Homo* populations evolved across Eurasia,
  eventually producing distinct regional lineages
- In Europe, *Homo heidelbergensis* (~600,000 BP) evolved as the ancestor of
  both Neanderthals and (via Africa) *Homo sapiens*
- In East Asia, *Homo erectus* persisted until perhaps 50,000–100,000 BP
- The degree to which these Eurasian lineages contributed genetic material to
  modern *Homo sapiens* (via introgression during Out of Africa 2) is actively
  debated

## Historiography

The concept of "Out of Africa 1" as a coherent event was sharpened by aDNA
analysis in the 2010s, which clearly showed that non-African modern humans
carry Neanderthal DNA (from a contact event in the Levant ~100,000–70,000 BP)
and some carry Denisovan DNA — implying that the Eurasian hominin populations
descended from Out of Africa 1 did not simply disappear but interbred with
later modern human migrants.

Whether Out of Africa 1 represents one event or many, and exactly how the
various Eurasian *Homo* populations relate to each other taxonomically, remains
actively contested. The quality of evidence is thin: as Dennell notes, a
single new discovery could force re-evaluation of both the geography and
timing of early dispersals.
