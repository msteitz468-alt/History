## Narrative

The origins of the Kongo kingdom have been the subject of sustained scholarly revision. The dominant twentieth-century account — consolidated by Vansina (1963) and largely accepted by Thornton (2001) — held that a conqueror named Lukeni lua Nimi crossed the Congo River from the principality of Vungu on the north bank, seized the mountain site of Mbanza Kongo, and founded the kingdom by military conquest. Thornton's chapter in Bostoen and Brinkman (2018) presents a substantially revised vision, incorporating new linguistic evidence from the KongoKing project and a reappraisal of overlooked seventeenth-century sources.

The revised model proposes that the founding of Kongo was not a single conquest event but a multi-generational process of political agglomeration originating from multiple poles: the Vungu confederation (north bank), the sub-domain of Mpemba Kasi, the allied polity of Mbata, and — crucially — the ancient eastern empire of the [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza]]. Thornton dates the critical founding moment (Lukeni lua Nimi's seizure of Mbanza Kongo) to approximately 1390, based on genealogical calculation from Lukeni's successors. This is roughly a century later than the date of "1274" recorded by the chronicler Mateus Cardoso in 1624, which Thornton argues may refer to an earlier stage of the expansion from Vungu, not Lukeni's specific conquest.

### The Sequence of Expansion

Thornton reconstructs the process in four broad phases (illustrated in his Figures 1.1a–d):

**Phase 1 (c. 1280–1330):** Three major entities existed in the region: the Vungu confederation on the Congo's north bank; Mpemba (with Mpemba Kasi as its northern sub-domain) on the south bank; and the large empire of [[kongo-dia-nlaza|Kongo dia Nlaza]] to the east and southeast. Radiocarbon dating from KongoKing excavations at Kindoki (identified as Mbanza Nsundi) shows that hilltop settlement at that site dates to at least 1350, suggesting Nsundi's existence predates Kongo's founding and belonged to the Seven Kingdoms period.

**Phase 2 (c. 1330–1410):** Nimi a Nzima, the ruler of Mpemba Kasi, contracted a marriage alliance with Nsaku Lau, ruler of Mbata (itself a polity of the weakening Seven Kingdoms whose western vassals had begun breaking away). Their offspring was Lukeni lua Nimi. Operating from a mountain fortress in the Nsi a Kwilu district — where Cavazzi's seventeenth-century account recorded that "the first kings" were buried in a site held in "utmost reverence" in deep forest — Lukeni lua Nimi moved south, seized the mountain at Mbanza Kongo (possibly then unoccupied), and established the kingdom's capital.

**Phase 3 (c. 1410–1490):** During Lukeni's reign or those of his immediate successors, the alliance extended southward and eastward: Nsundi was taken (from the Seven Kingdoms), then Mpangu. Mbamba and Soyo along the western coast were added. The Mwene Kabunga and Mwene Vunda — existing religious and regional authorities near Mbanza Kongo — were integrated into the kingdom's political structure as electors and earth-priests.

**Phase 4 (c. 1490–1570):** Post-Afonso I expansion into the east continued, and Kongo came into conflict with the still-extant core of the Seven Kingdoms. The Jagas invasion of c. 1568–70, which nearly destroyed Kongo and forced Álvaro I onto river islands, may have been organized in defense of Kongo dia Nlaza. Álvaro I claimed the Seven Kingdoms in his titles in 1583, marking Kongo's maximum territorial extent.

### The Role of Mbata

Thornton argues that prior scholarship underweighted the Mbata alliance in reconstructing Kongo origins. Evidence from the earliest documentary layers — the letters of Afonso I (written 1509–1542), the geographical account of Duarte Lopes (rendered as Pigafetta 1591), and the coronation records of Mateus Cardoso (1622–24) — consistently portrays Mbata as Kongo's "co-regent": its ruler was the "first voice of Congo," without whose consent no king could be made; Kongo's kings were required to be the "closest relative" of the Mwene Mbata; and Mbata alone could field its own army and issue its own decrees. Both Cardoso and the later source of 1619 record that Kongo kings called the rulers of Mbata their "grandfathers" (*enkak'andi a Mwene Kongo*) and themselves their "grandchildren" (*nkotekolo*). Thornton argues this terminology suggests Mbata was the *senior* partner in the original alliance, a seniority that Lukeni lua Nimi gradually reversed as Kongo expanded south and west.

### Debate with MacGaffey

Thornton explicitly engages the competing interpretation of [[kongo-political-structure-1480|Wyatt MacGaffey (Chapter 2)]], who argues that in 1480 Kongo was not a kingdom in the European sense but a symbolic center where real power rested with local groups and the Mwene Kongo's authority derived primarily from his spiritual prestige. Thornton concedes this model may apply to Kongo's predecessor polities (Mpemba, the Seven Kingdoms, Vungu), but insists that by the time of the earliest documentary sources (pre-1550), Kongo showed clear signs of centralized governance: nobles could be removed from territories by royal decree, incomes were derived from royally-assigned territorial appointments, the state distributed money, and the king commanded substantial military resources.

## Causal Analysis

The primary causal driver was the *weakening* of the Seven Kingdoms of Kongo dia Nlaza, whose western vassals (including Nsundi and Mpangu) had broken away, leaving its former sub-units (including Mbata) seeking new alliances. This structural opening allowed the ruling family of Mpemba Kasi — a sub-domain of Vungu on the south bank — to negotiate a lateral alliance with Mbata that provided the political foundation for Lukeni lua Nimi's expansion. Thornton explicitly argues (following the linguists of the KongoKing project) that this should not be read as a mass demographic migration; linguistic evidence rules out major population replacement. Instead, it was a small armed force that seized power from an existing elite.

## Consequence Analysis

The founding of Kongo produced the largest and most powerful pre-colonial polity in West Central Africa, which at its height (mid-16th to early-17th century) controlled approximately 150,000 km² and maintained diplomatic relations with Europe and the Americas. The structural arrangements of the founding — particularly the Mbata co-regency and the roles of Mwene Vunda and Mwene Kabunga as electors — persisted as constitutional features for centuries. The [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave trade]] would later intensify internal competition and contribute to [[kongo-decentralization|political decentralization]] culminating in the [[battle-of-mbwila-1665|Battle of Mbwila (1665)]] and subsequent civil wars.

## Actors

- **[[lukeni-lua-nimi|Lukeni lua Nimi]]**: Traditional founder; personal name reconstructed from the title *Ntinu Wene* ("king of the kingdom") in Cardoso's account. Crossed from Coimba (within Vungu) to Mpemba Kasi, then moved south to found Mbanza Kongo.
- **Nimi a Nzima**: Lukeni's father; ruler of Mpemba Kasi; contracted the alliance with Nsaku Lau of Mbata.
- **Nsaku Lau**: Ruler of Mbata; Lukeni's maternal grandfather; his family established the hereditary Mwene Mbata dynasty whose "grandfather" status vis-à-vis the Kongo kings persisted into the seventeenth century.
- **Mwene Mpangala**: Pre-existing local lord at the foot of Mbanza Kongo's mountain; submitted to Lukeni in exchange for retention of hereditary rights; his descendants conducted an annual ceremonial challenge to the king's legitimacy as late as Cavazzi's time (1664).
- **Mwene Vunda**: Regional authority south of Mbanza Kongo on the Mbidizi River; became one of the three royal electors; the second "grandfather" of Kongo alongside Mbata.
- **Mwene Kabunga**: Pre-existing religious and secular authority near Mbanza Kongo with land-rights extending south to Luanda; described by Cardoso (1624) as "Supreme Pontiff" of the region; his family retained hereditary positions and participated in disputed royal successions (still recorded in 1673).

## Historiography

### Source Quality

The sources for the pre-Portuguese period of Kongo history are indirect and stratified. The primary tier consists of oral traditions embedded in early written texts: the letters of Afonso I (1509–42), the geographical account of Duarte Lopes as rendered by Pigafetta (1591), the coronation record and "History of the Kingdom of Congo" of Mateus Cardoso (1622/1624), and the Capuchin accounts of Giovanni Cavazzi (visiting 1664, publishing 1687) and Girolamo da Montesarchio. A secondary strand is the soldier-chronicler António de Oliveira de Cadornega (writing 1681, based on information gathered c. 1640s–50s near Okanga in eastern Kongo). Modern oral traditions — notably those collected by the Jesuit missionary-anthropologist Jean Cuvelier in the 1920s — are considered less reliable because they were radically altered by nineteenth-century political changes.

### Scholarly Debates

The major historiographical debate concerns the *origin direction* of the kingdom:
- **Vungu-crossing conquest model** (dominant from Cuvelier 1941 through Vansina 1963): Kongo founded by a conqueror from the north bank who crossed the Congo River and seized Mbanza Kongo by force. Thornton's own 2001 account was anchored in this model, though he emphasized the Mbata alliance.
- **Eastern origin hypothesis** (Van Wing 1921, revived by KongoKing project): Kongo's ultimate origins lay further east, in the region of Kongo dia Nlaza. Van Wing's original version was methodologically flawed; the KongoKing project revived it on sounder linguistic grounds (specifically the eastern-dialect derivation of the word *ngangula*, "blacksmith," an important character in Kongo traditions — Bostoen et al. 2013).
- **Thornton's synthesis (2018)**: Neither pure conquest from Vungu nor simple eastern origin, but a multi-generational process involving Vungu, the alliance with Mbata (itself from the Seven Kingdoms), and the gradual incorporation of Kongo dia Nlaza's territory.

MacGaffey (Chapter 2 of the same volume) contests the conquest model more broadly, arguing that "conquest" narratives are a general feature of Central African political legitimation rather than reliable historical records, and that Kongo in 1480 was better understood as a ritual polity (*primus inter pares*) than a centralized conquest state.

### Dating Methods

The c. 1390 date for Lukeni lua Nimi's founding of Mbanza Kongo rests on genealogical calculation backward from known kings. Radiocarbon dating from KongoKing excavations at Kindoki (Mbanza Nsundi) provides independent support: the site shows occupation by at least 1350, consistent with a period when Nsundi was still part of the Seven Kingdoms, before its incorporation into Kongo. Cardoso's 1624 date of "350 years before" (= 1274) is likely a rough generational estimate referring to the earlier Vungu-based expansion rather than Lukeni's specific conquest.

### Collection Coverage

Coverage of this founding period is substantially improved by the KongoKing project (2012–16), which produced new archaeological and historical-linguistic evidence. The volume under review (*The Kongo Kingdom*, Bostoen and Brinkman 2018) represents the current state of the art. Earlier synthesis in Thornton (1983), Hilton (1985), and Vansina (1963) remains important context. Pre-colonial Africa is generally weak in the collection; Kongo is an exception owing to early Portuguese contact and documentary records.
