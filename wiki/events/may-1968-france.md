## Narrative

In May 1968, student protests at the University of Paris (Nanterre, then the Sorbonne) escalated within days into the largest social upheaval in France since 1936. Immediate grievances were parochial — overcrowded, underfunded universities; a rigid, hierarchical curriculum inherited from the classical lycée tradition; proposed restrictive admissions policies — but they fused with a broader generational critique of consumer society, French Gaullist authority, and the Vietnam War. Police brutality against demonstrators at the Sorbonne on May 11, 1968 turned public sympathy toward the students; three days later the first factory occupations began. By the end of May roughly 10 million French workers were on strike, the largest such action in French history, and the Gaullist government appeared to be losing control of the country.

Students erected barricades, fought police with paving stones and Molotov cocktails, and covered central Paris with slogans ("The dream is reality," "It is forbidden to forbid") that mixed utopian, carnivalesque humor with genuine revolutionary intent. Charles de Gaulle disappeared for several hours on May 29 — it emerged he had gone to West Germany to confirm the loyalty of French troops stationed there before returning to offer workers a large wage increase, which split the student–worker alliance. Elections on June 23, 1968 produced a backlash in de Gaulle's favor: for the first time in French history a single party (the Gaullists) won an absolute parliamentary majority.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by` the university overcrowding produced by the postwar expansion of higher-education enrollment (French university enrollment more than tripled 1950–1965) without matching investment in facilities or reform of a curriculum still oriented toward training a narrow elite; `contributed_to` by the broader youth culture of the 1960s (see [[consumer-revolution]]) and its emphasis on authenticity and rejection of adult hypocrisy; `concurrent_with` similar, causally linked student unrest in West Germany (see [[the-1968-generation-germany]]) and Italy, connected transnationally by opposition to the Vietnam War and by events such as the near-fatal shooting of German student leader Rudi Dutschke, which triggered sympathy protests in Paris. `preceded_by`, not `caused_by`, the May 1968 factory strikes: workers' material grievances over pay and conditions were largely independent of student demands over university governance, and the alliance between the two groups was tactical and temporary.

## Consequence Analysis

Politically, May 1968 `produced` a short-term consolidation of Gaullist power (the June 1968 elections) but `enabled` a longer-term reconfiguration of the French Left: it discredited the French Communist Party's cautious, wage-focused response in the eyes of young radicals and `contributed_to` the turn some activists made toward revolutionary terrorism (see [[left-wing-terrorism-western-europe-1970s]]) and, more broadly and for far more participants, toward the "new social movements" — feminism, environmentalism — that later coalesced into Green parties (see [[greens-parties-western-europe]]). For the next two decades French political identity mapped substantially onto positions for or against May 1968. Its cultural legacy — the critique of hierarchy, the celebration of spontaneity and self-expression — outlasted its immediate political failure.

## Actors

[[de-gaulle-charles|Charles de Gaulle]] navigated the crisis to a decisive electoral victory; French students and factory workers acted as the two (non-identical) bases of the movement; [[the-1968-generation-germany]] provides the parallel West German case.

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (5th ed., 2011), present May 1968 as the "most spectacular episode" of a transnational European student movement, stressing both its utopian/carnivalesque character (drawing on interviews and contemporary manifestos, e.g. the Fišera documentary anthology *Writing on the Wall*) and its ultimate political failure to unseat de Gaulle. Historians divide over whether the "sixties" (of which May 1968 is the emblematic moment) represent an acceleration of interwar cultural trends (radio- and cinema-era loosening of morals) or a genuine rupture; Paxton and Hessler note both positions without adjudicating.
