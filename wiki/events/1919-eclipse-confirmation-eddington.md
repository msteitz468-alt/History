# 1919 Eclipse Confirmation of General Relativity (Eddington)

**May 29, 1919 solar eclipse expeditions (Príncipe and Sobral) confirm Einstein's prediction that the sun's gravity bends starlight, announced November 1919.**

## Narrative

With the end of the First World War, two British expeditions (led by Arthur Eddington, a Quaker pacifist, and Frank Dyson) traveled to observe the May 29, 1919 total solar eclipse. One party went to the island of Príncipe off West Africa; the other to Sobral, Brazil. Their goal was to measure whether starlight passing near the sun was deflected by the amount predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity (1.75 arc-seconds) rather than the Newtonian half-value.

Photographic plates were taken during the eclipse. Analysis showed the deflection matched Einstein's prediction. The results were announced at a joint meeting of the Royal Society and Royal Astronomical Society on November 6, 1919.

## Causal Analysis

The expeditions were enabled by the 1915 completion of Einstein's field equations, which produced the precise prediction of light bending as a consequence of spacetime curvature (equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass). Pre-war planning had been disrupted; post-armistice international scientific collaboration resumed. Eddington's enthusiasm and the dramatic contrast between "warring nations" (German theory confirmed by British observers) amplified impact.

## Consequence Analysis

The confirmation instantly transformed Einstein from a respected theorist into a global celebrity. Newspapers proclaimed a "REVOLUTION IN SCIENCE / NEWTONIAN IDEAS OVERTHROWN". "The scientific concept of the fabric of the Universe must be changed," declared The Times of London. "Scientists belonging to two warring nations had collaborated again!" (Leopold Infeld). Public frenzy ensued: "Now every coachman and waiter argues about whether or not relativity theory is correct" (Einstein to Grossmann).

The event produced the publicity paradox that defined Einstein's later public life and helped associate relativity (in popular imagination) with broader cultural modernism and relativism. It directly preceded Einstein's Nobel (delayed, awarded for photoelectric effect) and his 1921 US tour.

## Historiography

Isaacson (2007) draws on contemporary press reports, Eddington's accounts, and Einstein's correspondence to show both the scientific rigor of the expeditions and the instant cultural explosion. The announcement timing—nearly a year after the Armistice—created a powerful narrative of reconciliation through science. Later scholarship (including re-analyses of the plates) has affirmed the essential correctness of the 1919 results, though some early plates were marginal. The event remains the canonical public confirmation of GR.
