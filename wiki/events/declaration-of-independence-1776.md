# Declaration of Independence (1776)

Adopted by the Second Continental Congress on July 4, 1776 in Philadelphia. Primarily drafted by Thomas Jefferson with edits from John Adams, Benjamin Franklin and others. Articulated natural rights philosophy ("life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness") and listed grievances against King George III, justifying the colonies' separation from Britain.

## Narrative
The document transformed a colonial rebellion into a war for independent nationhood. Its universalist language had global reverberations.

### Congressional path and Samuel Adams ([[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Schiff]])

**Richard Henry Lee** introduced a resolution to dissolve all political connection with Great Britain on **Friday, 7 June 1776**. [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]] delivered what a New England colleague called the best speech he had heard from him to waverers; Galloway recalled an equal split until Adams “worked his art” to a one-vote majority. Lives, fortunes, and sacred honors—Adams would have reversed the sequence of the pledge. Congressional journals are silent on who was present for every step; the famous signing day was not 4 July and was likely in **August**, when Adams placed his “tidy, modest signature.”

To Adams that summer the text was still a “catalogue of crimes of the deepest dye”—justifying long-suffering America to honest men after a revolution in thinking already accomplished. He believed Canada would have joined had independence been declared in **1775**. He had anticipated, often by years, nearly every point in the Declaration and was later hailed as first to contemplate separation. Mid-1774 the word independence could make a man a “leper”; Washington (Oct 1774) insisted no thinking man desired separation; Jay never encountered the idea before 1775; Franklin in March had never heard a wish for it—until Lexington opened what Adams called the “important glorious crisis.”

## Historiography
The Declaration survives in Jefferson's own drafts and Congress's editorial revisions, extensively studied (Julian Boyd's textual reconstruction is the standard reference), alongside Jefferson's later correspondence and Adams's recollections about the drafting — sources that are rich but also self-serving, written decades after the fact amid rivalry over credit. Historians debate how much the document's natural-rights language reflected genuine Lockean/Enlightenment conviction versus rhetorical strategy aimed at securing French support and colonial unity; the tension between its universalist claims and the continuation of slavery by many signatories, including Jefferson, is a central and enduring interpretive controversy. Scholarship has also shifted from older "great document, great man" narratives toward examining the collective congressional editing process and the document's immediate function as a practical instrument for foreign alliance-seeking rather than solely a philosophical treatise. Its retrospective elevation into founding scripture is itself a subject of historiographical study.

**Schiff / memory culture:** scant contemporary attention—few located Rubicons in July 1776; signers spoke little of the Declaration and, other than Trumbull’s canvas, never assembled in the same room. Legend arrived later. Two generations on, Trumbull’s massive Declaration painting exhibited in Boston showed Adams barely discernible—upstaged by Gerry, blotted by R.H. Lee, “pilloried” between neighbors’ shoulders; Old Whigs fumed that “their Moses” was thrust out of sight (56 signers; 47 in the painting; omissions and inclusions inaccurate).
