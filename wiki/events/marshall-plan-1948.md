# The Marshall Plan (1948–1952)

The **Marshall Plan** — officially the **European Recovery Program (ERP)** — was the
massive US aid program (c. $13 billion, 1948–52) that financed the reconstruction of
**Western Europe** after the Second World War. Announced by Secretary of State **George C.
Marshall** in June 1947, it was the **economic arm of [[containment]]** and a decisive step
in the **division of Europe** into two blocs. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The
Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. I]].

## Narrative

By 1947 Western Europe faced a crippling **dollar deficit** — it needed American goods it
could not pay for — and the postwar Bretton Woods liquidity measures and UNRRA relief had
proved insufficient. Britain could no longer fund anti-Communist forces in Greece; the
French, Belgian, and Italian Communist parties were gaining among workers whose living
standards seemed threatened; Germany's currency had collapsed into barter. Marshall's
**Harvard speech (5 June 1947)** offered American aid to all of Europe — including, in form,
the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe — to rebuild a functioning continental economy.

The offer was structured so that **Stalin rejected it** (and forced the Eastern European
states, notably Czechoslovakia and Poland, to refuse) — seeing it, correctly, as a tool to
draw Europe into the American orbit. This, as Charles Maier shows, made the Marshall Plan a
principal mechanism of the **economic division of Europe**: it consolidated a Western bloc
built around a revived West Germany and accelerated the parallel **[[berlin-blockade-1948|crisis
over Germany]]**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Europe's postwar **dollar shortage** and economic collapse, which the Bretton
  Woods measures and UNRRA relief had failed to remedy.
- `contributed_to:` the political **danger of Communist advance** amid postwar misery (the
  French, Italian, and Belgian Communist parties) — and the strategy of [[containment]].

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **recovery of Western Europe** (industrial output surged), the
  **consolidation of the Western bloc**, and impetus toward **European integration** (the
  OEEC, the road to the European Coal and Steel Community).
- `produced:` the **economic division of Europe** and a sharpened Cold War — Stalin's refusal
  (and the USSR's countermoves, the Cominform of 1947 and later Comecon).

The Marshall Plan is the paradigmatic case of **economic statecraft as grand strategy**: it
revived the West European power centres that [[containment|Kennan]] deemed vital, bound them
to the United States, and drew the dividing line of the [[cold-war|Cold War]] across the
continent.

## Actors

- [[united-states]] (George C. Marshall, Truman) — author and funder; the states of **Western
  Europe** — recipients (via the OEEC); the **Soviet Union** — which rejected it and barred
  its bloc. See [[containment]], [[nato]], [[cold-war]].

## From Range 02 (Hitchcock in Leffler & Westad Vol. I)

- Delivered "some $12.3 billion in aid to Europe between 1948 and the end of 1951."
- Harvard speech (5 June 1947): "The remedy lies in breaking the vicious circle and restoring the confidence of the European people in the economic future of their own countries and of Europe as a whole." Purpose: "the revival of a working economy in the world so as to permit the emergence of political and social conditions in which free institutions can exist."
- Paris conference response (Molotov to Stalin): Western partners “are eager to use this opportunity to break in to the internal economies of European countries and especially to redirect the ﬂows of European trade in their own interests.” Kremlin viewed it as "a Trojan horse, designed to undermine the centerpiece of its postwar strategic desiderata – the security zone in Eastern and Central Europe."
- Long-term: represented "the first stage in the construction of that community of ideas, economic links, and security ties between Europe and the United States we know simply as 'the West.'" Europeans adopted American management styles, multinational corporations, higher productivity standards, and consumption patterns ("Americanization of Europe"). Contributed to OEEC and momentum toward 1957 Treaty of Rome.
- After Korea (1950), program shifted toward rearmament; ECA replaced by Mutual Security Agency (1951).

## From Range 03 (Naimark in Leffler & Westad Vol. I)

- Soviet response to offer: In a little over two weeks (end June–early July 1947), Soviet leadership "changed its directives three times in encrypted telegrams sent to the leaders of the East European Communist Parties." Initially all people’s democracies "should express interest"; then "should participate in the conference... but should disagree with its substance and withdraw"; finally "not to participate at all in the conference or in the implementation of the plan." East European leaders "accepted seriatim all of these directives without question."
- Only serious complication: Czechoslovak government (non-Communist majority) "completely committed to the Marshall Plan process." Stalin "ruthlessly pressured the Czechoslovak government delegation" at Moscow meeting 9 July 1947, "forcing them to reject outright the Marshall Plan and refuse participation." Jan Masaryk wrote: “I went to Moscow as a Foreign Minister of an independent sovereign state; I returned as a lackey of the Soviet Government.”
- Broader impact on bloc: "The intensification of the Cold War terminated any lingering restraints on the full-scale Sovietization of the region." Marshall Plan, "its angry rejection by the Soviets, and the establishment of the Cominform" accelerated full one-party consolidation, show trials, and Berlin blockade.
