# Battle of the Field of Blood (1119)

## Narrative

At the **Field of Blood** (*Ager Sanguinis*; plain of **Sarmada** west of Aleppo) in 1119, the Artuqid ruler **Il-Ghazi** (Ilghazi), now lord of Aleppo, annihilated the army of **Roger of Salerno**, regent of **[[antioch|Antioch]]**. Roger was killed and Antiochene power in northern Syria sharply reduced. For Muslim contemporaries it was proof that the Franks could be beaten in open battle; for the Franks it was the worst disaster between the First Crusade and Hattin.

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] places the battle after the end of Great Saljuq sultanic expeditions into Syria: after Bursuq’s 1115 disaster, “the Saljuq sultans never again sent an army to fight the Franks in Syria. Henceforth jihad against the Franks would be a local affair” of amirs, atabegs, and military men. In 1118 Roger of Salerno took **Aʿzaz**, opening Aleppo from the north; Il-Ghazi agreed with **Tughtakin** of Damascus to march on Antioch, first raiding Edessa for non-interference and taking **Atharib**. He raised a large Turcoman/Bedouin host (“many thousands”). Roger waited for Jerusalem and Tripoli with a much smaller force — some **700 knights** and perhaps **~3,000 foot** — on a steep hillock on the eastern edge of the bowl-shaped plain outside Sarmada.

On the night of **27 June** Il-Ghazi’s men (without waiting for Tughtakin) occupied the high ground around Roger’s camp. At dawn the qadi of Aleppo delivered a rousing speech; in windy, dusty weather arrows and encirclement broke the Franks. Ibn al-Qalanisi: “not one hour… ere the Franks were on the ground, one prostrate mass… their leader Roger was found stretched out among the dead.” The victory was also remembered as a miracle of the *abdal* (green-shrouded heavenly warriors of Syria) — a literary riposte to the Franks’ Holy Lance and ghostly armies at Antioch.

Muslim and Frankish sources make much of Il-Ghazi’s alleged drinking as the reason he did not press on to Antioch itself. Cobb argues realist goals guided him instead: he took **Atharib** and **Zardana** as listening posts between Antioch and Aleppo rather than risk a full siege of Antioch. A final clash at **Danith** saw both sides claim victory — “blurriness of loyalty and motive” characteristic of the early post-1099 years.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Antiochene pressure on Aleppo (including the 1118 seizure of Aʿzaz) and Il-Ghazi’s counter-offensive with Turcoman manpower.
`contributed_to:` the general fragmentation of post-Seljuk Syria, which both enabled Frankish footholds and occasionally allowed a single amir to concentrate force after the end of sultanic Syrian armies (post-1115).
`preceded_by:` failed or limited Mosul-centered *jihad* campaigns (Mawdud, Bursuq) that left the Frankish problem to local commanders.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` destruction of Antioch’s field army, death of Roger, and a pause in Frankish expansion inland.
`did not produce:` lasting Muslim unification or the fall of Antioch — that awaited Zangid and Ayyubid state-building.
`analogous_to:` later [[battle-of-hattin-1187|Hattin]] as a field-army annihilation, at smaller scale.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

See Narrative for operational detail. Cobb’s interpretive points: (1) Sarmada was the most significant of the smaller amir campaigns after sultanic Syria armies ceased; (2) the *abdal* miracle narrative deliberately answers Frankish miracle-memory of Antioch; (3) Il-Ghazi’s restraint toward Antioch was strategic, not mere drunkenness; (4) Danith’s mutual victory claims illustrate how fluid early twelfth-century Syrian politics remained.

## Historiography

Latin sources name the site for the blood spilled; Arabic chronicles (Ibn al-Qalanisi, al-ʿAzimi’s ode, later compilers) record Il-Ghazi’s triumph within Artuqid politics and the *abdal* motif. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] treats it as a major early counter-blow in the generation after 1099, before Zangi’s rise, and hedges force numbers as chronicler-scale claims.
