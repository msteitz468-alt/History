# Suez Crisis

## Narrative

Britain and France had divided the spoils of the collapsed Ottoman Empire after 1918, and Arab resentment at the thwarting of national aspirations — sharpened by the Zionists' 1948 creation of Israel — reached a new intensity in the 1950s under a generation of nationalist leaders such as Egypt's Colonel Gamal Abdel Nasser, who overthrew Egypt's pro-Western monarchy and turned to the Soviet Union for arms (accepted 1955). When the United States refused to fund a new high dam at Aswan, Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal — a vital artery for European petroleum shipments, then under international control.

British politicians across the political spectrum viewed Nasser through the prism of the 1930s, as a "Muslim Mussolini" whose contempt for legality threatened international peace, and believed Britain still possessed the means to enforce compliance with Western-style institutions as a matter of "civilized" international order. Britain, France, and Israel secretly devised a coordinated attack intended to seize the Canal and Cairo. Britain assembled a large expeditionary force — 80,000 troops, 150 warships, seven aircraft carriers, forty submarines — but it had to sail from Malta, a thousand miles and six days away. By the time it arrived, aerial bombardment of Cairo and Port Said had already earned the intervention near-unanimous condemnation at the United Nations. The Soviet Union threatened to intervene; the United States applied severe economic and political pressure on British premier Anthony Eden and French premier Guy Mollet to withdraw and accept mediation. Amid international outcry, Britain and France undertook a humiliating retreat; Egyptian-sunk blockships kept the canal itself obstructed, forcing Britain to endure six months of gasoline rationing at home.

## Causal Analysis

The crisis was `caused_by` Nasser's nationalization of the canal in direct response to the American funding refusal, which was itself `contributed_to` by Egypt's acceptance of Soviet arms. British and French military intervention was `caused_by` their calculation — mistaken — that they retained the capacity and international legitimacy to act as global enforcers; it was `contributed_to` by outdated 1930s-derived assumptions about appeasement and dictatorship. The intervention's failure was `caused_by` combined US and Soviet diplomatic pressure and by the sheer logistical distance of the expeditionary force from its target.

## Consequence Analysis

Suez `produced` a decisive collapse of British illusions of Great Power status: within a few years Britain wound up its Malayan and Kenyan military operations, abolished universal military service (1959), and granted independence in rapid succession to the Gold Coast (Ghana, 1957), Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Gambia, Kenya, and Tanganyika, redirecting its imperial identity toward the Commonwealth. It `contributed_to` France's turn toward an independent nuclear deterrent under de Gaulle and to renewed urgency behind European economic integration (the Treaty of Rome negotiations, concluded 1957, were `contributed_to` by the shared European humiliation of Suez). It `enabled` Nasser's emergence as the leading figure of Arab nationalism and deepened Cold War competition for influence in the Middle East.

## Actors

[[gamal-abdel-nasser|Gamal Abdel Nasser]] — Egyptian leader whose nationalization triggered the crisis and who emerged from it as the paramount figure of Arab nationalism. Anthony Eden — British prime minister who ordered the intervention and whose career it ended. Guy Mollet — French premier and co-belligerent.
