## Narrative

### Structural Background

The Genpei War (源平合戦, Gempei kassen) was the product of two decades of Taira dominance following the [[hogen-heiji-disturbances-1156|Heiji Disturbance]] (1159). [[taira-no-kiyomori]] had eliminated the Minamoto leadership, risen to Chancellor (1167) and First Rank, installed his infant grandson as Emperor Antoku (1180), and in 1179 placed Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa under house arrest. By 1180 "sixteen Taira ranked as senior nobles, more than thirty were courtiers, and more than sixty held appointments as provincial governors, guards officers, or members of the central bureaucracy" (Heike monogatari, quoted in [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], p. 697).

This concentration of power generated resentment throughout the court aristocracy and, critically, among warrior networks in the eastern provinces that had been built by the [[minamoto-clan]] over more than a century. The Kanto warrior bands (bushidan) had long-standing ties to the Minamoto through the legacies of Yorinobu, Yoriyoshi, and Yoshiie.

### Outbreak (1180)

Minamoto no Yorimasa (1104–80), an aged Minamoto warrior-courtier who had loyally served the court and become the first Minamoto to reach senior noble rank, joined a conspiracy with Prince Mochihito (Go-Shirakawa's second son) to issue a call to arms against the Taira. The call gave the rebellion imperial legitimacy. Yorimasa was defeated and killed at the Battle of the Uji River. Though suppressed immediately, Mochihito's call reached Yoritomo at Izu and Minamoto no Yoshinaka in Shinano.

**Minamoto no Yoritomo (1147–99)** — Yoshitomo's third son, exiled after Heiji and "placed in the custody of a trusted Taira ally, Hojo Tokimasa" (p. 379) — raised the Minamoto banner in the east in 1180. He mobilized the eastern warrior networks built over generations by the Seiwa Genji. Despite initial setbacks, the eastern provinces became the Minamoto base. Yoritomo established his headquarters at Kamakura in Sagami — land first given to the Minamoto by Taira no Naokata in the 1040s and made the site of Minamoto worship of Hachiman by Yoriyoshi in 1063.

**Minamoto no Yoshinaka (1154–84)**, a cousin of Yoritomo, rose independently in Shinano and pressed toward Kyoto from the north. He drove the Taira out of Kyoto in 1183, but his brutal treatment of the capital alienated the court. He was destroyed by forces loyal to Yoritomo in early 1184.

### The War's Course (1180–1185)

Following Kiyomori's death by illness in early 1181, Taira leadership passed to his sons (Munemori, Shigemori had already died in 1179) — capable men but without Kiyomori's political genius. The Taira retreated from Kyoto in 1183 before Yoshinaka, taking the child emperor Antoku and the imperial regalia with them.

After Yoshinaka's elimination, Yoritomo's brothers — especially **Minamoto no Yoshitsune (1159–89)**, who had been assigned to the priesthood as an infant after Heiji and had grown into a brilliant field commander — led the Minamoto armies westward. The Taira were driven from their strongholds on the Inland Sea coast.

The war concluded at the **Battle of Dan-no-ura** (1185), fought in the straits between western Honshu and Kyushu. The Taira were annihilated; Emperor Antoku drowned with the imperial regalia; the Taira leader Munemori was captured. The Heike monogatari describes the end in terms of Buddhist impermanence — all the proud Taira reduced to nothing.

### Aftermath: Yoritomo's Consolidation

Yoritomo did not pursue the war as a court official but as an independent military power based in Kamakura. His instrument was the [[bushidan]] network of the eastern warriors, formalized through the new institution of gokenin (shogunal retainers), who owed direct personal loyalty to the Kamakura lord in exchange for confirmation of their landholdings.

Yoritomo moved systematically to eliminate rival power centers within the Minamoto: his cousin Yoshinaka was destroyed in 1184; his brilliant brother Yoshitsune — whom Yoritomo suspected of independent ambitions — was hounded to his death in 1189, taking refuge with the Oshu Fujiwara at Hiraizumi until Yoritomo destroyed that last independent warrior power as well.

The [[kamakura-period]] was formally established with the appointment of Yoritomo as Seii-taishogun (Barbarian-subduing Generalissimo) in 1192. See [[minamoto-no-yoritomo]].

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause was structural: Kiyomori's concentration of all military and political power in a single warrior house, without institutional means of accommodating the ambitions of other warrior leaders or the court's ideological independence, made conflict inevitable once Kiyomori's personal ability was removed by death. The proximate trigger — Mochihito's call to arms — gave the rising imperial legitimacy it needed to mobilize the [[bushidan]] networks of the east.

The deeper causes go back to the entire trajectory traced in Chapter 10 of [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]: the emergence of professional warriors (tsuwamono) from the 10th century, the development of [[bushidan]] organizations in the 11th century, the introduction of warriors into capital politics through the Hogen and Heiji disturbances in the 1150s, and the failure of the court to develop any institutional framework for managing the political ambitions of warrior houses. The court remained dependent on warrior force without ever resolving the question of warriors' proper political role.

Yoritomo's specific advantages:
1. The Kanto warrior networks built over three generations of Seiwa Genji leadership
2. The Hojo family's custody of Yoritomo at Izu — which converted a Taira instrument of control into a Minamoto asset
3. The Taira's strategic overextension — governing the court while needing to fight a war across all of Japan
4. Kiyomori's death before the war's decisive phase

## Consequence Analysis

The Genpei War was not merely a change of which warrior house dominated court politics; it was a systemic transformation. The Kamakura shogunate created a parallel governmental structure in eastern Japan — the first warrior government (bakufu) — that operated alongside (and gradually subordinated) the imperial court system. The shoen system and its complex legal relationships continued; the imperial court continued to exist; but "supreme military authority was now institutionally separated from the imperial government and vested in a warrior organization" that answered to the Kamakura shogun.

The war also settled the character of Japanese warrior society for the following seven centuries: the [[bushidan]] lord-vassal bonds developed through the 10th–12th centuries became the template for all subsequent feudal organization.

## Historiography

The Genpei War is the subject of the Heike monogatari (Tale of the Heike), the greatest work of medieval Japanese literature, as well as several related war tales (Hogen monogatari, Heiji monogatari). These texts are invaluable but must be used critically: compiled in the Kamakura period under Minamoto-successor rule, they shaped the war as a Buddhist morality tale of Taira pride and fall. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] (Chapter 10) provides a more analytical account, grounding the war in long-term institutional development rather than the literary narrative of hubris and nemesis. The chapter's contribution is particularly strong on the earlier history that created the conditions for 1180: the warrior band system, the Hogen and Heiji disturbances, and Kiyomori's political methods.

The war is also treated in Chapter 9 of the same volume (on late Heian political history), which this chapter explicitly cross-references: "The political history of the two decades following the Heiji Disturbance until the beginning of the war between the Minamoto and Taira (the Gempei War, 1180–85) is discussed more fully in Chapter 9" (p. 695).
