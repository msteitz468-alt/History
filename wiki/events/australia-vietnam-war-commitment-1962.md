## Narrative

Australia sent military advisers to South Vietnam in 1962. In April 1965 Menzies announced that Australia would support its ANZUS ally by providing an infantry battalion to assist the South Vietnamese government. The battalion would include national servicemen, selected by a lottery of birth dates under the conscription scheme reintroduced in late 1964. By January 1966 when Menzies retired, "Australia's participation in the war in Vietnam was generally supported, as was conscription; however, both were soon to become matters of fierce conflict, with a sharp generational edge."

Harold Holt expanded the commitment three times; most significantly, in March 1966 he announced the despatch of a two-battalion task force including national servicemen — the largest Australian commitment. He declared at a Washington visit that Australia would go "all the way with LBJ." By late 1967, however, Holt informed a "disappointed Johnson administration that Australia had reached the upper limit of its military engagement in Vietnam."

## Strategic Rationale

The commitment was framed by Australia's forward-defence doctrine: defending Australia "as far from our own soil as possible." Vietnam was conceived as an extension of the broader containment of communism in Southeast Asia, operating through the SEATO framework established after the 1954 Geneva Conference divided Vietnam. The source notes retrospectively: "the perception of aggression by the People's Republic of China as a major factor in the war between North and South Vietnam was wrong and that anti-communism had distorted the Australian government's capacities to understand the profound changes taking place in its region."

For Holt, Vietnam was also an opportunity to solidify the US alliance: "the Vietnam War was conceived unashamedly as an opportunity to solidify that bond and promote the interposition of American military power in the region."

## Conscription and Domestic Opposition

The government introduced two years of compulsory military service for 20-year-old men in late 1964, with selection by birth-date lottery. "An anti-conscription movement formed almost immediately." Opposition grew as American casualties spiralled and after the Tet offensive of early 1968, when President Johnson announced he would pursue negotiated peace and not seek re-election. Nixon's June 1969 withdrawal of the first US forces and the articulation of the Guam Doctrine (placing more responsibility on US allies for their own security) undermined the strategic rationale.

The pinnacle of the anti-war movement came in May 1970 with the first Vietnam Moratorium demonstration, which "drew hundreds of thousands of marchers across the country." In August 1969 the Gallup Poll found for the first time that "a majority of respondents favoured Australia leaving South Vietnam." The movement was led by Labor's parliamentary left, personified by Jim Cairns.

As the Coalition government "trailing behind the White House, wound down Australia's involvement in Vietnam," anti-war energies dissipated and shifted toward supporting a Labor election victory.

## End

When Gough Whitlam won the December 1972 election, "the residual contingent of military advisers stationed in South Vietnam was ordered home" in the first fortnight of the new government; conscription was simultaneously abolished and gaoled draft resisters released.

- produced: mass anti-war movement and generational dissent — "the controversy over Vietnam was the wellspring of an age of dissent"

- produced: domestic questioning of the Cold War framework for Australian foreign policy, accelerating the shift to regional engagement under Whitlam
- contributed_to: Holt's political difficulties during 1967 and Gorton's subsequent leadership problems

## Historiography

John Murphy, *Harvest of Fear: A History of Australia's Vietnam War* (Allen & Unwin, 1993); Peter Edwards, *A Nation at War: Australian Politics, Society and Diplomacy during the Vietnam War 1965–1975* (Allen & Unwin, 1997). The commitment is generally interpreted as a product of Cold War strategic misperception combined with alliance dependence. The anti-war movement's role in reshaping Australian political culture — catalysing new social movements on gender, environment, and Aboriginal rights — is a major theme in 1970s social history.
