# Sack of Baghdad (1258)

In **1258** **[[hulegu|Hülegü]]** ended the **Abbasid Caliphate** with a systematic siege of
**[[baghdad|Baghdad]]** — engineering concentration on the Ajami tower, flooding of the Abbasid
field army's camp, and the execution of Caliph al-Musta'sim (carpet trampling).

## Narrative

After destroying the Ismaili fortresses (1256), Hülegü advanced from Azerbaijan. Caliph
al-Musta'sim, preoccupied and misled by his Shi'a wazir Ibn al-Alqami, failed to prepare.
Baiju crossed the Tigris on a bridge of boats; Abbasid forces were defeated on the plain when
dykes were broken and their camp flooded (possibly with wazir complicity). Siege began 29 January;
catapult fire breached the Ajami tower by 1 February. Al-Musta'sim surrendered; Hülegü executed
him and notables. The city was given over to thirty-four days of pillaging from 13 February.

### Weatherford narrative (2004) — setting, siege, sack

[[weatherford-genghis-khan-making-modern-world-2004|Weatherford]] describes Baghdad as the
largest and richest city in the Muslim world: Abbasid capital founded 762; the Caliph as
thirty-seventh successor to the Prophet Muhammad—titular head of the Muslim world,
emperor-and-pope in one; wealth in palaces, mosques, schools, gardens, fountains, baths, and
bazaars; Muslim majority with Christian churches and Jewish synagogues/schools; both sides of
the Tigris linked by a bridge; massive walls.

**Casus (Weatherford):** after the Assassins’ fall, Hülegü advanced classic Mongol legal
grievances—the Caliph had failed to assist against the Ismailis despite earlier allegiance to
Genghis; if he would not surrender, conquer and capture. The Caliph scoffed that Muslims from
the Maghreb to Morocco would rise and that God would not allow the fall. November 1257: march
on Baghdad with vassal armies of Armenia, Georgia, and Turkic tribes; pontoon boats on
Tigris/Euphrates; by the final week of January 1258 the city was encircled, suburbs occupied,
and filled with refugees.

**Secret Christian contacts (Weatherford FACT attribution):** Hülegü’s mother and two wives
were Christian, as were many of his men and his Georgian/Armenian vassals; Christian envoys
slipped between city and camp; priests were exempted from the kowtow; he “fed the dreams of
Christians and Jews of finally freeing themselves from Muslim domination.” The Caliph sent
**Catholikos Makikha** (Christian patriarch) with a Muslim minister to offer formal submission,
enormous tribute, and Friday prayers in the Great Khan’s name; Hülegü scoffed—too close to
total victory.

**Military means (Weatherford):** date-palm trunks as missiles; deep ditch and rampart (not
enough wood for full Russian-style timber encirclement); gunpowder formula altered for rapid
blast; iron tubes (bamboo insufficient) as handheld and wheeled weapons; ceramic/metal cases
with shrapnel or secondary explosion; smoke bombs, proto-grenades, simple mortars, incendiary
rockets; wall-undermining explosives; preference for fighting from distance. Hülegü destroyed
dams and diverted the Tigris to **flood the Caliph’s army camp**, forcing troops into the city
(Weatherford: psychological “wall of water” analogous to timber walls at Russian cities).

**Chronology (Weatherford):** **5 February 1258** Mongol forces broke through the walls; after
five days the Caliph capitulated (~10 February). Order: surrender weapons, leave goods, march
out; the defending army bolted—Mongols chased and cut them down.

**Looting and Christian role (full Weatherford report):** Hülegü sent **Christian troops** into
the city to collect loot; those who refused evacuation were killed. By Mongol order, churches
and Christian property remained secure from plunder; Hülegü presented one of the Caliph’s
palaces to Catholikos Makikha. Christians inside Baghdad joined fellow believers to loot and
**slaughter Muslims**; they defiled and destroyed mosques and turned many into churches.
Weatherford cites an Armenian chronicler’s exultation: 515 years since founding; Baghdad like
an “insatiable leech” now punished—“the measure of its iniquity was full.” Looting lasted
**seventeen days**; the city was set afire, accidentally or deliberately. Christians were
allowed to destroy the tombs of the long line of Abbasid Caliphs.

**Death of the Caliph (Weatherford; dual variants retained):** Hülegü locked the captive Caliph
for three days without food or water (Grigor of Akanc), then heaped gold before him and ordered
him to eat the gold, scolding him for accumulating wealth not army. He sentenced the Caliph and
his male heirs to death but, as a gesture of rank, allowed bloodless Mongol execution: they
were either **wrapped in carpets** or **sewn into sacks** and kicked to death by warriors or
trampled by horses. (Wiki already notes carpet trampling; Weatherford preserves both carpet and
sack variants.)

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Möngke's directive to subdue the Middle East; Abbasid failure to submit in person;
  Weatherford adds the legal grievance of non-assistance against the Assassins.
- `contributed_to:` [[kitbuqa|Kitbuqa]]'s Syrian operations and the Mamluk response; exploitation
  of Christian/Jewish communal hopes inside and around Baghdad (Weatherford).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **[[ilkhanate|Ilkhanate]]** as a Near Eastern power; end of Abbasid temporal rule.
- `enabled:` Berke's Muslim outrage and Golden Horde–Ilkhanid war; Mamluk–Ilkhanid confrontation.
- **Westward aftermath (Weatherford):** Crusader Bohemond of Antioch attacked Damascus from the
  Mediterranean with supplies for the Mongols; the Seljuk sultan sent an army from Anatolia;
  **Damascus surrendered** and was spared Baghdad’s fate; Mongol warriors reached Mediterranean
  beaches a second time (first 1241 under Batu via Europe). In seven years from
  [[karakorum|Karakorum]], Hülegü conquered or reconquered ~4,000 miles, adding millions of
  Arabs, Turks, Kurds, and Persians. Western limit fixed the next year at
  [[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ayn al-Jalut]] (3 September 1260).

## Historiography

Accounts diverge sharply along religious and political lines: Rashid al-Din's *Jami al-Tawarikh*, written under Ilkhanid Mongol patronage a generation later, is broadly favorable to Hülegü and downplays the massacre's scale, while Mamluk-era Muslim chroniclers (writing from Cairo, the Mongols' great rival) and later authors such as Ibn Kathir present the sack in far more catastrophic terms, with casualty estimates ranging from the low hundreds of thousands to inflated figures approaching two million that cannot be verified against any independent count. The Christian chronicler Bar Hebraeus, writing from a Syriac Christian perspective sympathetic to Mongol rule (which had relieved some Christian communities of Muslim dominance), gives a comparatively measured account. The role of the Shi'a wazir Ibn al-Alqami — portrayed by hostile Sunni sources as a traitorous collaborator who deliberately weakened the city's defenses — remains one of the most historiographically contested points, since the accusation served later Sunni polemical purposes and cannot be separated cleanly from the actual military and administrative failures of the Abbasid court.

**Weatherford (2004)** amplifies the **Christian collaboration and exultation** strand (Makikha,
Armenian chronicler, palace gift, mosque-to-church conversions) as central to the sack’s
character, not a side note. He reports—and **rejects as absurd/apocryphal**—an Armenian
chronicle story that Hülegü ordered 100,000 piglets from Armenia (2,000 per Arab city), forced
Muslims to tend pigs and eat pork, and decapitated refusers: useful only as evidence of
Christian contempt, not Mongol practice.

**Weatherford interpretive claim (THESIS — not wiki fact):** the Mongol army “accomplished in a
mere two years what the European Crusaders from the West and the Seljuk Turks from the East
had failed to do in two centuries,” and “no other non-Muslim troops would conquer Baghdad or
Iraq again until the arrival of the American and British forces in 2003.”
