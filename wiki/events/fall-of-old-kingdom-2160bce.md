# The Fall of the Old Kingdom

The disintegration of the centralized Egyptian state around 2180–2160 BCE,
ending [[old-kingdom-egypt|Old Kingdom Egypt]] and opening the
[[first-intermediate-period|First Intermediate Period]] — one of the classic state
collapses of antiquity, and a key test case in the debate over
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|environmental vs. structural
causes]] of collapse. The fragmentation it opened was ended ~120 years later by the
[[reunification-of-egypt-2040bce|Theban reunification]] under
[[mentuhotpe-ii|Mentuhotpe II]]. Treated in [[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I,
Part 2]] (Ch. XIV, W. Stevenson Smith).

## Narrative

After the extraordinarily long reign of **Pepi II (Phiops II)** — reputedly ~94
years — the monarchy fractured. The **Seventh and Eighth Dynasties** at Memphis
were a rapid succession of ephemeral kings (Manetho's "seventy kings in seventy
days") leaving almost no monuments — the one known building, the small pyramid of
Ibi, was unfinished and uncased. These weak kings leaned on powerful provincial
families (Shemay and Idi at Koptos, raised to vizier). Finally, under the last
Memphite king (Horus Demedjibtowy), the city of **Heracleopolis revolted
successfully and brought the Old Kingdom to an end**, inaugurating the era of
competing regional powers that would eventually pit Heracleopolis against a rising
**Thebes**.

## Causal Analysis

Smith's account is firmly **structural**:
- `caused_by: [[unknown|decentralization]]` — the **nomarchs** (provincial
  governors) had turned their offices into **hereditary** local dynasties,
  building power bases at Elephantine, Coptos, Cusae, and elsewhere; the
  Fifth-Sixth Dynasty creation of "Governor of Upper Egypt" already signalled the
  crown's struggle to hold the south.
- `caused_by: [[unknown|fiscal exhaustion]]` — "immense constructions undertaken
  at royal expense and innumerable funerary endowments **exempt from taxation**
  had exhausted the king's resources"; crown lands fragmented through inheritance
  into ever-smaller units, a "gradual equalization of wealth" draining the centre.
- `contributed_to:` the **over-long reign of Pepi II** itself — a geriatric king
  presiding over a slow hemorrhage of royal authority.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **First Intermediate Period** — collapse of central authority,
  the rise of autonomous nomarchs (Ankhtify at Mo'alla, the Inyotefs at Thebes),
  and eventually the Heracleopolitan vs. Theban struggle resolved by
  [[unknown|Mentuhotpe II's reunification]] (Middle Kingdom, Section 7).
- `produced:` a **pessimistic literature** of lament and social inversion (the
  Admonitions tradition) — the cultural mirror of the breakdown, "foreign to the
  spirit of the times that had gone before."

## Historiography

The competing explanation is **climatic**: Barbara Bell's influential thesis (1971)
attributed the collapse to a sequence of catastrophically **low Nile floods** /
regional drought — supported by famine references (the emaciated figures on Unas's
causeway; the legendary "seven years' famine of Djoser"; First Intermediate
nomarchs boasting of feeding their people). CAH (Smith, 1971) emphasizes the
structural-political causes instead; the two readings frame the case in
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism]]. The period is among the
**darkest in Egyptian history** for contemporary records, reconstructed largely
from the Abydos king-list, decrees, and provincial tomb biographies.
