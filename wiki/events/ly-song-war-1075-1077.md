## Narrative

The war of the 1070s emerged from the specific, novel problem of drawing a formal border where none had existed before, compounded by internal Song bureaucratic factionalism. Under **Ly Nhat Ton** and then the child-king Ly Can Duc (regency of Ly Dao Thanh and, from 1073, Lady Y Lan), Ly authority had expanded into upland border territories (Cao Bang, Lang Son) previously mediated by semi-independent chieftains — a buffer eliminated when the Nung leader Nung Tri Cao's rebellion of the early 1050s was crushed between Ly and Song forces. From the mid-1050s, activist Song border officials (beginning with Xiao Zhu) agitated for military settlement of the frontier question, while the distant Song court maintained an official pacifist "appeasement control" policy, articulated in the 1060s by the official Han Qi as resting on the lesson of the failed 980–981 invasion: negotiation was cheaper and more effective than conquest of remote, unhealthy, uncontrollable territory.

This changed with the rise of the reformer Wang Anshi (1021–1086) under Emperor Shenzong from 1069. Wang considered appeasement "cravenly unprincipled" and, drawing on successful "nibbling" tactics used against Tibetan and southwestern Chinese frontier peoples, authorized escalating military preparation on the Viet border from 1070, cycling through officials (Xiao Zhu, then Shen Qi, then Liu Yi) as each proved either insufficiently aggressive or recklessly overconfident. The immediate trigger was a 1075 Song effort to cut trade routes between Nan Zhao (Yunnan) and Thang Long.

Ly Thuong Kiet, commander of Ly forces, chose to strike first. Late in 1075, a combined land-sea force of 80,000–100,000 Viet soldiers crossed the border, captured the Song ports of Qinzhou and Hepu within three days, besieged and took Nanning (Song's regional headquarters) after a 42-day siege, and destroyed tens of thousands of Song personnel and the assembled Song southern navy before withdrawing with plunder and captives. Ly Thuong Kiet posted proclamations at crossroads citing three grievances: Song's harboring of Ly-defector chieftains, Song's border militarization, and — pointedly — an appeal to Wang Anshi's own domestic political enemies, framing the invasion as sympathetic to those Chinese suffering under Wang's Agricultural Loans and Public Service Acts.

Song, despite vigorous internal dissent (traditionalist officials Fu Bi, Zhang Fangbing, and the naval expert Su Ziyuan all argued against invasion, citing terrain, climate, and the now-demonstrated strength of Viet forces), launched a large retaliatory expedition in late 1076 under Guo Kuei (senior) and Zhao Xie (deputy), roughly 50,000 troops plus over 100,000 porters. The Song army advanced to the Cau River, the last natural obstacle before Thang Long, where the Viets had built multi-layered bamboo defenses and anchored over 400 war boats. A month of failed Song attempts to force a crossing, followed by a Viet night assault that was repulsed with the drowning of two Ly princes, produced a bloody stalemate. With Song forces depleted by disease and the monsoon approaching, a Ly message from the child-king apologizing for border violations and requesting resumed tributary relations gave Song commanders a face-saving pretext to withdraw. Song attempted to hold Lang Son and Cao Bang afterward but was gradually pushed out; a final border settlement was reached in 1088.

## Causal Analysis

The war's outbreak is best understood as **contributed_to** by structural pressures on both sides operating simultaneously: Ly territorial expansion into the border uplands removed the ambiguous buffer zone that had let both empires avoid confrontation, while Wang Anshi's reform-era assertiveness reversed decades of Song frontier passivity at precisely the moment that buffer disappeared. Neither factor alone was sufficient; Taylor is explicit that "the war we have described was a war of mutual aggravation" (line 1552) rather than one-sided aggression. Ly Thuong Kiet's pre-emptive 1075 strike **caused_by** the accumulating Song military build-up under Liu Yi is distinguished from later Song invasion, which was **caused_by** both the humiliation of the 1075 raid and Wang Anshi's own political need to respond.

## Consequence Analysis

The 1075 pre-emptive strike's destruction of the Song southern navy directly **enabled** the successful Viet defense of the Cau River line in 1076–77, since Song forces never achieved naval support for the river crossing. The stalemate and negotiated Song withdrawal **produced** the 1088 border settlement, which Taylor notes was "basically the same as the Sino-Vietnamese border today" — an unusually durable outcome for the period. The war also **enabled** a lasting shift in Sino-Vietnamese relations toward negotiated tribute rather than periodic invasion, a pattern that (per Taylor) proved more typical of the subsequent centuries than war itself.

## Historiography

The most vivid documentation is Ly Thuong Kiet's own campaign, whose casus belli proclamations and the border-consciousness poem later attributed to him ("The mountains and rivers of the south are the dwelling place of the southern emperor...") are treated by Taylor with explicit source-critical caution: the poem's Tang-style Literary Chinese prosody makes it unlikely to have been intelligible to Viet soldiers in the field or to have been composed by Ly Thuong Kiet, who was "neither a literary man nor known to have produced any other literary works" (lines 1577–79); Taylor considers it plausible the sentiment is authentic to the era even if the specific text is a later literary product, and notes its later reuse as an ideological touchstone during the thirteenth-century Mongol crisis when Thang Long claimed status as the last outpost of civilization. On the Song side, the internal debate over the invasion (Fu Bi, Zhang Fangbing, Su Ziyuan's dissents) is unusually well documented and reflects Song bureaucratic factional politics as much as frontier policy — Taylor treats this as evidence that the war cannot be separated from Wang Anshi-era Song court struggles.
