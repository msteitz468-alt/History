# Cuban Revolution (1953–1959)

The armed overthrow of Fulgencio Batista's dictatorship by the 26th of July Movement led by Fidel Castro, culminating in Batista's flight on 1 January 1959 and the establishment of a revolutionary government in Cuba. Che Guevara was a central military commander and political figure; the experience shaped his subsequent theory and practice of revolution.

## Narrative

The revolution began with the failed 26 July 1953 attack on the Moncada Barracks in Santiago de Cuba, for which Fidel Castro and others were imprisoned. After amnesty and exile in Mexico, the movement launched the *Granma* expedition, landing on 2 December 1956 with 82 men. Only a handful survived the initial army attacks and reached the Sierra Maestra mountains in eastern Cuba.

From the Sierra Maestra, the rebels built a rural base, conducted hit-and-run operations, and gradually expanded control while maintaining links to urban underground networks (the Llano). Internal debates between the Sierra military leadership and the Llano political wing were significant. Che Guevara commanded a column and played a prominent role in the 1958 offensive. The capture of Santa Clara in late December 1958 by Che's forces was decisive; Batista fled on 1 January 1959. Rebel columns entered Havana days later.

## Causal Analysis

The Batista regime (1952 coup) was corrupt, repressive, and closely tied to U.S. interests and organized crime. Extreme rural poverty, land concentration, and urban unemployment created fertile ground. The 1954 Guatemalan coup (witnessed by Che) demonstrated to many that electoral or reformist paths were closed. The movement combined armed foco with broader political mobilization and appeals to Cuban nationalism (José Martí tradition).

**caused_by:** the Batista dictatorship and structural inequalities.  
**contributed_to:** U.S. alienation from Batista late in the game and the movement's ability to present itself as a broad democratic front initially.

## Consequence Analysis

The revolution rapidly radicalized: agrarian reform, nationalizations, execution of Batista officials at La Cabaña (Che as commander), break with the United States, declaration of socialist character (1961), alliance with the USSR, and the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962). Cuba became a model and exporter of revolution for Latin America and Africa. Che's later campaigns were direct extensions. The U.S. response (Bay of Pigs, embargo, assassination plots) locked Cuba into the Cold War.

**produced:** [[events/cuban-missile-crisis-1962]] context; [[concepts/focoism]]; the Cuban socialist state.  
**enabled:** Global iconography of Che and Third World guerrilla movements.  
**contributed_to:** Polarization of the Americas during the Cold War.

## Actors

- Primary: Fidel Castro (political/military leader), Ernesto "Che" Guevara (column commander, later minister), Raúl Castro.
- Opposed: Fulgencio Batista and his army/security forces; initially the United States (later overt hostility).

## Historiography

Official Cuban historiography presents a unified, heroic narrative centered on the 26th of July Movement and the Sierra. Early Western accounts often treated it as a classic Latin American *caudillo* story or focused on the romance of the guerrillas. Post-1990s scholarship (including Anderson's biography of Che, using new diaries and interviews) emphasizes internal divisions (Sierra vs. Llano), the speed of radicalization after victory, the role of executions and repression in consolidation, and the contingency of the Soviet alliance. The revolution's place in broader Cold War and decolonization histories is still debated: nationalist anti-imperialist success vs. Soviet satellite vs. unique Caribbean path. Collection coverage of the event itself was previously weak; this source supplies the first dedicated treatment.
