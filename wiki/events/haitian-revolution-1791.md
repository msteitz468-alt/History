# Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)

The successful slave insurrection and independence struggle in French
Saint-Domingue, the single most profitable slave colony in the Americas. It
produced the only permanently successful large-scale slave revolt in modern
history and the second independent republic in the Western Hemisphere.

## Narrative

Revolt began in August 1791 in the colony's rich northern plain, spreading
rapidly amid a complex three-way struggle among enslaved insurgents,
free people of color demanding civil rights, and white planters resisting
both. Revolutionary France's abolition of slavery in its colonies (1794)
briefly aligned the insurgency with Paris under leaders including Toussaint
Louverture, who consolidated control of the colony. Napoleon's 1802
expedition to reimpose French rule and restore slavery reignited full-scale
war; despite Louverture's capture and death in French custody, his
successors — notably Jean-Jacques Dessalines — defeated the French
expeditionary force, ravaged by both combat and yellow fever, and declared
independence on 1 January 1804.

## Causal Analysis

The revolution grew out of Saint-Domingue's uniquely brutal plantation
economy (with import-driven, high-mortality slavery rather than natural
population growth) combined with the ideological rupture and metropolitan
weakness opened by the French Revolution. Free-colored grievances over
exclusion from civil rights under the *Code Noir* provided an additional,
distinct fault line that intersected with the slave uprising rather than
caused it directly.

## Consequence Analysis

Haitian independence permanently ended slavery in the territory and stood as
a existential warning to every other slaveholding society in the Americas,
hardening pro-slavery ideology in the US South and the Caribbean. Napoleon's
defeat there is widely credited as a major factor in his decision to sell
the Louisiana Territory to the United States in 1803.

## Historiography

C.L.R. James's *The Black Jacobins* (1938) established the revolution as a
central event of Atlantic and world revolutionary history rather than a
peripheral colonial disturbance; more recent scholarship (echoed in this
collection's `williams-thesis-abolition-debate` controversy page) debates
the relative weight of slave agency versus metropolitan French politics and
economic decline in explaining both the revolution and later abolition
movements. The collection's coverage of the Americas and the Atlantic slave
system remains thin relative to Old World topics; this page marks a
significant, still-narrow entry point into that gap.
