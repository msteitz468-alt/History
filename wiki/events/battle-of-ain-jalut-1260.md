# The Battle of Ain Jalut (3 September 1260)

At **Ain Jalut** in Galilee, the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]]** army of Egypt defeated a
**[[mongol-empire|Mongol]]** force under **Ked-buqa** — "the first major check to Mongol
expansion" and one of the decisive battles of the thirteenth century.
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Ch. 16c) places it at the climax of the Mongol drive into
the Near East.

## Narrative

In 1258 **Hülegü** had sacked **Baghdad**, ending the Abbasid Caliphate; in 1260 the Mongols
took Aleppo and Damascus. But Hülegü then withdrew the bulk of his army eastward — probably on
news of the Great Khan **Möngke's death** (1259) and the looming succession struggle — leaving
his general **Ked-buqa** with a reduced force. The Egyptian sultan **Qutuz**, urged on by the
**Bahri Mamluks** (including **Baybars**) who had fled there, marched into Palestine and met
Ked-buqa at Ain Jalut. The Mongols were defeated "by a predominantly Turkish army employing
much the same tactics as themselves"; Ked-buqa was killed and the Mongols chased from Syria.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Mongol invasion of Syria** (1260) following the sack of Baghdad.
- `enabled:` **Hülegü's withdrawal** of most of his army after Möngke's death, which reduced
  the Mongol force to roughly the Mamluks' size. The Christians of the coast declared
  **neutrality** (the southern crusader states), letting the Mamluks march unimpeded.

## Consequence Analysis

- It **halted the Mongol conquest** of the Near East: the Euphrates became a long-contested
  frontier where the Il-khans would be turned back again (Homs 1281).
- It **secured the Mamluk Sultanate** and the survival of an independent Islamic power in
  Egypt and Syria, giving Qutuz "retrospective legitimacy" and making him "the chief power in
  Syria."
- It cleared the way for **[[mamluk-sultanate|Baybars]]**, who assassinated Qutuz weeks later
  and built the Mamluk state that would destroy the crusader states (see [[fall-of-acre-1291]]).

Edbury cautions that the Mongol force was a detachment, not Hülegü's main army; Ain Jalut did
not prove the Mongols unbeatable so much as mark the limit of their westward reach — a limit
the Mamluks then defended for decades.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] situates ʿAyn Jalut (Goliath’s Spring) in the Mamluk–Mongol–
Frankish triangle. After Hülegü took Aleppo (Jan 1260) and Damascus (March 1260), local
Christians were said to have celebrated (Eastern Christian Mongol allies; relief from *dhimmi*
status). **Bohemond VI** of Antioch/Tripoli unambiguously allied with the Mongols via his
Armenian father-in-law; Latin Acre maintained **benevolent neutrality**. Hülegü left for the
east (Great Khan succession) leaving **Kitbuga** (Christian) with a reduced force and a pliable
Ayyubid of Homs as Mongol governor of Syria.

Hülegü’s letter demanded **Qutuz** surrender (“we are the army of God on His earth…”). Qutuz
executed the Mongol ambassadors and displayed their heads at Cairo’s gates, then launched a
*jihad* campaign; late July 1260 he marched from Egypt. Acre Franks offered men — Qutuz
declined. On **3 September 1260** Kitbuga attacked twice; Qutuz countercharged; Syrian Ayyubid
troops switched sides; Kitbuga was killed; Mongols fled toward the Euphrates; occupation forces
left Syria. Immediately afterward Qutuz reorganized Syria; **Baybars**, passed over for Aleppo,
killed Qutuz on the Egypt desert border and was proclaimed sultan (publicly enthroned 25 Nov
1260).

**Thesis:** In hindsight it is easy to call ʿAyn Jalut the Mongol high-water mark in the Near
East, but contemporaries “could not be so consoled.” Mongols continued to threaten Mamluk domains
for decades until a Cairo treaty in **1323**.

## Historiography

NCMH V and Edbury stress that the Mongol force was a detachment after Hülegü’s withdrawal, not
proof of Mongol invincibility broken once and for all. May’s operational analysis emphasizes
steppe-trained Mamluk cavalry and Syrian desertions. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] adds
ambassadorial insult, Frankish neutrality/alliance patterns, Qutuz’s refusal of Acre troops, and
the long post-1260 Mongol threat that denies any simple “turning point = end of danger” reading.

### Weatherford (2004)

[[weatherford-genghis-khan-making-modern-world-2004|Weatherford]] gives sparse operational detail
and treats Ayn al-Jalut chiefly as a **strategic limit marker**. FACT as he states it: a Mamluk
army—“mostly slaves purchased via Italian merchants from Kipchak/Slavic Russia for [the]
Egyptian sultan”—moved out from Egypt and met a Mongol **detachment** at Ayn al-Jalut, the
Springs of Goliath, near the Sea of Galilee (modern Israel); on the morning of **3 September
1260**, a year after [[mongke-khan|Möngke]]’s death, “the Mamluks defeated the Mongols. The
empire had reached its western border.” He links the Mamluk manpower pool in part to the
Crimean slave trade that followed the [[mongol-invasion-of-europe-1241|European campaign]]
(Italian merchants buying Mongol prisoners for Egyptian service)—a causal irony, not an
operational reconstruction of the battle.
