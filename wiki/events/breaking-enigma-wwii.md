# Breaking of the Enigma Cipher (WWII)

British codebreakers at [[places/bletchley-park|Bletchley Park]], building on cryptanalytic
techniques developed by Polish intelligence in the 1930s, succeeded during the Second
World War in systematically breaking German military communications encrypted with the
**Enigma** machine. [[turing-alan|Alan Turing]] was a central figure in this effort,
designing the electromechanical **Bombe** device (developing earlier Polish work) to
automate the search for daily Enigma settings, and contributing key theoretical insights
into the machine's vulnerabilities, particularly for the naval variant used by U-boats.

## Impact

The resulting intelligence, codenamed **Ultra**, gave the Allies sustained insight into
German operational planning and is widely credited by historians with shortening the war
by a significant margin — estimates vary, but the effect on the Battle of the Atlantic
(tracking and evading U-boat wolfpacks) was especially direct and consequential. The work
also drove major advances in early computing, including the Colossus machines built later
in the war to break the more complex Lorenz cipher used for high-level German
communications.

## Historiography

The Ultra program remained classified for decades after the war, and its scale and
significance only became publicly known from the 1970s onward (notably following F.W.
Winterbotham's 1974 book *The Ultra Secret*), which substantially revised the historical
understanding of Allied intelligence's contribution to the war's outcome. Turing's own
role received renewed public attention and formal government apology and pardon (2009,
2013) decades after his 1952 prosecution and 1954 death — a history that shaped later
popular and historiographical treatment of the codebreaking effort as inseparable from the
question of his mistreatment by the postwar British state.
