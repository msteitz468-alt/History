## Narrative

The Korea Protectorate Treaty (Eulsa Treaty) of November 17, 1905 stripped Korea of its diplomatic independence and placed its foreign affairs under Japanese control, with [[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]] installed as Resident-General. It was the direct consequence of Japan's victory in the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]]: at Portsmouth, Russia recognized Japan's "paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea." The United States (Taft-Katsura memorandum, July 1905) and Britain (under the renewed [[anglo-japanese-alliance-1902]]) both signaled they would not oppose Japanese primacy in Korea.

### Ito's Mission

On November 2, 1905, [[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]] sent for [[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]] and commanded him to travel to Korea as special ambassador, carrying a letter to Korean Emperor Kojong explaining that peace with Russia had been achieved and that the two empires must draw closer. The letter, while courteous in tone, framed the forthcoming protectorate as a mutual security arrangement for "peace throughout East Asia" (Keene, ch. 56).

On November 15 Itō had his audience with Emperor Kojong — but before Itō could state his mission, Kojong launched a lengthy catalogue of Japanese abuses: Japanese control of Korean banking (through the Daiichi Bank), postal and telegraphic systems, the emasculation of Korean armed forces, the use of military law to shoot Koreans who violated Japanese railway protection orders, and the murder of Queen Min (1895), which the emperor attributed to Japanese complicity even if the actual perpetrators were Korean. Kojong concluded by urging Itō to "put himself in the place of Koreans faced with the present crisis" (Keene, ch. 56).

Itō replied that Korea owed its very survival to Japanese strength — citing the Treaty of Tientsin (1885) and Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895) — and barely concealed his displeasure at having to listen to "tales of long ago" before being permitted to deliver his imperial message. The exchange reveals the fundamental tension: Korea had experienced the Russo-Japanese War as an invasion and occupation of its own territory, conducted ostensibly on its behalf, with increasingly brutal consequences for its population.

### Terms and Consequences

The protectorate treaty effectively ended Korean diplomatic sovereignty. Japan assumed control of Korea's foreign affairs; a Japanese Resident-General (Itō) supervised Korean governance. Japanese-run banking, communications, and security had already been established during the war years. The Korean army had been so "severely reduced by order of the Japanese that they were powerless to suppress even banditry, let alone foreign attack" (Keene, ch. 56). Korean officials who resisted were removed.

Meiji's letter to Kojong had promised that "the security and dignity of Your imperial house will not suffer in the slightest but will be safely preserved" — a promise that the 1910 annexation would break entirely.

### The Ostensible Justification

Throughout the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] and afterward, Japan maintained that it had fought two wars (against China in 1894–95, against Russia in 1904–05) to preserve Korean independence. Keene addresses this directly: "Japan's determination to preserve Korean independence … the Japanese themselves destroyed Korean independence in the year the Russo-Japanese War ended." The "independence" rationale was, in Keene's assessment, "clearly no more than a pretext" (Keene, ch. 54).

### Path to Annexation

Itō remained Resident-General until his assassination by Korean nationalist An Chung-gun in Harbin on October 26, 1909. The assassination, Keene argues, "intensified feelings in Japan that the Koreans were lawless and could not govern themselves" and likely accelerated annexation, though the decision to annex had already been made a year earlier. The formal annexation treaty was signed on August 22, 1910, ending Korean sovereignty (Keene, ch. 59).

## Causal Analysis

The protectorate was the culmination of a strategic logic operating since at least 1876 (the Treaty of Ganghwa): Japan was determined to prevent any other great power from establishing dominance over Korea, which Japanese strategists regarded as a dagger pointed at Japan's heart. Having destroyed Chinese influence (1895) and Russian influence (1905), Japan had no remaining rival in Korea and moved rapidly to convert preponderant military and economic influence into formal colonial control. The key external enabler was the combination of British acquiescence (under the alliance) and American acquiescence (Taft-Katsura) — no great power was willing to challenge Japanese primacy.

## Consequence Analysis

The protectorate destroyed Korean sovereignty in stages: diplomatic independence first (1905), military capacity already curtailed during the war, economic autonomy through Japanese banking control, and finally full annexation (1910). Korean resistance — armed and diplomatic — proved ineffective against Japan's overwhelming military presence. The removal of [[ito-hirobumi]] (through assassination) eliminated the Japanese figure most likely to have argued for a more moderate course, though Keene notes that even Itō, by 1905, was the instrument of protectorate imposition rather than its opponent.

## Historiography

Keene's account draws on Japanese court records, Itō's conversations with Kojong (presumably from Japanese diplomatic archives), and Emperor Meiji's letter to Kojong (reproduced in full). The Korean perspective is represented through Kojong's complaints to Itō and through Keene's retrospective framing of the "Korean independence" pretext. The collection is weak on Korean internal resistance, nationalist movements, and the Korean experience of Japanese colonial rule — essential context that requires Korean-language or specialist sources absent from the current collection.
