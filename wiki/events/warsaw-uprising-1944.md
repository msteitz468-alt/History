# The Warsaw Uprising (August–October 1944)

The citywide rising of the Polish Home Army against the German occupation, launched on 1 August 1944 as the Red Army reached the Vistula — and crushed over 63 days while Soviet forces stood by. German SS and police units, including the Dirlewanger and Kaminski brigades, massacred tens of thousands of civilians in the Wola district in the first week. Distinct from the [[warsaw-ghetto-uprising-1943|Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943]]. Documented for the wiki from [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)]].

## Narrative

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]]: with the Red Army approaching Warsaw after [[operation-bagration-1944|Bagration]] and Stalin proclaiming a rival puppet government, the Home Army — poorly armed (its weapons committed to rural partisan war) and fearing Stalin would brand inaction pro-German — rose on **1 August 1944**, hoping to hold the capital when Soviet forces arrived. Evans notes its commanders "had paid little attention to the ghetto uprising the previous year, and learned nothing from its fate." For two months SS and police units under Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski — employing Ukrainian auxiliaries, Soviet renegades, and convicts drafted from the camps — confined, reduced, and annihilated the insurgent pockets, razing most of the city. An insurgent nurse's testimony, quoted by Evans, describes SS men kicking the heads of the hospital wounded ("Blood and brains were spattered in all directions"), then returning to burn the hospital with the wounded inside. German losses ran to 26,000 killed, wounded, and missing; **Polish dead — men, women, and children — exceeded 200,000**. Himmler ordered the city and population destroyed outright: "it is a blessing that the Poles are doing it," he told Hitler — the chance to end the "Polish problem." The last resisters surrendered 2 October 1944; Wilm Hosenfeld, ordered to interrogate survivors ("I try to rescue everyone who can be saved"), recorded the captured insurgents marching out singing, heads high.

## Historiography

**The Soviet-halt controversy.** Stalin held the Red Army at the Vistula bridgeheads, refused Allied supply flights the use of Soviet airfields, and moved in only after the surrender. Evans's reading is blunt: "From Stalin's point of view, the uprising was a success: it inflicted heavy losses on the Germans, and it wiped out the politically inconvenient Polish Home Army as well." Defenders of the halt cite over-extended supply lines after a 450-mile advance; the debate remains politically charged in Polish–Russian memory.

## Note

Wilm Hosenfeld — the German officer who hid Władysław Szpilman in November 1944 and protected Poles and Jews through his sports-administration post — served in the Warsaw military administration during the uprising; the Soviets convicted him of war crimes on that basis and he died in captivity on 13 August 1952.
