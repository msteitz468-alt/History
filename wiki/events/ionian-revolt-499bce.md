# The Ionian Revolt (499–494 BCE)

The **Ionian Revolt** was the rising of the Greek cities of Asia Minor against
[[achaemenid-empire|Persian]] rule — "the first major occasion on which Greeks tried to
seize the initiative," and, in Herodotus' phrase, the **"beginning of evils for Greeks and
barbarians."** [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 8, Oswyn Murray) treats it as the
opening act of the Greco-Persian conflict that defined Classical Greece.

## Narrative

The trigger was the **failed Persian expedition against Naxos** (499): **Aristagoras**,
tyrant of Miletus (nephew of Histiaeus), had urged it, guaranteed its costs, and — when it
collapsed after a four-month siege — faced ruin. He therefore **organized revolt**, laid
down his own tyranny, and **proclaimed [[isonomia]]** ("equality of rights"); a general
expulsion of the Persian-installed tyrants followed across Ionia. His embassy to mainland
Greece won **Sparta's refusal** ([[cleomenes-i|Cleomenes]] would not commit) but **Athens'
20 ships and Eretria's 5**.

The revolt's high point came in **498**: a Milesian-led force with Athenian and Eretrian
help marched inland from Ephesus and **burned [[sardis|Sardis]]** (the temple of Cybele
accidentally destroyed) before being caught and beaten near Ephesus — after which the
Athenians went home. The fire at Sardis nonetheless spread the revolt to the **Hellespont,
Caria and Cyprus**. The Ionians won the **sea battle off Cyprus** but lost the island on
land; the Carians, after defeats, annihilated a Persian army in the **ambush on the Pedasus
road**. The struggle reached **stalemate** in 496–495.

The end came at **Lade (494)**: the Persians concentrated on Miletus by land and sea, and the
Ionian *koinon* gathered **353 triremes** off the island, choosing the Phocaean **Dionysius**
to train them. But the **Samian and Lesbian contingents defected** in the battle; the fleet
broke, the Chians fighting through. **Miletus was stormed, its men killed or deported to
Mesopotamia, its women and children enslaved**, the oracle at Branchidae burned. A ruthless
reconquest of the islands and Hellespont followed.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **failure at Naxos** and Aristagoras' personal insolvency (the immediate
spark, in Herodotus' personal-politics framing).
contributed_to / enabled: a deeper **political** cause — the demand for [[isonomia]], which
made "freedom from Persia and freedom from tyranny... identified"; and a deep **economic**
cause — the **ruin of mercantile Ionia** under Persian expansion: the conquest of Egypt
(525) broke the [[naucratis|Naucratis]] trade, the Scythian and Thracian campaigns cut the
grain, timber and silver routes, and the "royal markets" of Lydia and Egypt (and their
Greek-mercenary employment) vanished. Murray notes a **class dimension**: the revolt
appealed to the *isonomia*-loving **rowers**, while the hoplites and cavalry (outside
Miletus) would defend only their own cities — a fatal failure to mobilize the land forces.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **sack of Miletus** and "the end of Ionian history" — the cities that had
created Greek poetry, philosophy and science did not recover their eminence for half a
millennium. Artaphernes re-surveyed the cities for fairer tribute and bound them to
**arbitration**; Mardonius (492) replaced the restored tyrants with **"democracies."**
enabled: the **Persian Wars proper** — the burning of Sardis became the symbolic *casus
belli* "justifying" the Persian burning of Ionian temples and later the Athenian Acropolis,
and directing Persian vengeance first at **Athens and Eretria** → the
[[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]] campaign. The defeat also drove **[[miltiades]]** home
to Athens to become the next leader of resistance.

## Historiography

Our sole continuous source is **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]** (Books V–VI), whose
East Greek material Murray analyses as **folk-tale-structured oral tradition** shaped by the
demoralization of a defeated people — hence the self-myth of "Ionian softness." The
**Histiaeus** stories are a separate, largely fabulous *biographical logos*. Archaeology (the
Sardis destruction level, the Paphus siege-mound, the abandoned Miletus harbour), the
Milesian priest-list, the Persepolis tablets, and a possible **league electrum coinage**
supply the controls. Herodotus' apparent **lack of sympathy** for the revolt's *isonomia*
reflects its changed meaning for fifth-century Ionians under the Athenian empire, not simple
bias.

### Hall (2014)

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] follows Herodotus in naming Aristagoras and Histiaeus as prime instigators; Spartan aid failed; Athens sent 20 and Eretria 5 ships; revolt spread Hellespont to Cyprus and sacked Sardis; Lade (494) saw 353 Greek ships vs 600 Phoenician; Samian desertion and Chian stand; Miletus sacked after six-year siege. Shock at Athens (self-styled metropolis of Miletus) illustrated by Phrynichus’s *Capture of Miletus* (493/2), grief and 1,000-drachma fine. Persian imperial style before the revolt: respect for local customs if loyalty and tribute (formalized under Darius) were met; local administrators benefited Ionian aristocracies; Miletus negotiated especially favorable status with Cyrus (Hdt. 1.141.4). Hall also notes Herodotus may back-project an Eretria–Miletus vs Chalcis–Samos alliance (5.99) to justify Eretrian aid in 499. Preceded by Lydian tributary conquest of coastal cities under Croesus and Cyrus’s incorporation of Ionia.
