# Move of the Imperial Capital to Tokyo (1868–1869)

The transfer of the imperial court from Kyōto — the capital since 794 CE — to Edo, which was renamed **Tōkyō** (Eastern Capital) in September 1868. The move was accomplished in stages and was never formally announced as permanent; it was presented as a series of imperial "progresses" to the east, but the emperor never returned to Kyōto as a resident. The transfer was among the most consequential single acts of the Meiji government.

## Narrative

### Ōkubo's Osaka Proposal (February 1868)

The question of the capital arose within weeks of the [[meiji-restoration-1868]]. In February 1868, Ōkubo Toshimichi proposed moving the capital to Ōsaka. His reasoning, as recorded by Keene ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]), was explicit: moving the emperor "would be a sign that the ways of the old regime, associated with the Kyōto nobility, had been rejected in favor of a new and enlightened government. Ōsaka was well suited to commerce with foreign countries, and this would promote a 'rich country and strong army' (*fukoku kyōhei*)." Most important to Ōkubo, moving the emperor out of the *Gosho* (the Kyōto imperial palace enclosure) would end his isolation from his people — "he would, like monarchs abroad, mingle with the common people in the new capital, accompanied by a mere one or two attendants."

The Court Council rejected this proposal on February 17, 1868. The Kyōto nobles, led by Nakayama Tadayasu (the emperor's maternal grandfather), attacked it as a plot by Satsuma and Chōshū to serve private interests. Their deeper motive was attachment to Kyōto, "the focal point of their whole lives."

### The Emperor's Journey to Ōsaka (April–May 1868)

Although the Ōsaka capital proposal was rejected, a partial version succeeded: the emperor was permitted to leave the *Gosho* for the first time since childhood to take symbolic command of the eastern campaign. On February 25, 1868, he visited Nijō Castle — the former symbol of shogunate power in the capital — and on April 14 he set out for Ōsaka as commander in chief.

The journey was itself a transformation: the emperor had never seen the sea. His first glimpse of the Inland Sea on April 19, reviewing the fleet off Mount Tempō, was, Keene writes, "surely one of the happiest days of the emperor's life." He remained in Ōsaka until May 28, receiving foreign ministers, attending military reviews, and meeting Ōkubo and Kido for the first time in substantive political audiences.

When the emperor returned to Kyōto, the brocade pennants above the palace gates were lowered — a symbolic acknowledgment that the military phase was over. But Ōkubo was dissatisfied: he feared the emperor back in Kyōto would be "as remote from his people as in the past."

### The Renaming of Edo and the First Imperial Progress (1868)

After the [[boshin-war-1868]] secured Edo for the imperial government, Edo was renamed **Tōkyō** (Eastern Capital) in September 1868. The emperor made his first "progress" (formal imperial journey) to Tōkyō in autumn 1868 for his coronation ceremony (*sokui no rei*, performed in Kyōto) and the subsequent journey east. The court was careful not to use language implying permanent relocation.

### The Second Progress and Effective Permanence (1869)

The emperor's second journey to Tōkyō in May 1869 effectively made the transfer permanent. Though Kyōto retained the formal trappings of a secondary imperial seat, the court, the government ministries, the foreign legations, and the administrative apparatus of the Meiji state were all in Tōkyō. The emperor did not reside in Kyōto again.

The consequences for Kyōto were visible within years. By 1871, conspirators plotting to restore Kōmei's *jōi* policies cited "the deterioration of Kyōto since the capital had been moved to Tōkyō" as one of their principal grievances ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

## Causal Analysis

The move was not a spontaneous decision but the outcome of a political struggle within the Meiji coalition. Ōkubo had a clear ideological rationale: a modern monarchy required a visible emperor, and visibility required breaking the Kyōto court's ceremonial control. The failure of the Ōsaka proposal led to Edo/Tōkyō by default — the imperial army was already there, the former shogunal infrastructure was available, and Tōkyō's commercial and geographic advantages over Ōsaka were arguably superior.

The Kyōto nobility's resistance was ultimately futile: they had lost the political initiative when the Satsuma-Chōshū alliance supplanted their role at court after the Restoration. Their attachment to Kyōto was a symptom of their broader decline as a political class.

## Consequence Analysis

The move was a civilizational rupture. Kyōto had been the seat of the imperial court since Emperor Kanmu moved there in 794 CE (see [[capital-transfer-nagaoka-784]] for the prior move). The transfer to Tōkyō ended that millennium-long arrangement and placed the imperial institution at the center of a rapidly modernizing urban economy. Tōkyō grew explosively under the Meiji government, absorbing foreign experts, new industries, Western architecture, and the central apparatus of a modern state.

The move also accelerated the marginalization of the traditional *kuge* (court nobility), who had been the Kyōto court's governing class. Stripped of proximity to the emperor and increasingly replaced by domain-origin samurai in the Meiji bureaucracy, the old nobility was effectively ended as a political force.

The symbolic dimension was also significant for foreign relations: the Meiji government in Tōkyō was far more accessible to foreign legations than the isolated Kyōto court had been, facilitating the rapid renegotiation of the unequal treaties that became the central foreign-policy goal of the Meiji decades.

## Actors

- **[[emperor-meiji]]**: Made the journeys; accepted the new capital. His willingness to leave the *Gosho* — unprecedented in his own experience — was a precondition for the transfer.
- **Ōkubo Toshimichi**: The intellectual and political driver of the capital transfer; his Ōsaka proposal, though rejected, established the framework.
- **[[iwakura-tomomi]]**: Managed the court politics that made the transfer possible.
- **Kyōto nobility**: Resisted the change, suffered the consequences.

## Historiography

Keene's account in [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] emphasizes the capital move's connection to the emperor's personal transformation — the first journey is framed through the emperor's delight at seeing the sea and meeting real people — alongside Ōkubo's political logic. The formal historiography of the move is complicated by the Meiji government's deliberate ambiguity: the capital was never formally "moved" but the emperor simply ceased to return to Kyōto. This ambiguity has been read as political caution (avoiding an explicit break with the imperial tradition) and as evidence that the transfer was ad hoc rather than planned.

The move's long-term consequence — Kyōto's decline as a political center — has been partly offset by its role as a cultural and tourist center, but contemporaries experienced the deterioration acutely, as the 1871 conspiracy evidence shows.
