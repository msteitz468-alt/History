# The First Punic War (264–241 BCE)

## Narrative

### The Mamertine Crisis and the Decision for War (264 BCE)

The war's trigger was the **Mamertines** — Campanian mercenaries (originally in
Agathocles' service) who had seized the Greek city of **Messana** on the Sicilian
Straits between 288 and 283 BCE. After **Hiero II** of Syracuse defeated them on the
river Longanus (c. 265 BCE) and advanced on Messana, a Carthaginian admiral installed
a Punic garrison in the citadel with Mamertine approval — blocking Hiero.

Uncomfortable under Punic protection, the Mamertines split: one faction wanted a
Punic agreement guaranteeing independence; the other appealed to Rome. The Roman
**Senate** debated and refused to sanction the proposal — caught between the moral
argument (the Mamertines had done to Messana what Rome's own garrison had done to
Rhegium, which Rome had subsequently annihilated) and the strategic argument (a
Carthaginian Sicily would encircle Italy and threaten Rome's southern allies). The
Senate's failure to act meant the question went to the **comitia** (popular assembly),
which, inflamed by the consul **Appius Claudius Caudex** with promises of booty, voted
for intervention.

C. Claudius crossed the Straits with an advance guard, persuaded (or coerced) the
Punic garrison commander to evacuate — who was subsequently crucified by Carthage for
his restraint. Rome was thus at war with **Carthage and Syracuse simultaneously**.

### The Opening Campaigns (264–263 BCE)

Appius Claudius's initial campaign in Sicily was inconclusive — later tradition
discredits him. In **263**, both consuls crossed with a full army of ~40,000 men.
Sixty-seven Sicilian towns surrendered. **Hiero II**, reckoning that Rome had better
prospects than Carthage, defected: he paid 100 talents, returned Roman prisoners, and
accepted treaty terms making him an allied king of a rump kingdom around Syracuse.
He remained loyal to Rome until his death in **215 BCE** — a friendship of fifty years.

### Siege of Acragas and the Naval Decision (262–261 BCE)

Rome besieged **Acragas** (Agrigento) — the main Carthaginian inland base — through
summer and autumn 262. A relief force under **Hanno** of ~50,000 men including 60
elephants arrived in December; after two months of counter-siege, a hard battle was
fought in which the elephants were thrown into confusion. The city fell; its 50,000
inhabitants were sold into slavery — a brutal act that permanently alienated Greek
sentiment in Sicily.

The deadlock of subsequent operations — Rome could take coastal cities only with
sea-power she lacked — forced a momentous decision: **Rome would build a fleet**.

### The Roman Navy (261–260 BCE)

Rome had virtually no navy; Carthage had ruled the western Mediterranean for
centuries. The Romans allegedly copied a Punic quinquereme that had run aground in
264 (archaeological support exists: a prefabricated Punic ship found off western Sicily
with numbered, pre-cut timbers suggests mass-production methods that Rome could copy).
One hundred quinqueremes and twenty triremes were built in sixty days from the felling
of timber — a stupendous organizational effort. Crews were provided mainly by the
maritime Latin and Italian allies (socii navales); Roman land-soldiers supplied the
boarding parties.

The key Roman innovation was the **corvus** ("crow"): a hinged gangway with an iron
spike, 36 feet long, mounted on a rotating pole in the prow. When dropped onto an
enemy deck, the spike grappled the ships together and Roman soldiers could board. The
corvus turned sea-battles into land-battles — neutralizing Punic seamanship with
Roman infantry.

### Battle of Mylae (260 BCE)

The consul **C. Duillius** took command after his colleague Cn. Cornelius Scipio ("Asina")
was captured at Lipara. Off **Mylae** (near the northeast corner of Sicily), Duillius with
~140 ships met a Punic fleet of ~130 under Hannibal. The Carthaginians, contemptuous
of Roman naval inexperience, attacked without forming battle line. The corvi grappled
the first 30 ships; the rest tried to outmaneuver but were caught in all directions.
Rome sank/captured ~50 ships including Hannibal's flagship — a hepteres formerly taken
from Pyrrhus. About 10,000 Carthaginians were killed or captured.

Rome had won her first naval battle. Duillius received the first **naval triumph** in
Roman history and a **columna rostrata** (column decorated with bronze rams) erected in
the Forum. His inscription survives (early imperial copy of the original); he was never
given a naval command again.

### African Expedition: Regulus (256–255 BCE)

After years of grinding Sicilian warfare, Rome attempted to strike at Carthage directly.
At the **Battle of Ecnomus** (256 BCE) — perhaps the largest naval battle in ancient
history — Rome fielded ~230 quinqueremes against a comparable Punic fleet. Rome's
four-squadron formation (possibly a wedge-spearhead) with corvi prevailed: 30 Punic
ships sunk, 50 captured, Roman losses only 24.

The survivors landed in Africa at **Aspis (Clupea)** on the Cap Bon peninsula, ravaged
the countryside, took 20,000 slaves. One consul returned with the fleet; **M. Atilius
Regulus** was left with 15,000 infantry, 500 cavalry, 40 ships. He defeated a
Carthaginian force at **Adys** on hilly ground that neutralized enemy cavalry and
elephants, then wintered at **Tunis**, within sight of Carthage. Peace negotiations
broke down over Regulus's impossibly harsh terms.

In spring **255 BCE**, Carthage hired **Xanthippus**, a Spartan mercenary commander,
who reorganized the army and insisted on fighting on level ground. With 12,000
infantry, 4,000 cavalry, and 100 elephants — Carthaginian citizens fighting for their
city's survival — he routed Regulus's army on the plain. The phalanx held behind a
screen of elephants; the superior cavalry enveloped and annihilated the Romans. Regulus
and 500 were captured; only 2,000 escaped to Clupea. Regulus died in captivity; the
later legend of his voluntary return to Carthaginian torture was probably invented to
obscure the fact that his widow tortured two Punic prisoners in Rome.

The rescue fleet (210 ships) beat a Punic interception off Cap Bon, rescued the
survivors — then was caught by a storm off Cape Pachynus. Of 264 ships, only 80
survived. Some **95,000 men** drowned (soldiers + rowers). Rome's
loss at sea from weather in this war vastly exceeded her losses to the enemy.

### Stalemate and the Long Sicilian War (254–242 BCE)

Rome rebuilt her fleet (220 ships by 254). Cn. Scipio Asina captured **Panormus**
(Palermo) — 14,000 inhabitants ransomed, 13,000 enslaved. Another great storm in 253
destroyed 150 ships. Both sides were exhausted.

The turning point came at **Panormus (250 BCE)**: **L. Caecilius Metellus** lured
Hasdrubal's army (including 142 elephants) into prepared trenches near the city wall.
Missiles maddened the elephants; they stampeded back through their own lines; Metellus
sallied out and inflicted a catastrophic defeat. 142 elephants were captured and
displayed in Rome's Circus — the people's first sight of African elephants.

In **249 BCE** **P. Claudius Pulcher** attempted a dawn surprise attack on the Punic
fleet at **Drepana** (Trapani). The attack failed; 93 Roman ships were captured —
the only serious Roman defeat at sea in the war. Pulcher had allegedly thrown the
sacred chickens overboard when they refused to eat ("Let them drink if they won't eat").
A second consul's fleet was wrecked by storm off Cape Pachynus: Rome was left with
almost no navy.

For the next several years, **Hamilcar Barca** (commanding from 247) waged a
brilliant guerrilla campaign from the mountain fortress of Heirkte behind Panormus,
raiding the Italian coast as far as Cumae while maintaining his position in western
Sicily. Rome could not evict him. The besieged Lilybaeum held for eight years.

### Battle of the Aegates Islands and the Peace (242–241 BCE)

Recognizing that only naval victory could break the deadlock, the Roman Senate funded a
new fleet by senatorial loan (groups of two or three wealthy men each financing one
quinquereme). Two hundred new, lighter ships — built on the model of a captured Punic
vessel of Hannibal the Rhodian — were ready by 242, **without corvi** (showing Rome had
learned Punic methods of sea-fighting).

The consul **C. Lutatius Catulus** blockaded Drepana and Lilybaeum. In spring **241 BCE**
the Carthaginians sent a supply/relief fleet of ~170 ships, undermanned and freighted
with stores. Off the **Aegates Islands** (Egadi) on the west coast of Sicily, Catulus
attacked despite heavy seas. The Punic fleet, weighed down with cargo, was quickly
defeated: 50 ships sunk, 70 captured (~10,000 prisoners). Punic admiral Hanno escaped
with 50 ships and was crucified at Carthage. **The war was over after 23 years.**

### Peace Terms (241 BCE)

Hamilcar negotiated directly with Catulus. First draft: Carthage evacuates Sicily;
does not make war on Hiero or his allies; returns all Roman prisoners without ransom;
pays 2,200 Euboeic talents over 20 years. Rome's popular assembly rejected these terms
as too lenient and sent ten commissioners to stiffen them: 1,000 talents added to be
paid immediately; time cut to 10 years; all islands between Sicily and Italy (Lipari,
Aegates) also evacuated.

Polybius's more formal account of the final treaty (III.27.2–6) adds mutual
non-aggression toward each other's allies. This ambiguity — whether later allies were
covered — became the legal dispute over Saguntum that triggered the Second Punic War.

### Roman Seizure of Sardinia (238 BCE)

Two years later, Carthage's discharged mercenaries revolted in **Africa and Sardinia**
([[mercenary-war-241-237bce|the Mercenary War, 241–237 BCE]]). While Carthage was
fighting for its survival, rebel mercenaries in Sardinia were expelled by the natives,
crossed to Italy, and appealed to Rome. Rome suddenly reversed its friendly policy,
voted to seize Sardinia, and threatened Carthage with a new war when Carthage protested.
Virtually without a fleet, Carthage capitulated: **Sardinia and Corsica were ceded and
1,200 more talents paid**.

Polybius calls this "contrary to all justice" — unprovoked aggression and treaty-
breaking. It is one of his most explicit moral condemnations of Roman policy. The
settlement permanently embittered relations and set the stage for Hamilcar's Iberian
strategy and the Second Punic War.

## Causal Analysis

The war was not the product of long-term Roman imperial ambition toward Carthage or
Sicily. Scullard argues both powers **drifted into conflict** through a series of
contingent episodes: Mamertine appeal → Roman intervention → Punic counter-
intervention → war. The senate's hesitation in 264 was genuine; the comitia vote was
driven partly by Claudius Caudex's personal ambition and the lure of booty.

The **structural background** was the progressive elimination of the smaller states
between the two powers (Tarentum, Syracuse's independence reduced to a client kingdom)
until Rome and Carthage were face-to-face across the Straits of Messana. The war was
**enabled** by the end of the Pyrrhic War and Rome's mastery of southern Italy; it
was **triggered** by the Mamertine appeal and the dynamics of the Sicilian situation
in the 260s.

The Roman seizure of Sardinia in 238 was a different matter: **pure opportunism**,
Polybius's judgment — Rome exploiting Carthaginian weakness during the Mercenary War.

## Actors

- **[[roman-republic|Roman Republic]]**: Senate provided strategic direction but showed
  no grand design; annual commands limited strategic continuity; no outstanding Roman
  generals emerged (Polybius); the Roman citizen-soldier and Italian allies provided
  the manpower that outlasted Carthaginian mercenaries
- **[[carthage|Carthage]]**: commercially motivated, wanted peace; maritime power
  without peer until Rome built a fleet; undermined by political instability (Hanno
  vs. Hamilcar factions) and over-reliance on mercenary forces; showed extraordinary
  resilience in rebuilding after catastrophic naval losses
- **[[hiero-ii-of-syracuse|Hiero II of Syracuse]]**: the war's decisive swing actor;
  his defection from the Punic alliance in 263 gave Rome its Sicilian logistics base;
  his fifty-year loyalty was foundational to the Roman position in Sicily
- **Hamilcar Barca**: the most capable Carthaginian commander; his Heirkte/Eryx
  guerrilla (247–241) was militarily impressive; his sense of injustice at the peace
  and Sardinia drove his Spanish strategy
- **C. Duillius**: won Mylae (260) and Rome's first naval triumph; his columna rostrata
  commemorated in the Forum
- **M. Atilius Regulus**: the African expedition — brilliant in conception, disastrous
  in execution; his inflexibility in negotiation and failure to use Numidian allies
  doomed the campaign; the legend of his honourable death made him a Roman hero
- **Xanthippus**: the one non-Roman general of the war whose intervention was
  operationally decisive (defeat of Regulus 255)

## Historiography

The primary narrative source is **Polybius** (Books I–I, 62), who used the pro-Roman
**Fabius Pictor** and the pro-Carthaginian **Philinus of Agrigentum** as his two
principal sources, explicitly acknowledging both were partisan. Polybius's own
assessment — that Rome won through moral qualities and institutional superiority of the
citizen army — is a structural argument that influenced all subsequent accounts.

The war's chronology is complicated by the unreliability of the Roman calendar in
this period. Morgan (1977) argues the Roman calendar was as much as a month ahead
of the Julian in the early years of the war; this affects exact dating of battles.

The question of Rome's strategic intent in entering the war — whether there was a
conscious Sicilian ambition or mere reactive contingency — is an active debate. Heuss's
view (1949) that Carthage had no hostile intentions toward Italy, and that Rome
therefore need not have feared encirclement, is a revisionist position; the majority
view sees Roman fears as real even if exaggerated in Polybius's account.

The collection's coverage is strong: CAH VII.2 Ch. 11 (Scullard) is the authoritative
treatment; Scullard's *History of the Roman World 753–146 B.C.* (1980) provides a
companion narrative. Philinus's work does not survive; its reconstruction from
Polybius's citations is a major historiographical project.
