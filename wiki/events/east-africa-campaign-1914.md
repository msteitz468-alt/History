# East Africa Campaign (1914–1918)

The longest and most famous of the sub-Saharan African campaigns of the First World War. It began with the first British shot in Togoland (12 Aug 1914) context but unfolded as a four-year contest across a vast tropical theatre, ending with the last German surrender of the war (Lettow-Vorbeck, 25 Nov 1918 at Abercorn).

## Narrative

**1914–1915 opening.** Governor Schnee favoured coastal neutrality/open towns to protect economic achievements; Lettow-Vorbeck (general-staff appointee) insisted on using the colony as a distraction for Britain. Tension immediate. "On 5 August Schnee knew that Britain and Germany were at war." Königsberg put to sea. Tanga (2–5 Nov 1914): IEF B under Aitken landed exhausted after long voyage; dense bush, poor naval support, Indian units disintegrated on left; Lettow committed reserves for envelopment; British re-embarked abandoning stores. "Aitken had handed his adversary a major victory." Jasin (Jan 1915) German success but costly (15% losses). Königsberg blockaded and sunk July 1915 in Rufiji; crew/guns transferred to Lettow. Rubens blockade-runner (Mar 1915) delivered critical ammunition despite British pursuit. "the bulk of her cargo... was brought ashore." Raids on Uganda railway (48 attacks by May 1916). British remained largely defensive under Kitchener policy; colonial administrators divided.

**1916 Smuts offensive.** After South-West Africa conquest, South African political needs aligned with imperial desire for full conquest. Smuts (political general) replaced Smith-Dorrien; command amateur-heavy, logistics separated from field HQ. "Smuts’s aim was to manoeuvre rather than to fight." Northern thrust (Kilimanjaro area, Kondoa Irangi, Pangani river, Handeni, Morogoro, Ulugurus, Mgeta); Belgian Tombeur from west took Tabora; Northey from SW. "the British crossed the Rufiji on 17 January [1917]." But "what Smuts saves on the battlefield he loses in hospital, for it is Africa and its climate we are really fighting not the Germans." Tsetse, rains, porters (135k–175k needed on Rufiji), disease (half South African units lost in months). Germans retreated south of central railway, preserving force. Wahle’s epic march from Tabora linked with Lettow. Wintgens/Naumann raids drew thousands of pursuers across huge distances ("Naumann... had conducted a classic guerrilla operation... marched almost 3,200 kilometres").

**1917–1918 final phase.** Smuts departed claiming victory; Hoskins rebuilt (Africanization: Nigeria Regiment, expanded KAR to 22 bns). Lettow crossed Ruvuma into Portuguese Mozambique, living off land, evading traps. Portuguese forces (Gil) checked at Newala by Looff. British naval blockade and supply from Dar es Salaam/Kilwa/Lindi. Lettow sustained ~1,100 Europeans + 7,300 askaris fit by end-1916 declining further but cohesive. Final surrender only after European armistice: "On 25 November 1918, two weeks after the signature of the armistice in Europe, at Abercorn in Northern Rhodesia Colonel Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck surrendered, the last German commander of the war to do so."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` extension of European war to colonies (no local casus belli); British CID priority on cruiser threat and wireless (Tanga/Dar es Salaam objectives); Lettow's professional view that colony must serve European war by distraction ("It and the great war can react off each other"). 

`contributed_to:` pre-war German colonial development (railways enabled concentration); Schütztruppen training and askari loyalty (wives/children policy); resupply via Rubens/Marie; British command discoordination and underestimation of terrain/porters (Tanga, Smuts' envelopments repeatedly evaded).

`preceded_by:` 19th-century colonial conquest methods; Maji-Maji/Herero precedents for German rule and African resistance.

The campaign was never a sideshow for participants: "relatively the impact of the war on the dark continent was as great as that on Europe, that few black families were unaffected."

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` conquest and partition of German East Africa (British mandate Tanganyika, Belgian Ruanda-Urundi, Portuguese Kionga); British acquisition of key infrastructure (central/northern railways, ports).

`produced:` unprecedented African mobilization — "somewhere over 2 million Africans served... upwards of 200,000 of them died." Porters often majority of "ration strength"; disease (dysentery, malaria) primary killer.

`enabled:` long-term erosion of colonial legitimacy and tribal structures; broadening of "new black elites that were urbanized, westernized, and politically aware." Prelude to decolonization.

`concurrent_with:` other colonial theatres (Cameroons, South-West Africa) that tied down Entente resources until 1916.

`part_of:` global First World War; "the First World War was far more than just a European conflict."

## Actors

- **Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck**: Schütztruppen commander; prioritised European distraction over colony preservation; sought decisive battles early (Tanga, Jasin) then mobile defence. "Lettow-Vorbeck’s brilliant campaign... was the climax of Africa’s exploitation: its use as a mere battlefield."
- **Heinrich Schnee** (Governor): favoured protecting colonial development and neutrality; clashed with Lettow over coast and open towns.
- **Jan Smuts**: South African political general; manoeuvre-focused, casualty-averse; achieved territorial conquest but not destruction of enemy force.
- **Louis Botha** (SWA parallel): used Boer mobility successfully in smaller theatre.
- **A.E. Aitken** (Tanga): lost nerve after initial progress; re-embarked.
- African askaris/porters on all sides: principal fighters and sustainers; "the Great War in Africa... was fought principally by the Africans themselves."

## Historiography

Strachan emphasises that Lettow was no pure guerrilla theorist but a conventional officer seeking envelopment/decisive battle within constraints; reputation amplified post-war by German militarism, Boer parallels, and later insurgency lenses. British accounts stressed climate/disease to mask command and Indian troop issues. African perspectives (porters, askari loyalty to Germans among Beti etc.) underrepresented in contemporary sources. "the African campaigns of the First World War bore more relationship to the nineteenth-century campaigns of colonial conquest than they did to the Great War itself." Collection coverage note: Strachan provides operational detail and quotes; gaps remain in African agency and post-1918 impacts.
