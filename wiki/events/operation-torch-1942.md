# Operation Torch (November 1942)

The first major Allied amphibious invasion of the war in the European theater. US and British forces landed in French Morocco and Algeria to open a second front, secure the Mediterranean, and set conditions for the defeat of Axis forces in North Africa. The landings (November 8, 1942) initiated the "novice" phase of Allied Mediterranean logistics, exposing profound shortfalls in planning, beach and port organization, service force strength, and transportation that shaped the subsequent race for Tunis and the entire theater's development.

## Narrative

Operation Torch was the United States' first major offensive actions against Axis European forces. President Roosevelt decided to proceed on July 25, 1942; logistics planning began shortly thereafter at a "frantic pace" with only three months until the landing date. "Neither the British nor the American General Staffs would care to offer Torch as a model of planning."

The operational objectives were to execute landings, secure ports and lines of communication, then trap Rommel's forces between the British First and Eighth Armies before winter rains and before significant Axis reinforcement of Tunisia. The theater was a joint US-British venture, but each nation maintained independent parallel support systems due to incompatible supply systems.

Three task forces executed simultaneous landings across a vast area:

- **Western Task Force** (American, ~44,760 men under Major General George S. Patton Jr.): US 3rd and 9th Infantry Divisions, 2nd Armored Division. Landed western French Morocco at Safi, Casablanca, Fedala, Mehdia, and Port Lyautey. Mission: occupy ports and airfields; prepare to occupy Spanish Morocco if needed. Support commanded by Brigadier General Arthur Wilson.

- **Center Task Force** (~37,000 Americans + 3,600 British under Major General Lloyd Fredendall): US 1st Armored Division, 1st Infantry Division, 1st Ranger Battalion. Landed beaches around Oran, Algeria, to seize the port and airfields. Support commanded by Brigadier General Thomas Larkin.

- **Eastern Task Force** (predominately British ~45,000 + 10,000 US under initial US command of Major General Charles Ryder of the 34th Infantry Division, later British First Army): British 78th Division, 6th Commando; US 168th and 39th Combat Teams. Landed Algiers area to seize the city and airfields at Blida and Maison Blanche, then drive east into Tunisia (up to 400 miles).

Landings began in the early morning hours of November 8, 1942. Vichy French resistance was limited and uncoordinated; Admiral Darlan ordered cease-fire by November 10-11. French naval sorties were beaten back. The surprise prevented a stronger defense, but the real test was sustainment.

**Beach and port operations were chaotic across all sectors.** Ships arrived out of order; navy coxswains landed on wrong beaches or wrong ships in darkness. Beach parties were often not landed with assault battalions or failed to perform due to lack of leadership, planning, and training. At Fedala (Western), rising surf stranded landing craft; "Conditions on the beaches during the night presented a scene of indescribable confusion." No beach parties on at least three beaches. Supplies piled up mixed and unmarked; "The shore party had neither the workforce nor the vehicles to remove matériel from the beaches and ports to inshore dumps."

By end of first day: only 39 percent of troops, 16 percent of vehicles, and just over 1 percent of supplies ashore in key sectors. At Fedala, 225 craft stranded on two beaches; "Of all the landing craft destroyed or damaged at Fedala, not a single one was hit by enemy fire." Losses from inexperience, high beaching, underwater obstacles, and lack of tugs/recovery craft. High surf (4-6 feet by Nov 9) suspended operations on some days.

Ports were equally disastrous. Trained stevedores were scarce. The Calvert took five days to unload. At Casablanca/Fedala, "a continuous pile of miscellaneous supplies on the piers of Casablanca that averaged 10 feet high, 20 feet at the base, and approximately 700 yards in length" (by Nov 16), including a "lethal mix" of steel matting, aviation fuel, Vienna sausages, .45 ammunition, and 105-mm incendiary. The D+5 convoy (arriving Nov 18) found "as though some gigantic overhead scoop full of supplies had suddenly emptied its contents." No American ships entered Fedala or Casablanca harbors on Nov 11-12 despite the critical buildup period.

Patton spent 18 hours on beaches D-Day trying to motivate clearance. On D+1 he recorded "The beach was a mess and the officers were doing nothing." It took 45 days to bring order; docks were "in really fine shape" only by Dec 23.

Center Task Force faced parallel problems. The 48th Surgical Hospital personnel landed dispersed over three miles; no medical equipment arrived until D+4. Graves registration denied by G3 "on the grounds that only combat forces were important." The Engineer Amphibian Brigade concept (boat + shore regiments) failed in execution: British C2 and crews; units untrained for shore duty; supplies dumped indiscriminately "to the closest dump regardless of type"; "There was no coordinated control over the landing area and little security for stocks."

Eastern (Algiers) landings were somewhat smoother due to rehearsals and light resistance, but beaches still "chaotic. Supplies were dumped with little thought or plan." British throughput was limited.

The fundamental issue: "no one had realized just how big a task the clearing of the beaches would be and as a result had not planned for the right force to take care of this job." On first two convoys, Center Task Force had "only 260 supply personnel in a total force of more than 60,000." Local Arab labor proved "limited, unskilled, and in need of constant supervision."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Interwar neglect of service forces (US Army <12% service in 1942 vs 34% in 1918 AEF) and navy focus on capital ships over assault shipping and logistics. 

`contributed_to:` Eisenhower/Clark decision (endorsed by Eisenhower) to prioritize combat units over cargo trucks ("Half of the cargo trucks were to be left on the piers") due to fears of German move into Spain or attacks from Spanish Morocco. Patton's substitution of Jeeps for deuce-and-a-halfs ("one deuce and a half equaled ten Jeeps").

`preceded_by:` Late tactical plans; no central approved plan until <48 days before sail; logistics planners working from guesses and National Geographic data. Last-minute changes (e.g., Oct 16 ration reduction to 45 days) with no regard for "the physical restraints of port capacity and convoy limitations."

`concurrent_with:` Battle of the Atlantic shipping losses and competing global demands (Russia aid 4M+ tons).

The "physics of time and distance" overtook planners. Patton provided little guidance on logistics.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Immediate stalling of advances (e.g., Western TF attack on Casablanca halted D+1 for lack of food, water, fuel, transport despite little opposition). British First Army reached 50 miles from Tunis by Nov 16 but "Both the British and US logistic systems were stretched to the limit." "An Allied attack on November 24 failed"; German counteroffensives Nov 27 and Dec 1 forced withdrawal. Eisenhower paused for winter to build sustainment.

`enabled:` Establishment of base sections and communications zone in early 1943 (Mediterranean BS at Oran under Larkin; Atlantic BS at Casablanca under Wilson; Eastern BS at Constantine under Pence). TUP vehicle assembly (59% space savings) and rail reconstruction (locomotives reassembled in 76 hours) began the recovery. French rearmament program added hundreds of thousands of troops but massive new sustainment burden.

`contributed_to:` [[tunisian-campaign-1943]] (the "race" for Tunis was lost in part due to logistics; "The limiting factor in the fight for North Africa was shipping capacity."). Exposed need for balanced assault forces with port and beach units from D-Day. Lessons in "tyranny of distance" and requirement for service forces directly shaped later operations.

`part_of:` [[world-wars-era]] and the broader Allied Mediterranean strategy.

The operation succeeded in securing the lodgment and French cooperation but at the cost of a delayed drive on Tunis that allowed Axis reinforcement. "Had this same situation occurred facing a determined adversary, the entire invasion force might well have been pushed back into the sea."

## Actors

- **[[eisenhower-dwight-d]]**: Theater commander (AFHQ); approved final supply plan Oct 27 (after convoys sailed); made truck-reduction decision; recognized race for Tunis but paused operations in December to allow support buildup.
- **[[patton-george-s]]**: Western Task Force commander; "I don’t know anything about logistics. You keep me out of trouble." Personally worked beaches D-Day; later noted docks "in really fine shape" only after 45 days.
- **[[larkin-thomas]]**: Commander, Center Task Force SOS (later Mediterranean BS then SOS NATOUSA). Key figure in early base section development and theater sustainment.
- Arthur Wilson: Atlantic BS commander (Casablanca); later criticized lack of supply consideration "beyond the landing of troops on the beaches."
- Other service leaders: Brig. Gen. Arthur Pence (Eastern BS); Maj. Gen. Humfrey Gale (British CAO AFHQ); Maj. Gen. Everett S. Hughes (US deputy theater commander from Feb 1943); Brig. Gen. Carl R. Gray Jr. (Military Railway Service).

## Historiography

The landings succeeded politically and in securing the initial lodgment largely due to French weakness and weather luck (calm seas in some areas, "the calmest sea conditions of the past sixty-eight years"). Combat narratives often overlook that "the part of the fleet supporting the Western Task Force damaged or destroyed 242 of the 378 landing craft used in the operation" with almost no enemy action. Dworak emphasizes command failure at senior levels (Eisenhower downward) to plan for a "balanced assault force—one that could not only perform its combat mission but also sustain itself from the moment it landed on the beaches."

Key debates center on whether the truck reduction was avoidable given global shipping constraints and Spanish threat uncertainty, and on the balance between combat and service forces in the initial echelons. The "novice" phase is presented as necessary trial-and-error that enabled later success: "the Allies had much to learn before attempting the next amphibious landing."
