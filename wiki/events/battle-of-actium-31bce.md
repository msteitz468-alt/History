# The Battle of Actium (2 September 31 BCE)

The naval battle off western Greece in which **Octavian** and **Agrippa** broke the
fleet of **Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII** — the decisive engagement of the last
Roman civil war, leaving Octavian sole master of the Roman world and opening the road
to the **Principate**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 1 (Pelling).

## Background: the final breakdown

After the elimination of **Sextus Pompeius** and **Lepidus** (36) left only two
triumvirs, the partnership of Antony and Octavian dissolved. Antony's failed
**Parthian campaign** (36) and his liaison with **Cleopatra** — the
**[[ptolemaic-egypt|Donations of Alexandria]]** (34), the divorce of Octavia (32) —
gave **Octavian a propaganda triumph**, letting him cast Antony as the slave of a
foreign queen. In 32 the consuls and ~300 senators fled to Antony; Octavian had **all
Italy and the West swear a personal oath of allegiance** to him (*coniuratio Italiae*,
the *Res Gestae's* claim of *consensus Italiae*), opened Antony's will (revealing his
wish to be buried in Alexandria), and declared war — **on Cleopatra alone**, by a
revived **fetial rite**.

## Narrative

Antony massed a huge army and fleet on the **west coast of Greece**, his main fleet at
**Actium**, expecting (like Pompey in 48) Octavian to come to him and be harried at
sea. Instead **Agrippa struck first and fast** in 31 — taking Methone, Leucas, Patrae,
and Corinth — while Octavian crossed unopposed and seized the strongpoint above
Actium. Antony was soon **blockaded**, his camp ravaged by **disease, hunger, and
desertion** (Ahenobarbus and the client kings slipping away). With his army wasted, a
**sea-battle became his only option** — realistically, a **break-out**, not a victory.

On **2 September** Antony's fleet (~200–250 large ships) faced Octavian's larger,
nimbler fleet (~400). After hours of waiting for the afternoon wind, **Cleopatra's
squadron hoisted sail and drove through the centre**; Antony followed with perhaps a
hundred ships. The rest of his fleet, leaderless, soon surrendered; the abandoned army
went over within days. Casualties were small (~5,000) — "a very lame affair," but a
decisive campaign lost.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **breakdown of the triumvirate** and Octavian's superior politics
(Italy and the West won by propaganda and the oath); the **immediate cause** of the
defeat was **Agrippa's swift opening campaign**, which turned Antony from likely victor
into a blockaded loser within weeks. Pelling stresses that, before the campaign,
**Antony still looked the stronger** — the military odds were on him.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **collapse of Antony's cause** and, a year later, the **fall of
Alexandria** and the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra (30) — and the **end of a century
of civil war**.
enabled: **Octavian's sole rule** and the construction of the **[[roman-republic|Principate]]**
(Augustus). Pelling's verdict: Octavian's mastery of propaganda "may not have won him
the war, but it did much to win the ensuing peace. For Rome, the right man won."

## Historiography

No contemporary narrative survives; the sources (Plutarch's *Antony*, Cassius Dio,
the Augustan poets — Horace, Virgil, Propertius) are shaped by **Octavian's victory
propaganda**, which inflated a near-bloodless break-out into an epic triumph over the
"eastern menace." Pelling reconstructs the campaign as decided in its first weeks by
Agrippa, not in the battle itself.
