# Caesar's Conquest of Gaul (58–50 BCE)

The eight-year campaign in which **[[julius-caesar|Caesar]]** subjugated all of Gaul
to the Rhine and the Atlantic — the largest single conquest of the Republic, the
making of Caesar's army, wealth, and political power, and a catastrophe for the
Gallic peoples. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 10 (Wiseman); the primary
account is Caesar's own **Commentaries**.

## Narrative

- **58 — the Helvetii and Ariovistus.** Caesar stopped the migration of the
  **Helvetii** (defeated at Bibracte) and then turned on the German king
  **Ariovistus** — a "friend of the Roman People" — driving him back across the Rhine.
- **57 — the Belgae.** He marched north against the **Belgic** coalition, narrowly
  winning the desperate battle against the **Nervii** on the Sambre; the **Atuatuci**
  were stormed and **53,000 sold into slavery**. Caesar declared Gaul conquered "in
  two seasons."
- **56–55 — the periphery.** The maritime **Veneti** were crushed; **young P.
  Crassus** subdued **Aquitania**; Caesar bridged and crossed the **Rhine** into
  Germany, and made two expeditions across the Ocean into **Britain** (55, 54) — feats
  that astonished Rome and won unprecedented thanksgivings.
- **54–53 — revolt and reprisal.** A rising under **Ambiorix** destroyed a legion and
  a half (the Eburones' attack on the winter camps); Caesar took bloody vengeance,
  recrossing the Rhine and devastating the Eburones.
- **52 — Vercingetorix and Alesia.** The Arvernian noble **Vercingetorix** united
  Gaul in a great revolt with a scorched-earth strategy (the burning of Avaricum,
  the repulse at **Gergovia**). Caesar besieged him in **Alesia**, building **double
  rings of fortification** to hold off both the garrison and a vast relief army, and
  won the decisive victory; Vercingetorix surrendered.
- **51–50 — pacification.** Mopping-up (Uxellodunum, where the defenders' **hands
  were cut off** as a warning) cowed the last resistance. Gaul was conquered.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the genuine **frontier instability** (Helvetian migration, Germanic
incursions) that justified intervention; contributed_to by **Caesar's own need** for
the glory, plunder, and army that conquest would bring — Wiseman stresses he repeatedly
went **beyond defensive necessity**, attacking even a Roman "friend" (Ariovistus) and
laying himself open to charges under his own provincial law.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **Roman province of Gaul** to the Rhine; a vast **demographic
catastrophe** (mass enslavement and slaughter); and for Caesar, a **battle-hardened,
personally loyal army** and an **immense fortune** poured into bribery, loans, and
the building of Rome.
enabled: the **military and financial power base** for the
[[roman-civil-war-49-45bce|civil war]] (Cycle 6) — the Thirteenth Legion would cross
the Rubicon.

## Historiography

Caesar's seven-book ***Bellum Gallicum*** (with an eighth by Hirtius) is the central
source — a literary masterpiece and a **work of political propaganda**, written
largely year-by-year for the Roman electorate, foregrounding *populi Romani maiestas*
and vengeance for slain Italian *negotiatores*, promoting **Labienus**, and softening
defeats (Gergovia). Wiseman reads it as campaign literature aimed at the voters of
Rome and Italy who supplied Caesar's troops.
