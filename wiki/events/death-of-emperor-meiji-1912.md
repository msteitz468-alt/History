## Narrative

Emperor Meiji died shortly after midnight on the morning of July 30, 1912, of heart failure
following progressive uremia, diabetes, and chronic hepatitis. He was fifty-nine years old. His
funeral, held September 13–15, 1912, at the Aoyama parade grounds in Tokyo and culminating in
interment at Fushimi Momoyama near Kyoto, marked the end of a forty-five-year reign that had
transformed Japan into a great power.

### The Final Illness

Signs of failing health had been accumulating since at least 1911. Emperor Meiji canceled
military reviews on medical advice, found stairs exhausting, and clung to routine while visibly
deteriorating. At a Privy Council session on July 15, 1912, he fell asleep multiple times at his
seat — an event that shocked ministers and advisers. On July 19 he staggered and fell at the
dinner table, going into a coma. The physicians diagnosed uremia and revealed to the nation for
the first time on July 20 that the emperor was critically ill, disclosing that he had suffered
from diabetes since 1904 and chronic hepatitis since 1906. His physicians defended their failure
to treat him aggressively earlier by noting that "although they went every morning to the palace
intending to examine him, he always refused, saying he did not need an examination. They had not
dared to oppose his commands."

Chamberlain Hinonishi Sukehiro described the emperor as a stoic who "had trouble with his eyes
and teeth from some time back, but he never complained to anyone…. He avoided doctors as much
as possible." From July 20, his daughters and the crown princess took turns at his bedside; the
crown prince was quarantined with chicken pox and could not attend his dying father. The empress
sent the court ritualist to Ise Shrine to pray for recovery.

The emperor's end came at shortly after midnight on July 30, 1912, his immediate cause of death
recorded as heart failure.

### Transition to Taishō

At 1:00 a.m. on July 30, the home minister carried the sacred sword and jewel, the imperial
seal, and the seal of state to the Hall for State Ceremonies. The ceremony of transmission was
performed, and Emperor Taishō issued a rescript announcing the new reign-name. The new emperor
was invested with the uniform of supreme commander. His mother, the dowager empress Shōken,
insisted that her son take the seat of honor, saying: "You have acceded to the position of the
sovereign of the entire realm, and you must sit in the place of honor."

On August 27 the late emperor was given his posthumous name, Meiji — the first time in either
Japan or China that an emperor's posthumous name was taken from the *nengō*. As Keene notes,
"the nengō Meiji was so closely linked to the extraordinary events of his reign that no other
name seemed suitable."

The site chosen for interment — Fushimi Momoyama (Old Castle Mountain), south of Kyoto, on the
ruins of Toyotomi Hideyoshi's Fushimi Castle — reflected a wish the emperor had expressed in
1903, when he had told the empress at dinner that after he had lived out his "hundred years," his
tomb should be at Momoyama. The empress recalled this wish when his condition deteriorated.
Construction of the Meiji Shrine in Tokyo was intended to "soothe the wounded feelings" of
Tokyo's inhabitants who had petitioned for a nearby tomb.

### The Funeral

The funeral service on September 13, 1912, at the Aoyama parade grounds was deliberately Shintō
in character, "breaking the long tradition of Buddhist rites after the death of an emperor" — a
decision that required inventing suitably "ancient" rituals since there were no modern precedents.
The hearse — lacquered black, roofed in Chinese style, decorated with more than 3,000 metal
ornaments, weighing more than three tons, drawn by five oxen — departed the palace at 8:00 p.m.
The Guards Military Band played *Kanashimi no kiwami* (Extremity of Grief). A reporter described
the crowds: "The tens of thousands of people present swallowed their voices and corrected their
posture. Then they surrendered themselves completely to the waves of sound suffused with grief."

Prince Arthur of Connaught attended representing the King of England. Yi Kang, elder son of the
former emperor of Korea, marched in the procession alongside Japanese princes of the blood.
At midnight, sixty million people bowed in simultaneous silent prayer at the sound of cannon
salutes.

That same night, General Nogi Maresuke and his wife Shizuko committed *junshi* (suicide
following one's lord) at their residence — see [[nogi-maresuke]] and [[nogi-junshi-1912]].

The coffin left by special train to Kyoto at 1:40 a.m. on September 14. Interment at Fushimi
Momoyama was completed by 7:00 a.m. on September 15.

### Reactions

Reactions to the emperor's death were worldwide. The *Times* of London praised his "remarkable
faculty of judging character" and "absolute willingness that others should wear the laurels of
success." A Russian newspaper compared him to Peter the Great but found a crucial difference:
"Peter needed such talents in order to create single-handedly a new Russia, whereas the mikado
could do without them. Japan had so many able men that the mikado had only to choose the most
capable to assist him." Chinese newspapers mourned him as having "brought light to the yellow
men" and credited his defeat of Russia as having forged for the first time a sense of pan-Asian
racial solidarity.

The novelist Tokutomi Roka, born in the first year of Meiji and often critical of the government,
wrote that "the death of His Majesty closed the volume of Meiji history…. I felt as if Emperor
Meiji had gone off taking with him half my life." Natsume Sōseki, initially annoyed by the
premature suspension of public entertainments during the emperor's illness, nonetheless wrote a
panegyric once he died.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: meiji declining health 1911 1912` — Cumulative deterioration from diabetes
  (diagnosed 1904), chronic hepatitis (1906), and the physical demands he continued to impose
  on himself through maneuvers and public ceremonies.
- `contributed_to: [[high-treason-incident-1910]]` — Keene suggests that learning for the
  first time on January 18, 1911 that any of his subjects had plotted to kill him was one of the
  psychic shocks that accelerated his physical decline.
- `preceded_by: [[meiji-restoration-1868]]` — The forty-five-year reign that the death closed
  had begun with the Restoration; the death marked the formal end of that epoch.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: taisho era japan` — The Taishō emperor succeeded; the new reign-name
  opened a period of greater democratic experimentation before the militarist turn of the 1930s.
- `produced: [[nogi-junshi-1912]]` — General Nogi and his wife committed suicide that night,
  interpreting the emperor's death as their moment of final obligation.
- `enabled: meiji shrine construction` — The Tokyo shrine to the deified emperor was
  built in the absence of a nearby tomb; it remains the most-visited shrine in Japan at New Year.
- `produced: [[meiji-legacy]]` — The emperor's death crystallized the interpretive question
  of his legacy and historical significance.

## Actors

- **[[emperor-meiji]]**: Died July 30, 1912; stoic to the end, attending a university graduation
  ceremony and a Privy Council session in the weeks before his fatal collapse.
- **emperor taisho**: Succeeded immediately on the night of July 30; was suffering from
  chicken pox and unable to attend his father's deathbed.
- **[[nogi-maresuke]]**: Committed *junshi* on the night of the funeral, September 13.
- **empress shoken**: Managed the deathbed, recalled the emperor's wish for Momoyama
  interment, presided over the succession ceremony.
- **[[katsura-taro]]**: Prime minister; wrote the funeral eulogy read by Emperor Taishō.

## Historiography

Keene argues that the emperor's stoicism and self-erasure make him difficult to approach as a
biographical subject: "The emperor had almost no private side to him. He also had no preferences."
The politician Makino Nobuaki elaborated that the emperor "led almost no life apart from his work."
This quality — the near-total subordination of personality to role — is what made him effective
as a constitutional monarch but what makes him elusive as a historical figure.

Scholarly debate has centered on the extent of Meiji's personal agency: was the transformation
of Japan his achievement, or that of the genrō and officials who surrounded him? The foreign
press in 1912 offered both interpretations. The *Globe* held that "but for the personality of the
Monarch the statesmen would have been able to accomplish very much less"; the French editorialist
Le Correspondant identified his primary role as being "the living symbol of national life" rather
than a policy-maker. Both frames are still current in the scholarship.
