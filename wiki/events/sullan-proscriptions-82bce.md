# The Sullan Proscriptions (82–81 BCE)

The **published death-lists** by which Sulla, after his victory, condemned his
enemies without trial, confiscated their property, and barred their descendants
from office — "his most notorious legacy to Rome" and the template for later
political terror. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 6 (Seager).

## Narrative

After the **[[sullan-civil-war-83-82bce|Colline Gate]]**, Sulla's followers had
been killing across Italy at will. The proscriptions were instituted, paradoxically,
as a **response to protests at the arbitrariness** of these killings: pressed to say
whom he would spare, Sulla instead posted **lists of those to be punished**. The
named were **condemned to death without trial**; their property was confiscated and
auctioned (often to Sulla's favourites below value); rewards were paid to killers
and informers; and their **sons and grandsons were barred from office** while still
owing the duties of their rank.

Appian gives c. **40 senators and 1,600 equites**; Plutarch, 80 on the first list,
then two further lists of c. 220 each. Two consuls and two consulars headed the
first list (Carbo, the younger Marius, Norbanus, Scipio); the young **Julius
Caesar** was proscribed for refusing to divorce Cinna's daughter, but pardoned on
intercession. The terror was exploited across Italy by men like **Crassus** (who
proscribed a man in Bruttium simply for his estate) and **Chrysogonus**, Sulla's
freedman, whose machinations against Roscius of Ameria the young **Cicero** exposed
in 80.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: total victory in the [[sullan-civil-war-83-82bce|civil war]] and the
need to **regularize and limit** the indiscriminate slaughter already underway —
though the instrument became a vehicle for greed and private vengeance.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the physical **destruction of the Marian leadership** and a vast
**transfer of wealth and land** to the Sullani — the economic base of the new
regime and of the [[sullan-constitution|veteran colonies]]. The victims were
overwhelmingly the **rich** (senators and equites), not the rank and file.
enabled: a lasting trauma in Roman politics — the dread of "a new Sulla" that
Seager sees paralysing the post-Sullan Senate — and grievances (the disfranchised
proscribed families, the dispossessed of Etruria) that fed the risings of
**Lepidus** (78) and **Catiline** (63).

## Historiography

The hostile tradition (Sallust, Cicero, Plutarch) is partly checked against
Sulla's self-exculpation: Seager accepts Cicero's claim that Sulla was often
unaware of his agents' abuses (the Chrysogonus affair), while holding him
responsible for the institution itself. The figures are disputed.

**Beard ([[sources/beard-spqr-2015|SPQR]], 2015)** stresses that the terror was
not simply [[sulla|Sulla's]] personal cruelty but a broadly collaborative social
phenomenon: "another part of the story was the eagerness of so many to join in the
massacre, to settle old scores or simply to claim the financial rewards for the
murders" — citing Catiline's insertion of personal enemies onto the lists as an
example of the system being exploited for private profit and vendetta beyond any
single man's direction.
