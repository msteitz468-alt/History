# The Spanish Conquest of Peru (1532–1533)

The fall of the [[inca-empire|Inca empire (Tawantinsuyu)]] — the largest state of
pre-Columbian America — to **[[pizarro-francisco|Francisco Pizarro]]** and a band
of fewer than 200 Spaniards. With the [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|conquest
of Mexico]], it is one of the two foundational events of
[[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]] and, in the participants' own
estimation, the **most spectacular of all the conquests** — the ransom of
Atawallpa and the plunder of Cuzco far exceeding the treasure Cortés had won.
This page follows Burkholder and Johnson's
[[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|account]]; the indigenous-Andean
counter-voice (Guamán Poma) remains to be ingested.

## Narrative

**Early expeditions.** Pizarro, an illegitimate and unlettered Extremaduran who had
grown prosperous as an *encomendero* and co-founder of Panama, led probing
voyages south from 1524 with his partners Diego de **Almagro** and the priest
Hernando de **Luque**. Marooned with thirteen men on the Isla del Gallo, he
refused to turn back; proof of a rich civilization (a captured ocean-going raft
laden with gold, silver, and fine textiles) let him sail to **Spain** and secure a
royal *capitulación* naming him governor of Peru — and, fatefully, leaving Almagro
with a lesser title. The unequal rewards seeded the **factionalism** that would
later tear the conquerors apart.

**The empire he found was broken.** In the late 1520s (likely 1520s pandemic originating from the north) an epidemic (smallpox most probable per Crosby) swept the northern Andes *before* Pizarro, killing the Sapa Inca
**Wayna Qhapaq (Huayna Capac)** and many captains/royals with shocking rapidity ("their faces being covered with scabs"). The succession war that followed
pitted **Waskar (Huáscar)** in Cuzco against **Atawallpa (Atahuallpa)**, who
commanded the professional army on the northern frontier. Atawallpa had just won —
his forces had taken Cuzco and were savaging Waskar's supporters (especially the
**Cañari**) — when Pizarro landed in 1532. Disease and civil war had done the
invaders' work in advance. Pedro Pizarro: "Had this Huayna Capac been alive when we Spaniards entered this land, it would have been impossible for us to win it, for he was much beloved by all his vassals." "Had the land not been divided between Huascar and Atahualpa, we would not have been able to enter or win the land unless we could gather a thousand Spaniards for the task..." (Crosby Ch. 2).

**Cajamarca (16 November 1532).** Pizarro lured Atawallpa, fresh from victory and
escorted by an army of perhaps 40,000, into the central plaza of Cajamarca, where
the Sapa Inca entered with several thousand lightly armed retainers. At a signal,
hidden Spaniards fired harquebuses and two cannon into the packed square and the
cavalry charged. Not a single Spaniard was killed; at least 1,500 Andeans died,
and **Atawallpa was seized**. The capture paralyzed a state that ran entirely
through its god-king.

**The ransom and the regicide.** Believing the strangers would leave once sated,
Atawallpa offered to fill a room with gold and twice over with silver. The
Spaniards melted down ~13,420 pounds of gold and ~26,000 pounds of silver — each
foot soldier's share a fortune, Pizarro's seven times a cavalryman's, making him
one of the richest men in the Iberian world. Atawallpa, still treating Waskar as
his real rival, used his captivity to order his **brother's execution**. With the
ransom collected, Almagro's late-arriving men demanded Atawallpa's death so no
further loot could be claimed as "ransom"; Pizarro yielded, and in **July 1533**
the last reigning Sapa Inca was condemned without trial and **garroted** (commuted
from burning after a deathbed baptism).

**Consolidation.** Pizarro installed puppet rulers (Tupac Huallpa, then **Manco
Inca**), marched on Cuzco — winning the conquest's first true pitched battles with
the aid of anti-Atawallpa Andean allies — and founded a new coastal capital,
**Lima**, on 5 January 1535.

**The Inca rebellion (1536).** Driven past endurance by Spanish abuse, Manco Inca
escaped and besieged Cuzco with an army of up to 50,000, having learned to fight
the Spanish in broken terrain where cavalry was useless; relief columns from Lima
were annihilated. But he could not dislodge the few hundred Spaniards in Cuzco,
and a direct assault on Lima failed. Manco withdrew to **Vilcabamba**, where a
**neo-Inca state survived until 1572**.

**The conquerors' civil wars (1537–1548).** The conquest nearly destroyed itself.
Almagro seized Cuzco and was defeated and executed by the Pizarros (1538); his son
assassinated Francisco Pizarro in Lima (1541); the Crown's **New Laws (1542)**,
threatening the *encomiendas*, provoked **Gonzalo Pizarro's** revolt, which made
him master of Peru and killed the first viceroy before the priest **Pedro de la
Gasca** ended the rebellion and beheaded Gonzalo (1548). Royal authority — not the
violent men of the conquest — finally secured Peru.

**The end of Tawantinsuyu.** Viceroy **Francisco de Toledo** overwhelmed the
Vilcabamba kingdom and beheaded the last Sapa Inca, **Tupac Amaru**, in Cuzco's
central square in **1572**, before thousands of Spaniards and Andeans — closing the
forty-year conquest saga begun at Cajamarca.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[virgin-soil-epidemics]] — the epidemic death of Wayna Qhapaq, which
  set the dynastic crisis in motion.
- `caused_by:` the [[inca-empire|Waskar–Atawallpa civil war]] — which split the empire
  and gave Pizarro local allies and a paralyzed enemy.
- `contributed_to:` Spanish **steel, horses, and cannon** and the deliberate
  **Cajamarca ambush** copying Cortés's seizure of Motecuhzoma. As in Mexico, the
  decisive factor was less technology alone than a society already shattered by
  disease and internecine war, exploited by audacious commanders.
- `enabled:` the centralized, god-king structure of the Inca state — seizing one man
  decapitated the whole.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Viceroyalty of Peru** and the Andean core of
  [[colonial-latin-america|colonial Latin America]].
- `produced:` the [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí]] silver economy (deposits found
  1545) and the brutal colonial [[mita-and-andean-reciprocity|mita]] that fed it.
- `produced:` the **Andean demographic collapse** (see [[pre-columbian-american-population]]).
- `enabled:` the long survival of Andean identity through the
  [[inca-empire|neo-Inca Vilcabamba state]] and later messianic memory of the Inca.

## Actors

- [[pizarro-francisco|Francisco Pizarro]] — the commander.
- [[inca-empire|Tawantinsuyu]] — the empire that fell; Atawallpa, Waskar, Manco Inca,
  Tupac Amaru.
- **Black conquistadors** — slave and free Africans accompanied Pizarro (a free black
  served as second-in-command of his artillery at Cajamarca); battlefield service was
  a route to freedom and even, occasionally, to *encomiendas* (see Juan Valiente in Chile).

## Historiography

The conquest is documented overwhelmingly from the **Spanish side** (Pizarro's
secretaries, chroniclers like Agustín de Zárate, later the *mestizo* Garcilaso de
la Vega). The first full narrative history in English, **Prescott's** *History of
the Conquest of Peru*, appeared only in **1847** — a measure of how thoroughly the
Andean past had been erased. As with Mexico, modern scholarship stresses
**indigenous agency and division** (the Cañari and others who allied with the
Spanish against Atawallpa) and the **epidemic-and-civil-war** preconditions over
the older tale of a handful of supermen toppling an empire. The moral framing
belongs to the [[black-legend|Black Legend]] debate.
