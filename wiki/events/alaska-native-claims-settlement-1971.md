# Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA, 1971)

Federal settlement of Alaska Native aboriginal land claims that established **economic corporations** rather than lower-forty-eight-style reservations or IRA tribal governments. Central modern political event for Alaskan [[inuit]], [[yupik|Yuit/Yupik]], Athapaskan, and other Native groups within [[arctic-native-history-norse-to-modern]] and [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]].

## Narrative

By the 1960s U.S. termination was largely abandoned, but “equal opportunity” proved hard to implement; many Alaskan Natives still saw subtle White paternalism. The **Alaska Statehood Act 1958** said Natives would not be dispossessed of lands used or claimed, yet allowed the new state to claim more than 104 million acres (42M ha). In the 1960s mineral and oil companies claimed large tracts Natives considered theirs. Additional threats without Native consultation included Project Chariot (AEC nuclear harbor blast in northwest Alaska), Rampart Dam (interior flooding), and enforcement of the long-neglected Migratory Wildfowl Treaty.

Chief organizational response: the **Alaska Federation of Natives (AFN)**, 1966—an alliance of eight regional organizations—negotiated toward settlement. Settlement fell short of all sought goals because of Congressional opposition; White House staff were key; Native leaders praised Nixon. The **Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971** (effective settlement date conventionally tied to **18 December 1971** for enrollment) created regional corporations plus roughly 200 village corporations to receive monies for business ventures.

Washburn’s comparative frame: ANCSA’s unique feature was establishment of Native economic corporations **expected to supplant and replace tribal or village political organizations**; non-Natives could not “buy in” for twenty years; the act sought to insert Native Alaskans into the modern economy “without establishing any permanently racially defined institutions, rights, privileges, or obligations,” unlike IRA tribes. Form derived from small isolated clans/villages and a shared desire of Euro-Americans and many Natives to avoid the complex reservation/trusteeship system of the lower forty-eight; AFN had supported the economic approach.

Damas on Arctic Inuit-speaking corporations: **Arctic Slope Regional Corporation**, **NANA Regional Corporation**, and about half of **Bering Straits Native Corporation** (latter shared with [[yupik|Yuit]] speakers) were Inuit-speaking—these three received $185.9 million cash plus large lands. Land selection and cash proved difficult; many claims remained “interim conveyance” into the 1980s; most money went to corporations, 10% to individuals. Athapaskan-speaking interior groups (e.g. Kolchans via AFN) also took shares of cash and of ~15.6 million acres under ANCSA (Ray ch. 13).

Hardest provision (Damas): only a **twenty-year period** applied—an extension of lower-forty-eight **termination** policies—only those living 18 December 1971 benefited directly; after 18 December 1991 shares would become transferable to non-Natives and Native land taxable. 1980s anxiety over corporation financial failures drove AFN lobbying; **February 1988** Reagan signed amendments lifting post-1971 birth restrictions and altering stock-alienation and land-taxation restrictions (P.L. 100-241). By the 1990s “chronic obstacles” remained (land conveyance, corporation instability, IRS escrow)—final resolution years or decades out (McNabb 1992).

Washburn on post-1991 politics: approaching the end of exclusive Native ownership sparked 1980s movements to continue special Alaskan Native status, protect corporate land from taxation/loss/bankruptcy, and cover Natives left out of the original settlement; efforts to transfer corporate lands to tribal authority were rebuffed; Congress allowed state-regulated trusts and added a disclaimer that the 1991 amendments neither helped nor hindered tribal claims. On **15 October 1993** Interior Assistant Secretary Ada Deer announced federally recognized Alaska tribes with status equal to the lower forty-eight—hoping courts would accept; Washburn: **“The ultimate political status of Alaska Natives remains to be determined.”**

Outward village transformation 1960–80 (housing, schools, supermarkets, TV, vehicles); Burch: one really significant change was Natives taking control of their own affairs at regional and village levels.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` conflict between state land selection under the Alaska Statehood Act 1958 and unextinguished Native claims amid oil/mineral development pressure.
- `contributed_to:` AFN coalition politics (1966); Nixon-era federal willingness to settle; desire on multiple sides to avoid IRA reservation complexity (`contrasts_with: [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]]`).
- `contributed_to:` specific project threats (Chariot, Rampart, wildfowl treaty enforcement) that mobilized Native opposition without consultation.
- `preceded_by: [[termination-policy]]` / termination-era logic — the 20-year stock and tax cliff recycled termination assumptions even as hard termination was abandoned.
- `part_of: [[arctic-native-history-norse-to-modern]]` · `part_of: [[native-american-renaissance-1960-1995]]`

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` corporate property and cash regime for Alaska Natives; regional/village investment institutions.
- `enabled:` Inuit- and Yuit-speaking corporations (Arctic Slope, NANA, Bering Straits share) and Athapaskan regional shares to manage land and capital.
- `produced:` 1980s reform politics and 1988 amendments; ongoing dualism between corporate and tribal recognition claims (Deer 1993).
- `contributed_to: [[tribal-self-determination]]` debates without resolving Alaska’s place relative to lower-forty-eight tribal sovereignty.
- `analogous_to:` James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement 1975 and later Canadian comprehensive claims (Washburn treats James Bay as first modern Canadian settlement; different legal form).
- Social-economic: accelerated incorporation into wage/consumer economy; did not end dependency or health/education disparities tracked elsewhere in Damas/Washburn.

## Actors

- **Alaska Federation of Natives** — principal negotiating body.
- **[[inuit]]** speakers — Arctic Slope, NANA, part of Bering Straits.
- **[[yupik]]** (Yuit) speakers — share of Bering Straits corporation.
- Interior Athapaskan groups (see [[dene]] / northern interior process) — ANCSA acreage and cash shares.
- Federal executive and Congress; later Ada Deer (Interior).

## Historiography

Damas (ch. 14) embeds ANCSA in the Arctic contact-to-self-determination arc, stressing the termination-like 20-year clause, Inuit/Yuit corporate map, and Burch’s “control of own affairs” judgment. Washburn (ch. 15) frames ANCSA within the continental “Native Renaissance,” contrasts corporate vs IRA models, and leaves ultimate political status open after 1988/1993. Ray (ch. 13) notes Kolchan/AFN participation and 15.6 million acres for Athapaskan-speaking groups. Interpretive tension: success as self-determination vehicle vs incomplete extinguishment of tribal sovereignty claims and corporate instability. Reliability: statutory and political narrative strong; corporation-level economic outcomes uneven and still contested at volume close (early 1990s).
