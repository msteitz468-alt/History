# The Capture of Valerian (260)

The **unexampled capture of a reigning Roman emperor** — **Valerian**, taken prisoner by the Sassanian
king **[[shapur-i|Shapur I]]** near **Edessa** in 260, the single most shocking event of the
[[third-century-crisis|third-century crisis]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 14
(**Richard N. Frye**).

## Narrative

In Shapur's **third western campaign**, the Persians were besieging Edessa and Carrhae when Valerian
advanced to relieve them with a reported **70,000 men**. The emperor and his officers, probably seeking
to negotiate peace, were instead **seized**; Valerian and a large part of his army were carried off as
prisoners into Sassanian territory and resettled in **Khuzistan** (around the new town of Gundeshapur).
Shapur's trilingual **Res Gestae Divi Saporis** (the *ŠKZ*) records the triumph, and **five rock reliefs**
in Fars depict the captured emperor — kneeling before, or led by, the mounted Shapur — the most famous
images of Sassanian victory over Rome.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **expansionist energy of [[shapur-i|Shapur I]]** and the centralized
  [[sassanid-empire|Sassanid state]], a far more dangerous adversary than Parthia had been.
- `contributed_to:` the **structural over-extension** of Roman defences during the crisis — Valerian,
  having divided imperial responsibility with his son [[gallienus|Gallienus]] (West), faced the eastern
  war amid plague and a collapsing frontier.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **high-water mark of Sassanian prestige** — but *not* permanent conquest, since Frye
  stresses Shapur sought booty and prisoners rather than annexation.
- `enabled:` the **rise of [[palmyra|Palmyra]]** — with Roman power in the East shattered, it was
  **[[zenobia|Odenathus]]** of Palmyra, not Rome, who counter-attacked, recovered **Carrhae and Nisibis**,
  and became the de facto defender of the Roman East (the seed of Zenobia's later secession).
- `contributed_to:` the deepening crisis of **[[gallienus|Gallienus]]'** sole reign, the proliferation of
  usurpers, and the secession of the [[gallic-empire|Gallic Empire]].

## Historiography

The episode is reported from radically different angles — the boastful Sassanian *ŠKZ* and rock reliefs
versus hostile or moralizing Roman/Christian sources (Lactantius' lurid tale of Valerian flayed and
stuffed). Frye treats the bare fact of the capture as certain and emblematic, while cautioning that
Shapur's aims were limited; the **dates of the associated sacks of Antioch** (253 vs 256) remain disputed,
and the precise circumstances of the capture (battle or treacherous parley) are unrecoverable.
