## Narrative

On 12 October — in 633 or 634, with Kirby preferring 634 (see below) — [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]], king of the northern Angles and the fifth overlord south of the Humber, was "defeated and slain in battle in Hatfield Chase by Cadwallon ap Cadfan, king of the Britons of Gwynedd, acting in concert with a Mercian force under [[penda-of-mercia|Penda]] (HE II, 20)." The battlefield lay in a strategically sensitive zone south of the Rivers Idle, Don, and Trent — Hatfield Chase (Heathfeld land), through which ran Ryknield Street linking York and Doncaster to Lincoln. Edwinstowe has been suggested as the specific site.

Eadwine's son Osfrith "perished with his father"; another son, Eadfrith, surrendered to Penda; the queen Aethelburh, accompanied by Paulinus, fled to Kent with her daughter Eanflaed, her young son Uscfrea, and Osfrith's son Yffi. The young princes were later sent for safety to the Frankish king Dagobert I, where they died in infancy.

After the battle, Osric son of Eadwine's uncle Aelfric took the kingship of the Deirans and Eanfrith son of [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]] that of the Bernicians, fracturing Eadwine's overkingship. Cadwallon slew Osric in the following summer and Eanfrith when he came to sue for peace. The destruction of the Deiran royal court was near-complete. Into the power-vacuum stepped [[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]], who slew Cadwallon at [[battle-of-heavenfield-634|Heavenfield]].

## Causal Analysis

The proximate cause was Eadwine's sustained military offensive against Gwynedd. Welsh tradition recalled his "siege of Cadwallon on the island of Ynys Lannog (Priestholm)" (Welsh annals s.a. 629), his capture of the Mevanian Islands (Anglesey, Man), and his push into the heartland of Gwynedd. The poem Moliant Cadwallon ("In Praise of Cadwallon"), likely composed at Cadwallon's own court, portrays Eadwine as "the deceitful" and celebrates Cadwallon's return from Ireland (or exile) with a victorious host.

Structural causes included: the coalescence of [[mercia|Mercia]] under Penda as a formidable midland power; the weakening of Eadwine's east Anglian alliance following the assassination of Eorpwald (c. 628–631); and the opportunity for Æthelfrith's exiled sons to return from among the Picts and Scots — their proximity at the moment of Eadwine's fall was probably not coincidental: "the reappearance…of Eanfrith and subsequently of Oswald in Bernician territory at so crucial a moment…could imply military assistance for them from the Picts or the Scots or both and even perhaps a certain ability to act in concert with the British king, Cadwallon."

## Consequence Analysis

The battle was, as Bede said, an event which "was to have profound repercussions." It shattered the first great Deiran hegemony and opened a generation of Bernician domination. The flight of Paulinus to Kent terminated the first York episcopal mission and delayed the establishment of a northern archbishopric by a hundred years. Penda's retention of Eadfrith as a hostage — and his eventual killing of him — extinguished the direct male Deiran line descended from Eadwine.

The battle also destroyed the alliance that had won it: "The alliances did not survive the overthrow of Eadwine for any length of time." Penda returned to Mercia; Cadwallon overreached by continuing to ravage and was destroyed at Heavenfield by Oswald.

## Actors

- **Cadwallon ap Cadfan** — king of Gwynedd; the driving force of the campaign. May have believed himself descended from the Votadini and motivated partly by solidarity with the northern Britons under Anglian pressure.
- **[[penda-of-mercia|Penda]]** — Mercian aetheling, "a most vigorous young man of the royal race of the Mercians" (HE II, 20); used the victory to establish his Mercian kingship.
- **[[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]]** — killed in battle, aged 47.

## Historiography

The principal debate is chronological: Bede dates the battle to October 633 (from a 616 accession for Eadwine); Kirby argues for October 634 (from a 617 accession), based on the June 634 papal letter to Honorius of Canterbury that was co-requested by Eadwine and implies he was alive at that date. The precise location of Hatfield Chase is not resolved. Welsh bardic sources (Moliant Cadwallon, a fragment about Eadwine's severed head at Aberffraw) add texture but are difficult to use as history. Kirby does not adjudicate whether Cadwallon and the Bernician aethelings were acting in concert; he regards it as probable but unprovable.
