# 4.2-Kiloyear Event

A severe, multi-decade aridification and cooling episode centered around
2200 BCE, identified from speleothem, ice-core, and lake-sediment proxy
records across the Northern Hemisphere. It is formally adopted by the
International Commission on Stratigraphy as the boundary marking the start
of the Meghalayan Age (2016).

## Narrative

Climate proxies from Mesopotamia, the Eastern Mediterranean, and South Asia
converge on evidence of a sharp, sustained drop in rainfall beginning around
2200 BCE and lasting roughly a century. The event is chronologically
associated with — though not uncontroversially proven to have directly
caused — the collapse of the Akkadian Empire in Mesopotamia, the end of
Egypt's Old Kingdom and onset of the First Intermediate Period, and the
decline of Harappan urbanism in the Indus Valley.

## Causal Analysis

The proximate physical mechanism (shifts in North Atlantic and monsoon
circulation, possibly compounded by volcanic aerosol forcing) remains
debated among paleoclimatologists. Its role in specific civilizational
declines is likewise contested: `contributed_to` rather than `caused_by` is
used throughout, since each affected society also faced independent
political, administrative, or military stresses that interacted with the
climatic shock.

## Consequence Analysis

Where a causal role is accepted, the event is credited with accelerating
agricultural failure, urban abandonment, and political fragmentation across
several Bronze Age civilizations simultaneously — an early example of a
transregional climate shock with hemispheric-scale historical consequences.

## Historiography

The 4.2-kiloyear event is a live case in the broader climate-and-collapse
debate that also surrounds the Late Bronze Age Collapse
([[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce]]): proponents (drawing on high-resolution
paleoclimate proxies) argue for a strong causal role in Old World collapses
around 2200 BCE, while critics caution against climate determinism and stress
that Akkadian, Egyptian, and Harappan declines each had substantial internal
political and economic dynamics that would likely have produced instability
regardless of climate. The 2018 ICS ratification of the "Meghalayan" age
boundary has intensified scholarly and popular attention to the event.
