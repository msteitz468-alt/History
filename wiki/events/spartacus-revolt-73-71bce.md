# The Revolt of Spartacus (73–71 BCE)

The greatest **slave insurrection** in Roman history — the Third Servile War — in
which a band of escaped gladiators grew into an army of tens of thousands and
defeated consular Roman forces before being crushed by **Crassus**. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 7 (Seager).

## Narrative

In **73** seventy-four gladiators broke out of a school at **Capua** under the
Thracian **Spartacus** (a former Roman auxiliary) and the Gauls **Crixus** and
**Oenomaus**, and seized a position on **Vesuvius**. They swelled rapidly with
slaves and free herdsmen — Spartacus is reported to have commanded **70,000** — and
routed a succession of Roman commanders: the praetor **Glaber** (outflanked on
Vesuvius), then Varinius and others. A strategic split divided the rebels:
**Spartacus wanted to march north out of Italy** so the men could disperse to their
homelands; **Crixus' Gauls and Germans preferred plunder**, devastating the south.

In **72** both consuls took the field; Crixus was destroyed at Mount Garganus, but
Spartacus beat the consuls in Picenum, reached Cisalpine Gaul — then unaccountably
**turned back south**. The command passed to **M. Licinius Crassus**, who restored
discipline by reviving **decimation**, drove Spartacus into Bruttium, and penned him
behind a wall across the toe of Italy. The pirates who had promised to ferry the
slaves to Sicily betrayed them. Breaking out, Spartacus was finally brought to
battle and **killed (71)** — his body never found. Crassus **crucified 6,000
prisoners along the Via Appia** from Capua to Rome; **[[pompey]]**, returning from
Spain, annihilated 5,000 fugitives and claimed in a dispatch to the Senate that
**he** had ended the war — the root of his lasting rivalry with Crassus.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **mass chattel slavery** of late-Republican Italy and the
particular brutality of the gladiatorial schools.
contributed_to: by the **absence of Rome's main armies** — Pompey and Metellus in
the [[sertorian-war-80-72bce|Sertorian War]], M. Lucullus in Thrace — which left
Italy defended only by improvised forces.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: a brutal restoration of order and the spectacle of the crucified Via
Appia; politically, the **elevation of Crassus** (rewarded an ovation with a laurel
wreath) and a propaganda windfall for **Pompey**.
enabled: the alliance-of-convenience that brought **Crassus and Pompey jointly to
the consulship of 70** — and with it the restoration of the tribunate and the
breaching of the [[sullan-constitution|Sullan settlement]].

## Historiography

Plutarch's *Crassus* and Appian's *Civil Wars* I; Sallust's *Histories* (fragments).
The sources give Spartacus a sympathy unusual for a slave leader but no coherent
programme — Seager treats the northward strategy, not any project of social
revolution, as his real aim. Modern reception (from the abolitionists to Marx) has
made Spartacus an icon the ancient evidence barely supports.
