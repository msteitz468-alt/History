# Battle of Dupplin Moor (1332)

A decisive victory for the small "Disinherited" force under Edward Balliol over a much larger Scottish army led by the Earl of Mar. It provided a key positive example of post-Bannockburn English tactical evolution (dismounted infantry in defensive formation supported by flanking archers) that would be replicated at Halidon Hill and influence the Hundred Years' War system.

## Narrative

After landing and minor actions, the Disinherited (c. 1,500 men, mostly fighting on foot except a small contingent of German mercenary cavalry, under Edward Balliol, Henry Beaumont, and others) crossed the River Earn (possibly by night ford) and encountered a much larger Scottish host (chroniclers claim 20,000–40,000 under Donald, Earl of Mar) on Dupplin Moor.

The Scots deployed in two or three divisions/lines. The Disinherited formed three infantry divisions in a defensive posture. Archers were positioned on the flanks.

The Scots charged first, confident in numbers, initially pushing the English line back 20–30 feet. English archers then "blinded and wounded the faces of the first division of the Scots by an incessant discharge of arrows," causing disarray. Smaller Scottish squadrons were forced to mass into the larger body, leading to crowding and loss of cohesion.

The battle became a prolonged push of infantry masses from sunrise into the afternoon. The Disinherited maintained formation unity and used archery harassment on flanks and rear. Eventually the English/disinherited began a steady advance; Scottish leaders fled on horseback, leaving their troops leaderless and unable to disengage. The Disinherited advanced uniformly, killing or routing the rest.

Balliol occupied Perth; he was crowned at Scone on 24 September.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the Disinherited's bid to restore Balliol claims against the Bruce regency/minority after the Treaty of Northampton's collapse.
- `contributed_to:` post-Bannockburn learning by the Disinherited/English exiles of dismounted infantry tactics and use of archers to disrupt charges (explicit contrast to mounted failures).
- Scots' overconfidence and massed charge into a prepared defensive line + flanking fire led to self-disorder.

## Consequence Analysis

- Balliol's short-lived triumph; main Bruce forces remained, leading to his expulsion the following year.
- Provided the tactical template Edward III would use at Halidon Hill (1333): defensive formation of dismounted men-at-arms with archer wings.
- Explicitly cited by Edward III as proof that a smaller force could defeat a larger Scottish army.
- Early demonstration of the combined-arms defensive system (infantry line + archery harassment) that would mature in the HYW (Crécy, etc.).

## Actors

- Edward Balliol: claimant leader; crowned after victory.
- Henry Beaumont and other Disinherited lords: key commanders in the small professional force.
- Donald, Earl of Mar: Scottish commander; his larger host defeated by tactical discipline rather than numbers.

## Historiography

Contemporary English and Scottish chronicles (Anonimalle, Brut, Thomas of Burton, Lanercost, Pluscardensis, Wyntoun, Baker) differ on exact numbers, the river crossing details, and the precise sequence of the charge. DeVries emphasizes the unity of the defensive formation, the disruptive effect of archery on faces and cohesion (causing massing), and the eventual uniform English advance once Scottish leadership fled. Modern views see it as a pivotal tactical experiment for English armies. Cross-referenced in DeVries with Bannockburn (negative lesson) and later HYW battles (positive continuity).
