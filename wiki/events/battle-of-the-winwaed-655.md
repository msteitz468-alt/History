## Narrative

On 15 November in 655 or 656 (Bede gives 655 at HE III, 24, but Kirby notes this may need emending if Eadwine died in 634 rather than 633), [[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]] of Northumbria defeated and killed [[penda-of-mercia|Penda]] of Mercia on the banks of the River Winwæd, probably the River Went, a tributary of the Don, "swollen by autumnal rains." The battle was the culmination of a crisis that had begun with Penda's coalition-led siege of Oswiu at Iudeu (probably Stirling).

Penda had assembled an impressive army of thirty duces, including Cadafael ap Cynfedw, king of Gwynedd; Aethelhere, king of the East Angles; and Oethelwald, king of Deira (Oswald's son). Having besieged Oswiu at Iudeu, Penda obliged him to surrender the ten-year-old Ecgfrith as a hostage and to pay great treasure. Penda and his allies then withdrew, apparently accepting the tribute as the price of peace.

Oswiu pursued them with a smaller force and caught the coalition "homeward bound and perhaps disinclined for a renewed engagement." Before battle was joined, "Cadafael (HB ch. 65) and Oethelwald (HE III, 24) withdrew." Aethelhere of East Anglia and "the majority of Penda's duces perished in the ensuing battle in which, according to Bede, more died in the flood-waters of the river than were slain by the sword (HE III, 24)." Penda was killed — "there must at least be a possibility that Penda's decapitation by Oswiu was an avenging of Penda's earlier dismemberment of Oswald's body."

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause was Penda's decision to press north and besiege Oswiu. Kirby identifies a mix of Mercian and British motivations: Penda "regarded Oswiu's activities in general as contrary to Mercian interests — in particular his involvement, for example, with the eastern Saxons, and possibly his likely reception of Cenwealh from the upper Thames valley whom Penda had expelled." For Cadafael of Gwynedd, "Oswiu's domination of the north Britons, perhaps particularly the Votadini, may have been of principal concern." For Oethelwald of Deira, grievances against his uncle Oswiu — who had murdered Oethelwald's kinsman Oswine — probably played a role.

The crisis was enabled by Penda's steady consolidation since Maserfelth (642/643): he had "spent the intervening thirteen years consolidating his position," establishing the Middle Angles under Peada, attacking and eventually killing Anna of East Anglia, and expelling Cenwealh of the Thames valley Saxons.

## Consequence Analysis

The victory at the Winwæd was the hinge of the seventh century. Oswiu re-established the northern Anglian overkingship: his son Alhfrith became co-ruler over the Deirans; he entrusted the southern Mercians to Peada (his son-in-law); he retained the north Mercian territory. He "established his rule over the southern Anglo-Saxon kingdoms for three years after Penda's death (HE III, 24)." Simultaneously, "the Welsh annals record that 'Oswiu came and took booty' (AC s.a. 658), presumably in the Welsh marches."

The southern hegemony collapsed c. 658–659 when Wulfhere, Penda's young son, led a coup expelling Oswiu's Mercian governors — but the battle had permanently ended Mercian pagan domination of English politics and opened the path for Northumbrian cultural and ecclesiastical florescence.

Oswiu's increased power and prestige after the Winwæd also shifted the dynamic in northern ecclesiastical politics: "it cannot be entirely coincidental that the following years witnessed the heightening of tensions which threatened to divide Oswiu's kingdom from within," culminating in the [[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]] in 664.

## Actors

- **[[oswiu-of-northumbria|Oswiu]]** — victor; king of Bernicia and Deira.
- **[[penda-of-mercia|Penda]]** — killed; the dominant figure of the previous two decades.
- **Aethelhere of East Anglia** — killed in the battle.
- **Cadafael ap Cynfedw of Gwynedd** — withdrew before battle ("Cadafael 'Battle-decliner'," as later remembered).
- **Oethelwald of Deira** — withdrew before battle; not heard of again.

## Historiography

Kirby's principal chronological argument: if Eadwine died in 634 (not 633), then Penda's twenty-two-year reign yields 634–656 rather than Bede's 633–655, and the Winwæd falls in November 656. The two accounts attributing responsibility for the war — Bede's passage pointing to Aethelhere and the Historia Brittonum's pointing to Penda — are difficult to reconcile; Kirby notes Bede's passage "is now generally regarded as corrupt." The identification of the Winwæd with the River Went is accepted by most scholars but not archaeologically confirmed.
