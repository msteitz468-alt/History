# French Invasion of Russia (Patriotic War of 1812)

The **French Invasion of Russia**, known in the Russian tradition as the **Patriotic War of 1812**, was a pivotal campaign of the Napoleonic Wars. Launched by Napoleon Bonaparte in June 1812 to force Russia back into the Continental Blockade, the invasion resulted in the near-total destruction of the French Grande Armée and initiated the military collapse of the First French Empire.

## Narrative

On June 24, 1812, Napoleon's Grande Armée—numbering approximately 420,000 troops (later rising to over 600,000, including troops from twelve allied and satellite nations)—crossed the Niemen River. Russia opposed them with only 120,000 troops divided into two main armies commanded by Prince Michael Barclay de Tolly and Prince Pyotr Bagration.

Unable to face the invaders in a major battle, Barclay de Tolly initiated a policy of strategic retreat, drawing the French deep into the Russian interior and stretching their supply lines. The two Russian armies successfully united near Smolensk, but after the city was captured and burned by the French in August, public outrage forced Alexander I to put the veteran **Mikhail Kutuzov** in supreme command.

Kutuzov agreed with the retreat policy but felt it necessary to fight before surrendering Moscow. On September 7, 1812, he gave Napoleon battle near the village of **Borodino**, seventy-five miles west of Moscow. The Battle of Borodino was exceptionally bloody: in a single day of fighting, the French suffered 58,000 casualties and the Russians 42,000, including General Bagration. Kutuzov disengaged and withdrew, and on September 14, Napoleon entered Moscow.

Napoleon's expectations of victory were disappointed. Alexander I refused to negotiate while a single French soldier remained in Russia. During the first days of occupation, Moscow (built largely of wood) was burned down, possibly started by the Russian governor Count Theodore Rostopchin. Short of supplies and isolated, Napoleon began his retreat on October 19, 1912.

The retreat quickly became a rout. Kutuzov's victory at Maloiaroslavets blocked the southern route, forcing the French to return via the devastated path they had entered. As the winter set in, the Grande Armée was harassed by pursuing Russian troops, Cossack cavalry, and peasant guerrilla bands. In late November, the remnants of the French army narrowly escaped capture while crossing the Berezina River. By December, only 30,000 to 50,000 of the initial invading force struggled out of Russia.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Violation of the Continental Blockade:** Alexander I had allowed trade with neutral ships in Russian ports from 1810, effectively exiting the Continental Blockade, which Napoleon saw as a direct threat to his economic war against Great Britain.
- `caused_by:` **Geopolitical Antagonisms:** Tensions over Napoleon's annexation of the Duchy of Oldenburg (whose duke was Alexander I's relative) and the expansion of the French-controlled Duchy of Warsaw, which threatened Russia's hold on its Polish territories.
- `contributed_to:` **Logistical Failure:** The vast distances of Russia and the lack of roads made it impossible for the French supply services to feed the army. More French soldiers died of hunger and epidemics than of cold or combat.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **Destruction of the Grande Armée:** Napoleon lost his best cavalry, his veteran core, and his reputation for invincibility.
- `produced:` **Collapse of the French Empire:** The defeat encouraged Prussia, Austria, and Sweden to switch sides, forming the coalition that defeated Napoleon at Leipzig (1813) and captured Paris (1814).
- `produced:` **Russia as European Hegemon:** Alexander I emerged as the liberator of Europe, allowing him to establish Congress Poland and play a leading role at the Congress of Vienna.
- `produced:` **Rise of Patriotic Sentiment:** The war was a popular, patriotic conflict. Russian peasants fought both in the regular army and in guerrilla detachments, fostering a sense of national unity that influenced later cultural and political movements.

## Actors

- **Alexander I:** The Russian emperor whose determination to continue the war even after the fall of Moscow prevented Napoleon from securing a peace treaty.
- **Napoleon Bonaparte:** The French emperor whose strategic miscalculations in Russia led to the ruin of his empire.
- **Mikhail Kutuzov:** The veteran commander whose policy of disengagement and harassment successfully destroyed the invading army.
- **Prince Barclay de Tolly & Prince Bagration:** Coordinated the early strategic retreat and the unification of the Russian forces.

## Historiography

The war of 1812 has a rich historiographical tradition:
- Russian imperial historians glorified the role of the tsar and the military command.
- Leo Tolstoy's *War and Peace* offered a fatalistic view, celebrating the collective spirit of the Russian people while downplaying the role of individual generals.
- Soviet historians initially neglected the war but later, during the Stalinist era (especially during World War II, the "Great Patriotic War"), heavily emphasized the popular, patriotic nature of the conflict and the guerrilla role of the peasantry, sometimes revisionistically overstating it.
