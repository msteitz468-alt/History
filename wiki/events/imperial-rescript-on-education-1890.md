# Imperial Rescript on Education (1890)

Issued by [[emperor-meiji]] on 30 October 1890, the Imperial Rescript on Education
(*Kyōiku Chokugo*) established the moral framework of Meiji public education: loyalty to the
emperor, filial piety, and self-sacrifice in state service. Complementing the institutional
machinery of the [[meiji-constitution-1889]], it supplied a Confucian-nationalist ideology to
bind the constitutional order. Linked to [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]].

## Narrative

The promulgation of the [[meiji-constitution-1889]] in February 1889 created a constitutional
state with a bicameral Diet, but the oligarchs around [[emperor-meiji]] worried that rapid
Westernization had eroded the moral foundations of the polity. The emperor himself had long
displayed unusual interest in education and had encouraged his Confucian adviser Motoda Nagazane
to write texts inculcating loyalty and filial piety. At a conference of provincial leaders in
February 1890, governors urged the minister of education to end the disproportionate emphasis on
the West and to establish a new, specifically Japanese educational policy.

The emperor commanded Minister of Education Enomoto Takeaki to compile a set of educational
precepts, but Enomoto failed to produce a suitable text. His successor, Yoshikawa Akimasa,
produced a first draft — Confucian in tone but criticized for lacking anything distinctively
Japanese. Inoue Kowashi (1844–1895), regarded as the intellectual brain of the Itō faction,
was then asked his opinion. He objected to Yoshikawa's draft on several grounds: a rescript on
education should not read like a political command or a military order; it should not risk
arousing religious disputes by mentioning worship of the gods; and it should be clear, simple,
and not partisan. He was tasked with writing a better draft.

Inoue composed a draft, showed it to Motoda, revised it, and submitted a temporary draft to the
emperor, who read it with great care. The emperor found the treatment of the four Confucian
virtues inadequate and returned it for further revision. After many conferences between Inoue and
Motoda, adding and deleting individual characters, the final text was submitted on October 21. The
emperor read and pondered every word, giving his approval only on October 24. The Rescript was
promulgated on October 30, 1890 — just a month before the first Diet opened.

## Content and Analysis

The official English translation of the Rescript opens:

> "Know ye, Our subjects: Our Imperial Ancestors have founded Our Empire on a basis broad and
> everlasting, and have deeply and firmly implanted virtue; Our subjects ever united in loyalty
> and filial piety have from generation to generation illustrated the beauty thereof. This is the
> glory of the fundamental character [*kokutai*] of Our Empire, and herein also lies the source
> of Our education."

The text enjoins subjects to be filial to parents, harmonious in marriage, true in friendship,
modest and moderate in bearing, benevolent to all, and to "pursue learning and cultivate arts."
Most significantly, it commands subjects to "respect the Constitution and observe the laws" and,
if emergency arises, to "offer yourselves courageously to the State."

The Rescript is notable for what it does and does not say. It grounds Japanese moral life in
"Imperial Ancestors" rather than in any universal principle. Its virtues — loyalty and filial
piety — are presented as uniquely Japanese, though they echo Neo-Confucian ethics. Keene
observes: "The importance that Chu Hsi gave to the study of the principles of nature is missing
from the rescript, whose emphasis is not on scholarly excellence but on the many generations of
Japanese, going back to the foundation of the empire, who have loyally and faithfully served the
imperial family." The Rescript is silent on whether education should be compulsory, whether girls
should receive equal education to boys, or what role Western learning (science, law, medicine)
should play — questions the earlier Charter Oath of 1868 had addressed more progressively.

## Cult and Controversy

Almost immediately, the Rescript took on quasi-sacred status. In January 1891 — just months after
promulgation — the principal of a Tokyo high school ordered teachers and students to bow before
the imperial signature on the Rescript "in the manner we used to bow before our ancestral relics."
The Christian teacher Uchimura Kanzō refused to bow, writing later: "As I was the third in turn
to go up and bow, I had scarcely time to think upon the matter. So, hesitating in doubt, I took a
safer course for my Christian conscience, in the august presence of sixty professors and over one
thousand students, I took my stand and did not bow! It was an awful moment for me." Uchimura
eventually bowed under pressure but the incident revealed the Rescript's potential to function as
a religious object — and the coercive power that potential carried. His act of conscience
inaugurated a series of controversies about the relationship between Christianity, individual
conscience, and state obligation.

The same autumn of 1890, photographs of the emperor and empress were distributed to schools with
the instruction that teachers and pupils must worship them on major holidays. These two practices
— bowing before the Rescript, bowing before imperial photographs — were the seeds of emperor
worship in the schools that would blossom into state religion under the militarist regime of the
1930s–1940s.

## Causal Analysis

The Rescript was simultaneously the product of genuine concern (by the emperor, by Motoda, and
by provincial governors) about moral disorientation in a rapidly Westernizing society, and a
deliberate instrument of state-building. Inoue Kowashi's drafting principles — keep it simple,
non-partisan, avoid religious controversy — were designed to make it universal and unchallengeable.
The result was a document that could be wielded by any subsequent government to demand any level
of sacrifice from citizens in the name of the imperial polity.

## Consequence Analysis

The Rescript became the ideological cornerstone of Japanese public education from 1890 to 1945.
It contributed to [[japanese-militarism]] by providing a moral language of self-sacrifice and
loyalty that the militarists of the 1930s mobilized. It contributed to the suppression of
Christianity, socialism, and liberal individualism — all of which could be condemned as
inconsistent with the Rescript's loyalty ethic. It was formally rescinded by the Japanese
parliament on June 19, 1948, under the Allied occupation.

## Historiography

The Rescript is central to debates about the nature of Meiji ideology. One interpretation
emphasizes its Confucian character — it was primarily a conservative response to the moral
vacuum created by Westernization, drawing on pre-existing ethical frameworks. Another emphasizes
its novelty as state construction: Inoue and Motoda were not simply restoring tradition but
manufacturing a specifically state-centered tradition, grounding moral obligation in the imperial
lineage rather than in universal virtue. A third line of interpretation, associated with studies
of Japanese fascism, emphasizes its instrumental role in enabling militarist mobilization in the
1930s–1940s.
