# The Taika Reform (645)

The political revolution that began turning the loose [[yamato-japan|Yamato]] confederation
of clans into a **centralized imperial state on the [[tang-dynasty|Chinese]] model** —
the foundational event of classical [[japan|Japanese]] government, named for the era-name
*Taika* ("Great Change"). From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History
of Japan*]] (ch. 3).

## Narrative

In **645** opponents of the over-mighty **Soga** clan — Prince **Naka no Ōe** and
**Nakatomi no Kamatari** (later founder of the [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara]]) — struck the
Soga strongman down at court and seized power. To mark the change they named the era
*Taika* and, on **New Year's Day 646**, promulgated a four-article reform edict:

1. **Land and people** formerly controlled by the *uji* clans were placed under
   **imperial administration**.
2. A **capital city** and provincial administrative system were to be built.
3. **Population and tax registers** and land laws were to be compiled.
4. A new **system of taxation** was introduced.

These were principles, not instant facts: their realization took decades, culminating in
the **Taihō administrative and penal Code (701–702)** and the building of [[nara-period|Nara]]
(710). The state thereafter took taxes (rice, products, labor, military service) directly
from the people, allotted ricefields held in theory by the Crown, and paid aristocrats as
**officials** of the emperor rather than as autonomous lords.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Soga coup of 645** — the seizure of power that created the opening.
- `contributed_to:` the **[[tang-dynasty|Tang]] Chinese model**, carried back by scholars
  and embassies (the experts who advised the reformers had spent ~25 years in China), and
  [[shotoku-taishi|Prince Shōtoku's]] earlier Confucian centralizing principles.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **centralized imperial state** — the Taihō Code, ~66 provinces and ~600
  districts, the census, and direct taxation — the order of the [[nara-period|Nara]] and
  [[heian-period|Heian]] periods.
- `enabled:` the **eclipse of the autonomous uji**: clan leaders kept their status but now
  held it as a near-monopoly of **bureaucratic office** (a deliberate "principle of
  compensation"), so the reform was a political but **not a social** revolution.

## Significance

Mason and Caiger highlight the Taika–Taihō state as "a singular example of a society
attempting to set up a centralized imperial form of government **without** a strong
element of armed force and preliminary conquest." Though the ideal of direct imperial rule
later faded (giving way to [[fujiwara-clan|Fujiwara]] familism and the [[heian-period|shōen]]),
its administrative geography and court-rank system persisted into the nineteenth century —
making 645 a genuine turning point in Japanese history.
