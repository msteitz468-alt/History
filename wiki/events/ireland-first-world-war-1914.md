## Narrative

Ireland's experience of the [[first-world-war-1914]] was simultaneously integral to the British war effort and deeply inflected by Irish political divisions. Approximately 210,000 Irish men and a few hundred Irish women served in the British armed forces; around 40,000 were killed. The war was, as Roy Foster wrote, 'one of the most decisive events in modern Irish history': it established the preconditions for the [[easter-rising-1916]], accelerated the decline of the [[home-rule-movement]] and the Irish Parliamentary Party, and created the conditions that made partition — and the [[irish-war-of-independence-1919]] — likely rather than contingent.

## Recruitment

On the outbreak of war in August 1914, both nationalist leader John Redmond and Unionist leader Edward Carson pledged their paramilitaries to the British war effort. Redmond's famous Woodenbridge speech committed the Irish National Volunteers (some 180,000 strong) to serve 'wherever the firing line extends.' Carson similarly mobilized the Ulster Volunteer Force, which had been established to resist home rule.

Total Irish recruitment figures are disputed — Fitzpatrick's estimate of around 144,000 new recruits during the war (in addition to 58,000 regulars and reservists at the outbreak) is slightly higher than Jeffery's. The best government figures suggest that up to 15 April 1916, 29,617 UVF members and 30,161 Irish National Volunteer members had enlisted, alongside 57,863 men with no political affiliation. In absolute terms, neither paramilitary was 'rushing to the colours': the UVF had around 100,000 members in July 1914 and the INV perhaps 180,000, meaning only a minority of each enlisted. UVF members were considerably more likely to enlist than INV members, reflecting the greater loyalty motivation among Unionists.

Irish recruitment was overwhelmingly urban: between the outbreak of war and 15 October 1916, 38,543 recruits came from Belfast, 21,412 from Dublin, and 8,360 from Cork. Rural recruitment in Ulster was not noticeably higher than elsewhere. Fitzpatrick's argument that 'fraternities' (sporting clubs etc.) explained recruitment patterns is criticized by Bowman in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] (ch. 30) as 'fundamentally flawed'; economic factors — unskilled labourers could increase earnings by 154 per cent by enlisting — and loyalty or political affiliation were more important.

## The Two Irish Divisions

The 16th (Irish) Division was identified with the IPP and nationalist Ireland; the 36th (Ulster) Division with the UVF and Unionism. Both were products of Lord Kitchener's reluctant accommodation of Ireland's paramilitary organizations. Edward Carson secured key concessions for the 36th: the 'Ulster' designation was new in Army Lists; battalion names connected units to pre-war UVF regiments. The 16th (Irish) Division was initially raised under the War Office without any nationalist identity; a nationalist identity was belatedly provided when War Office recruiting failed. General Lawrence Parsons's obstruction of nationalist patronage — refusing Catholic officers, declining Irish National Volunteer battalion titles — damaged IPP-sponsored enlistment.

At the Battle of the Somme (July 1916), the 36th (Ulster) Division's action on 1 July — the day the assault began — became the centerpiece of Ulster Unionist war commemoration. Cyril Falls's *History of the 36th (Ulster) Division* (1922), written with the support of Carson and Craig, was as much propaganda establishing the new Northern Ireland state's legitimacy as military history.

The two divisions served in adjacent but largely separate operational areas; anecdotal evidence of fraternization ('I wonder if we shall get into trouble for fraternising with the enemy') reflects the sectarian tensions rather than genuine reconciliation. One inter-divisional incident involved soda-water bottles labelled 'Boyne Water' being misread as a sectarian provocation.

## The Conscription Crisis 1918

Conscription was never introduced in Ireland during the war, in contrast to Great Britain (where it was implemented in January 1916). By early 1918, voluntary Irish recruitment had collapsed. The decision in April 1918 to extend conscription to Ireland — taken by Lloyd George's government despite warnings from the Chief Secretary, the GOC in Ireland, and the Inspector General of the RIC that enforcement would require 6,000 extra troops and would generate serious disorder — was made, as Adrian Gregory argues, 'for wider British political purposes': a war-weary population in Britain would not accept further combing-out of essential industries unless Ireland was seen to contribute.

The Military Service Amendment Act passed 16 April 1918, but its Irish provisions were never implemented. Nationalist Ireland united against it: the IPP walked out of the Commons on first reading; the Irish Trades Union Congress called a one-day general strike (23 April), effective everywhere outside Unionist Ulster; and the Catholic bishops entered the fray with a resistance pledge. The crisis 'turned nationalist Ireland very firmly against the British war effort' and accelerated the collapse of the IPP and the rise of Sinn Féin in the December 1918 election.

## Memory and Commemoration

Ireland's memory of the war was bifurcated and politically charged. Southern Ireland experienced what F.X. Martin called 'national amnesia' regarding the 200,000 Irish who served in British forces: 'It was much more fashionable to claim that one had a relation who had fought in the GPO in 1916 than one who had served at Gallipoli or on the Western Front.' Rehabilitation of the southern Irish war dead has occurred only since the 1990s, through the establishment of veterans' associations and the National Museum gallery at Collins Barracks.

Northern Ireland generated a Unionist war myth centered almost exclusively on the 36th (Ulster) Division at the Somme. The Ulster Tower at Thiepval — the first British memorial completed on the Somme, dedicated on 18 November 1921 by Field Marshal Sir Henry Wilson — was built using UVF funds and based on Helen's Tower at Clandeboye (where the 36th had trained), 'designed to remind both Ulster Unionists and the British government of the sacrifices that Ulster had made for the empire.' The Belfast Cenotaph was not unveiled until 1929; when it was, the 16th (Irish) Division ex-servicemen's association was initially not invited to lay a wreath.

Commemorative politics in Northern Ireland were further complicated by the IRA's 'poppy day' bombing at Enniskillen in 1987, which killed eleven people at a Remembrance Sunday ceremony, and subsequently softened by Sinn Féin participation in remembrance ceremonies as part of the peace process.

## Significance

The war's significance for Irish history lay less in the military experience itself — 'the wartime experience of Irish soldiers was not that dissimilar to that of British soldiers as a whole' (Bowman) — than in its political consequences:

1. The war created the preconditions for the 1916 rising by diverting the majority of Volunteers to the Western Front, putting separatists inside an IRB minority in an advantageous position, and providing the British state with reason to respond to rebellion with wartime methods
2. The conscription crisis of 1918 unified Catholic Ireland, the Catholic Church, and Sinn Féin against the British government and destroyed the IPP's claim to represent Irish opinion
3. The Ulster Division's sacrifice at the Somme became the founding myth of Ulster Unionism, making partition psychologically and politically difficult to reverse
4. The Irish regiments' wartime service — and subsequent disbandment with the establishment of the Free State — created a legacy of 'national amnesia' that only began to be addressed in the late twentieth century

## Historiography

Bowman's Oxford Handbook chapter (ch. 30 of [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]]) is the primary basis for this page. Key works include Keith Jeffery, *Ireland and the Great War* (2000); David Fitzpatrick (ed.), *Ireland and the First World War* (1988); Timothy Bowman, *Irish Regiments in the Great War: Discipline and Morale* (2003); Richard Grayson, *Belfast Boys* (2010). The field is hampered by the destruction of approximately 50 per cent of British servicemen's records in the Second World War and by the political uses to which the war's memory has been put in Northern Ireland.
