# The Carolingian Conquest of the Lombard Kingdom (774)

## Narrative

In 773–774 **[[charlemagne]]**, answering an appeal from **Pope Hadrian I** against Lombard
pressure, crossed the Alps, besieged **Pavia**, and in 774 deposed **Desiderius** — his own
former father-in-law — taking for himself the title **king of the Lombards** and annexing the
[[lombards|Lombard kingdom]] of northern Italy to the Frankish realm. He confirmed and extended
his father [[pippin-iii|Pippin's]] territorial grants to the papacy. A later Lombard revolt was
suppressed. The conquest folded northern Italy permanently into the [[carolingian-empire]] —
though the southern Lombard duchy of **Benevento** remained effectively autonomous, never fully
subjected.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the Frankish–papal alliance forged at the [[pippinid-coup-751|coup of 751]] and the
  papacy's appeals against Lombard expansion
- `contributed_to:` Charlemagne's repudiation of his marriage to Desiderius's daughter, which broke
  the brief Franco-Lombard rapprochement
- `enabled:` the precedent of Pippin III's Italian interventions (755–756)

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **Carolingian rule in Italy** — Charlemagne as king of the Lombards, later sub-kings
  installed at Pavia, and a Frankish aristocracy grafted onto Lombard institutions
- `produced:` the confirmation of the **papal territories**, deepening the Frankish–papal partnership
  that culminated in [[coronation-of-charlemagne-800|the imperial coronation of 800]]
- `enabled:` the survival of an **autonomous Lombard south** (the principalities of Benevento, later
  Salerno and Capua), and a fragmented Italy of Frankish, papal, Byzantine, and Lombard zones

## Historiography

NCMH II (Delogu, Ch. 12) stresses the **continuity** of Lombard administrative structures under Carolingian
rule and the limits of Frankish control in central and southern Italy, where the duchies of Spoleto and
especially Benevento retained autonomy. The conquest is documented from the Frankish annals, the *Liber
Pontificalis*, and Lombard sources such as Paul the Deacon.
