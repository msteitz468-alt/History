# The Wars of Spanish American Independence (1808–1826)

The dissolution of [[colonial-latin-america|Iberian colonial rule]] in the Americas
— the end of three centuries of empire and the birth of the Latin American
republics (and the Brazilian monarchy). Following Burkholder and Johnson, the
collapse is best understood **not** as a long-planned anticolonial revolt but as the
explosion of an **imperial legitimacy crisis** triggered from Europe, into which the
[[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon reforms']] alienation of the Creole elite had already
loaded the charge.

## Narrative

**The 1808 crisis.** Napoleon's invasion of Iberia, the abdications of the Spanish
Bourbons, and the imposition of Joseph Bonaparte shattered royal legitimacy.
Across Spain and the colonies, **juntas** formed *to rule in the name of the captive
Ferdinand VII* — initially an act of loyalism, not separatism. The **Cortes of
Cádiz** and the liberal **Constitution of 1812** (popular sovereignty, a free press,
elected municipalities, Indian representation) drew Americans into an unprecedented
constitutional politics.

**From autonomy to independence.** From 1810 creole juntas in **Caracas, Buenos
Aires, Bogotá, Quito,** and elsewhere seized local power; **Buenos Aires** (May 1810)
never returned to Spanish control. The decisive radicalization came with
**Ferdinand VII's restoration in 1814**: he abrogated the constitution, jailed
liberals, and chose to reconquer by **military force**. This militarization — harsh
royalist reprisals and property confiscations (Morillo's expedition) — pushed
"pacified" elites toward full independence as the only guarantee of their property.

**The southern campaign.** **José de San Martín** ("the Protector") forged an army
in Mendoza, crossed the Andes to liberate **Chile** (1817–18, with Bernardo
O'Higgins), then sailed to **Peru**, securing Lima's declaration of independence
(1821) — but could not break the royalist interior. After meeting Bolívar at
Guayaquil (1822), he resigned and went into exile.

**The northern campaign.** **Simón Bolívar** ("the Liberator"), aided by Haiti's
**Pétion** (in return for a pledge to abolish slavery) and by the *llanero* cavalry of
**Páez**, won Venezuela and crossed the Andes to triumph at **Boyacá (1819)**,
proclaiming **Gran Colombia** (Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador). Victories at
**Carabobo (1821)** and Sucre's **Pichincha (1822)** completed northern liberation;
Bolívar and **Sucre** then finished the war in the Andes at **Junín and Ayacucho
(1824)**, with Upper Peru becoming **Bolivia (1825)**.

**Mexico.** The insurgency began as a popular, social rising under the priest
**Miguel Hidalgo** (1810, the *Grito de Dolores*, the Guanajuato massacre) and the
*casta* priest **José María Morelos** — both excommunicated and executed (1811,
1815) amid brutal insurgency/counterinsurgency. Independence came, paradoxically,
from the **right**: after Spain's 1820 liberal Riego revolt frightened Mexican
conservatives, the royalist creole officer **Agustín de Iturbide** allied with the
surviving patriots in the **Plan of Iguala (1821)** — independence, Catholicism, and
equality of Spaniards and Americans. Iturbide crowned himself **Emperor Agustín I**,
but his empire collapsed within a year into a **federal republic (1824)**. Central
America briefly joined, then separated.

**Brazil.** A distinct path: the Portuguese court had fled to **Rio de Janeiro
(1808)**, raising Brazil to a co-kingdom and opening its ports. When the Lisbon
*cortes* tried to recolonize after 1820, the regent prince **Dom Pedro** refused to
return, declared independence (1822), and became **Emperor Pedro I** — independence
**without** a destructive war and **as a monarchy**, preserving slavery and elite
continuity.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Napoleon's 1808 invasion** — the proximate trigger that voided royal
  legitimacy and spawned the juntas.
- `contributed_to:` the [[bourbon-reforms|Bourbon reforms]] — Creole alienation
  (discrimination in office), new taxes, and the revolts of the 1780s (Túpac Amaru,
  the Comuneros) had primed the elite.
- `contributed_to:` **Enlightenment and Atlantic-revolutionary ideas** (the US,
  French, and Haitian revolutions; economic societies; Freemasonry).
- `caused_by:` **Ferdinand VII's 1814 absolutism** — the choice of reconquest over
  compromise that converted autonomists into separatists.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **independent republics** of Spanish America and the **Empire of
  Brazil** — the end of the Iberian American empires by ~1826.
- `produced:` a generation of **caudillo** politics and fragmentation (Gran Colombia
  soon split; the Río de la Plata fragmented into four nations).
- `enabled:` the **survival of colonial structures** — the [[hacienda]], the
  [[casta-system|racial hierarchy]], slavery (abolished only piecemeal; Brazil not
  until 1888), and the export economy — into [[latin-america-modern|modern Latin
  America]]. Many slaves who fought on either side won freedom, and Indian tribute
  was abolished in law, but old burdens often survived "in disguised form."

## Actors

- **Bolívar** and **San Martín** — the two great liberators; **Sucre**, **O'Higgins**,
  **Páez**, **Belgrano**, **Artigas**. In Mexico, **Hidalgo**, **Morelos**, **Iturbide**.
  In Brazil, **John VI** and **Pedro I**.
- The wars were socially broad: enslaved and free blacks, *castas*, and indigenous
  communities fought on **both** sides (in 1820s Peru, ~85% of royalist manpower was
  nonwhite), bargaining military service for freedom and rights. Women served as
  soldiers, spies, financiers, and managers (Juana Azurduy; Manuela Sáenz; Josefa
  Ortiz).

## Historiography

The conflicts were **small-scale, brutal civil wars** (the largest battle, Ayacucho,
pitted ~6,000 against ~9,300), marked by high desertion, frequent side-switching,
amnesties, and devastating disease (yellow fever at Veracruz; ~90% peninsular losses
under Morillo). Modern scholarship stresses the **legitimacy-crisis** framing (the
1808 vacancy and the Cádiz constitutional experiment) over older nationalist
"awakening" narratives, and emphasizes how much of the **colonial order survived**
the political rupture. Independence is the hinge between this volume and
[[latin-america-modern|modern Latin America]].
