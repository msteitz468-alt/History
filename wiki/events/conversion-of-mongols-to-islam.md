The conversion of the western Mongol ruling elites to Islam — occurring separately in the three major Mongol successor states between approximately 1290 and 1330 — was, for Hodgson, the most striking demonstration of Islamicate civilization's strength in the Later Middle Period. Having devastated the central Muslim cities and destroyed the Abbasid Caliphate, the Mongol conquerors were within two to three generations absorbed into the very civilization they had overrun.

## Narrative

### The Golden Horde (by 1290)

The Golden Horde was the first western Mongol state to turn Muslim. Its settled population in the Volga basin had been increasingly Muslim since High Abbasid times, building on the earlier Islamization of the Bulghârs around Kazan (who had become Muslim in the early 900s and were already a significant Islamic cultural centre before Mongol conquest). By 1290 the Horde's ruler was a Muslim — a transition facilitated by the already-Muslim character of the commercial network and urban population on which the Horde's revenues depended. The Horde's Muslim identity deepened over subsequent generations, with significant consequences: it permanently alienated the Orthodox Christian Slavic tributary princes from any sense of shared civilization with their overlords.

### The Ilkhanate (1295)

The [[ilkhanate]] required more prolonged internal conflict before its conversion. The Ilkhans presided over a religiously diverse court — Buddhist monks (especially Tibetan lamas), Nestorian Christians, Jews (at one point a Jewish physician served as chief vizier), and Muslims all competed for influence. The Muslim faction was not always dominant: the chief minister Juvaynî (a Muslim scholar) was executed after associating with a briefly-reigning Muslim Mongol (1282–84); Juvaynî's successor, a Jewish physician, attempted to eliminate Muslim influence from government.

In 1295, Ghazan — previously a Buddhist — took the throne and forthwith turned Muslim:

> "He insisted that the Mongol nobles turn Muslim too, and eventually the more old-fashioned Mongols were silenced and the Buddhist monks were largely driven from the realm. At Tabrîz, the capital, not only Buddhist temples but churches and synagogues were torn down." (Hodgson)

Ghazan's conversion did not immediately end war with the Muslim Mamluk sultanate in Egypt — by 1300 Damascus was occupied, and only in 1303 was the Mongol advance into Syria decisively defeated. But the Ilkhanate's Islamization meant that Islamic scholarship was now patronized rather than suppressed: Ghazan's vizier Rashîduddîn Fażlullâh produced comprehensive historical works (a world history drawing on Chinese, Mongol, and Muslim sources) before his execution at age 70 in 1318.

### The Chaghatay Khanate (1326)

The Chaghatay conversion was the most contested and least durable. The khanate's territory straddled a sharp frontier: the southwestern (Transoxianian) population was already Muslim, while northeastern (Yedisu steppe) Mongols held that Islam was incompatible with authentic Mongol identity. Only in 1326 did a Muslim khan succeed — and he was killed within a decade (1334) by northeastern rebels. The state then broke up, with the Syr-Oxus basin remaining Muslim but eastern areas only converting much later.

## Significance

Hodgson uses the conversion as evidence against the "decadence" thesis for the Later Middle Period:

> "In absorbing and converting the pagan Mongols who occupied the central Muslim territories, the international Islamicate society demonstrated its strength; and in the following two centuries it continued to develop in most respects along the lines laid out in the Earlier Middle Period."

The conversion was not simply a religious event: it represented the full absorption of the Mongol ruling elites into the Persian-language cultural sphere (the Mongols adopted Persian as their language of high culture), the integration of Mongol political traditions (including the *military-patronage-state* model) into Islamicate governance, and the extension of the Dar al-Islam northward into regions formerly outside it.

Several structural factors facilitated conversion:
- **Commercial networks**: Long-distance trade throughout the Mongol domains was already dominated by Muslim merchants; the Mongol lords depended on these networks and their Muslim operators
- **Cultural prestige**: Islamic civilization — Persian literature, Islamic scholarship, Ṣûfî spirituality — was the most sophisticated cultural tradition available to the Mongol conquerors in their western domains
- **Political utility**: Ruling Muslim majorities as a Muslim ruler was more efficient than maintaining an alien pagan identity; Islam provided legitimacy with the subject population
- **Ṣûfî accommodation**: The flexibility of Ṣûfî Islam, with its accommodation to local customs and its prestige of personal spiritual power, made conversion culturally feasible without demanding complete abandonment of Mongol traditions

## Comparison with Other Conversions

**analogous_to**: The conversion of the Seljuk Turks to Islam (10th–11th centuries), which followed a structurally similar pattern — nomadic conquerors absorbed into the urban Islamic civilization they had overrun. **contrasts_with**: The Mongol attempt to convert the Western Christian world (via Nestorian intermediaries and diplomatic overtures to the Papacy), which failed to produce any significant result.

## Historiography

Evidence for these conversions rests heavily on later Persian and Arabic chronicle and hagiographic traditions — including the Sufi-mediated conversion narratives for figures like Tughluq Timur that Formichi (2020) explicitly treats as retrospective hagiography composed generations after the fact to legitimate a ruling house's Islamic credentials, not contemporary record. Hodgson's synthesis, composed in the 1950s–60s and published posthumously in 1974, represents an earlier generation of Islamicate historiography; more recent scholarship, including Formichi's, has extended and complicated the picture, particularly for the Chaghatayid/eastern steppe conversions. Debate continues over how genuine and rapid elite conversion actually was versus a longer, more syncretic process, and over how much weight to give court religious performance as against continuing popular or steppe religious practice beneath it.
