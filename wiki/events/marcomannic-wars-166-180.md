# The Marcomannic Wars (166–180 CE)

The long series of wars on the **middle and upper Danube** by which **[[marcus-aurelius|Marcus
Aurelius]]** fought off a coalition of Germanic and Sarmatian peoples — "a war of many nations"
that brought **barbarians into Italy for the first time since the Cimbri** and consumed the rest
of his reign. Widely regarded as a **turning-point** in the history of the empire. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 3.

## Causes

CAH XI sets the wars against a deeper movement: **pressure building up between the Danube and the
Baltic**, "remoter barbarians" driving frontier peoples (the Marcomanni, Quadi, Sarmatian
Iazyges, and migrants like the Langobardi and Victuali) to seek entry into the empire — part, the
volume suggests, of the same upheaval as the **long migration of the Goths** from the Baltic to
the Black Sea. Two contingent factors turned pressure into catastrophe: the **denuding of the
Danube** of three legions and many troops to fight the **Parthian War**, and the **[[antonine-plague-165|Antonine
Plague]]** that ravaged the army and the empire's manpower.

## Narrative

After early incursions (the Langobardi and Obii repelled in Pannonia, 166/7), the dam broke: the
Marcomanni and their allies poured across the Danube, and in a shocking blow **invaded northern
Italy**, besieging **Aquileia** and sacking **Opitergium** — the first such invasion in nearly
three centuries. Marcus (with **[[lucius-verus]]** until his death in 169) made the Danube his
headquarters for the rest of his life, raising new legions (II and III Italica) and waging
grinding campaigns against the **Marcomanni and Quadi** and the **Iazyges**. The wars are
commemorated on the **Column of Marcus Aurelius** (and include the famous "rain miracle" of the
Quadic campaign). Marcus appears to have contemplated **annexing new trans-Danubian provinces**
(Marcomannia, Sarmatia) — a plan cut short by the revolt of **Avidius Cassius** (175) and finally
by his own death.

## Consequence Analysis

- `caused_by:` the migratory pressure on the Danube; `contributed_to:` by the Parthian War and the
  plague.
- `produced:` the **settlement of vast numbers of defeated barbarians within the empire** (from
  Germany to Moesia, and even in Italy — until a group settled at Ravenna seized the town and the
  policy was halted).
- `produced:` the consumption of Marcus' reign and treasury (he auctioned imperial treasures to
  fund the wars), and the unfinished settlement his son **[[commodus]]** hastened to conclude by
  buying peace (180).
- Marks the **turning-point** from the secure Antonine peace toward the **age of crisis** — the
  beginning of the sustained barbarian pressure on the northern frontier that would define the
  third century.

## Significance

The Marcomannic Wars shattered the illusion, voiced in Aelius Aristides' panegyric, that "war had
become only a memory." They revealed the empire's **vulnerability to coordinated frontier
pressure** once its forces were committed elsewhere, exhausted its resources, and inaugurated the
long defensive struggle on the Rhine–Danube line. They are conventionally taken, with the plague
and the accession of Commodus, to close the "golden age" of the High Empire.

## Historiography

The wars are frustratingly poorly narrated for events of such consequence. **Cassius
Dio**'s contemporary-adjacent account survives only through the later Byzantine epitome
of Xiphilinus, stripped of much original detail and chronological precision. The
**Historia Augusta**'s *Life of Marcus Aurelius* is notoriously unreliable — a much
later composite work prone to invention and anecdote — and cannot be trusted for
specifics without external corroboration. The **Column of Marcus Aurelius** in Rome
offers rich visual evidence of campaign episodes (including the famous "rain miracle"),
but correlating its reliefs with a precise chronology of events is difficult and
disputed among modern scholars. Marcus's own *Meditations*, written largely during the
campaigns, gives insight into his state of mind but almost no narrative detail. As a
result, the exact sequence, number, and geography of individual campaigns (166–180) are
substantially reconstructed by modern historians from a patchwork of epigraphic,
numismatic, and archaeological evidence rather than a coherent ancient narrative.
