# Blomberg–Fritsch Affair (1938)

## Narrative

In late January and early February 1938 the Third Reich was shaken by a double scandal involving the two most senior military leaders: War Minister Werner von Blomberg and Army Commander-in-Chief Werner von Fritsch. The affair was entirely unforeseen by Hitler and arose from personal matters, but its outcome dramatically strengthened Hitler's personal control over the armed forces and removed remaining voices of caution.

## The Blomberg Marriage Scandal

In September 1937, the widowed Field-Marshal Blomberg met the thirty-five-years-younger Margarethe Gruhn while walking in the Tiergarten. He became besotted. He asked Hitler (as supreme commander) for consent to marry, describing her as a 'girl from the people'. Hitler offered to be a witness (along with Göring) to demonstrate rejection of class snobbery. The secret wedding took place on 12 January 1938 in the War Ministry, attended only by the couple, their witnesses, and immediate family. No celebrations followed.

Within days, Berlin prostitutes were talking of one of their number having married the War Minister. An anonymous tip reached Fritsch. The Gestapo and Berlin Police Chief Helldorff discovered Gruhn's police record: pornographic photos (taken by a Jew of Czech origin with whom she was living), registered as a prostitute in 1932, theft accusation in 1934.

On 21 January Göring confirmed the identity. He awaited Hitler's return from Bavaria with the brown file. Hitler was stunned: prudery and racial prejudice combined ('a Jew of Czech origin'). He felt personally humiliated as witness. He paced his room muttering 'If a German Field-Marshal marries a whore, anything in the world is possible.' Goebbels: 'The worst crisis of the regime since the Röhm affair... The Führer looks like a corpse.'

Göring tried to persuade Blomberg to annul the marriage immediately. Blomberg refused. On 27 January Hitler had a final audience (initially heated, then calmer). Blomberg was offered rejoining in war; he departed for Italy on 28 January with a 50,000-Mark handshake and full pension. He survived the war praising the 'genius' of the Führer but was never recalled; he died shunned in Nuremberg prison 1946.

## The Fritsch Case

The same evening (24 January), in low mood, Hitler recalled a 1936 file Himmler had shown him about Fritsch allegedly being blackmailed by Berlin rent-boy Otto Schmidt for homosexual acts in late 1933. Hitler had ordered the file destroyed; Heydrich had kept it in a safe.

The 'reconstructed' file was on Hitler's desk by 2.15 a.m. on 25 January. Hitler was still considering Fritsch as possible War Minister successor on 26 January. He wanted assurance against further scandals.

Fritsch was confronted on the evening of 25 January in Hitler's library: head of army in civilian clothes facing his accuser (a proven blackmailer from Börgermoor camp) in the presence of Hitler and Göring. Fritsch vehemently denied everything and gave his word of honour. Schmidt insisted on identification. Hitler expected Fritsch to throw the file at his feet; the subdued reaction did not impress him. 'These sort of people always do that.'

Gestapo interrogation on 27 January remained inconclusive (details in Schmidt's story were demonstrably wrong — smoking, fur coat, rank title Fritsch had not yet held — but not acted upon). Justice Minister Gürtner produced a damning report (Fritsch had 'not proved his innocence'; the Hitler Youth boy story was damaging). Gürtner insisted on a military court trial.

## The Reshuffle (4–5 February 1938)

Hitler cancelled his 30 January Reichstag speech and cabinet meeting. Goebbels suggested Hitler take over the whole Wehrmacht with services as ministries.

Hitler decided (picking up Blomberg's farewell suggestion) to take personal command of the Wehrmacht, appointing no War Minister successor. Keitel (recommended by Blomberg) was initiated as his adviser. This shifted power from the army general staff to the Wehrmacht office directly under Hitler.

On 3 February Fritsch was asked for his resignation. On 4 February the sensational communiqué announced sweeping changes 'in the interest of the strongest concentration of all political, military, and economic forces in the hand of the supreme leader'. Twelve generals (besides Blomberg and Fritsch) dismissed; fifty-one other posts refilled (many in Luftwaffe). Brauchitsch replaced Fritsch. Göring received a Field-Marshal's baton as consolation. Neurath was 'elevated' to a never-meeting privy council; Ribbentrop took the Foreign Office. Schacht's replacement by Funk was included.

On 5 February Hitler addressed the generals with the police reports and Gürtner's assessment. The officers were benumbed; no objections raised. General Liebmann: 'the impression... was downright crushing... the army... had suffered a devastating blow'. That evening Hitler spoke emotionally to the last cabinet meeting of the Third Reich.

Blomberg and Fritsch were officially retired 'on health grounds'. Fritsch's innocence was established by military court 18 March 1938 (mistaken identity). Embittered but still claiming to be 'a good National Socialist', he volunteered for his old regiment and was killed near Warsaw 22 September 1939.

## Analysis (Kershaw)

The affair was not manufactured by Hitler; it arose by chance from Blomberg's personal life and the revival of an old file. Hitler was genuinely shocked and depressed. The outcome, however, was a major victory for him: absolute personal command of the armed forces, removal of the two most senior figures who had counselled caution, and a purge that emasculated potential opposition in the officer corps at precisely the moment his foreign-policy adventurism was entering a new phase.

The army swallowed the dominance without protest. Prestige damage was masked by the Anschluss triumph weeks later. The last institutional restraints on Hitler from within the military élite were broken. 'In this Reich, everyone in any responsible position is a National Socialist' (Hitler to Reichstag, 20 February 1938).

## From Evans, *The Third Reich in Power* (2005)

Evans's account (ingested 2026-07-02) matches Kershaw's on the mechanics and adds the structural framing: the men removed in the reshuffle — Blomberg, Fritsch, Neurath, and (via his November 1937 resignation) [[schacht-hjalmar|Schacht]] — were precisely those who had voiced doubts about the accelerated war timetable at the [[hossbach-memorandum-1937|Hossbach conference]]. The scandals were windfall pretexts exploited to complete, in one stroke, Hitler's unified control of military, foreign, and economic policy weeks before the [[anschluss-1938|Anschluss]]. Evans also notes Heydrich had preserved the Fritsch file after Hitler ordered it destroyed, and reads Göring's Field-Marshal baton as a deliberate containment of Göring's War-Ministry ambitions.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer adds documentary texture from Jodl's diary: 26 January 1938 — "What influence a woman, even without realizing it, can exert on the history of a country and thereby on the world!... One has the feeling of living in a fateful hour for the German people," referring to Erna Gruhn (Shirer gives this name rather than "Margarethe"; the wiki should note the name discrepancy across sources — Shirer 1960). Shirer notes Blomberg had five children by his first wife, the youngest of whom had married Keitel's son in 1937, and that Göring had pre-approved the marriage and helped ship off a "rival lover" to South America.

Shirer's account of the discovery adds an institutional detail: the police file was routed by Police Chief Count von Helldorf to Keitel rather than to Himmler, out of fear the SS would use it for blackmail — Keitel then passed it to Göring instead of protecting Blomberg. Jodl's diary (two days after Hitler's fury) records General Beck's own verdict, relayed via Keitel: "one cannot tolerate the highest-ranking soldier marrying a whore."

[EYEWITNESS-adjacent, documented] Naval adjutant Lt. von Wangenheim was sent by Raeder to Capri to press Blomberg to divorce "for the honor of the officer corps"; he went further, urging Blomberg to shoot himself and offering a revolver, which Blomberg refused, writing to Keitel that they had "quite different views and standards of life." Hitler had privately promised Blomberg restoration to supreme command "as soon as Germany's hour comes" — a promise never kept; Blomberg died in Nuremberg jail 13 March 1946 awaiting testimony (Shirer 1960).

On the Fritsch case, Shirer identifies the accuser by name as career criminal/blackmailer Hans Schmidt, who had actually blackmailed a different officer — retired cavalry captain "Rittmeister von Frisch" — near the Potsdam railroad station, and falsely identified General von Fritsch at the confrontation. Colonel Hossbach, Hitler's adjutant, defied orders to warn Fritsch in advance, who called the charge "a lot of stinking lies!" Fritsch was acquitted at court-martial 17–18 March 1938 once the real blackmail victim's identity was confirmed and Schmidt recanted under Göring's own questioning — but was never restored to command. He later challenged Himmler to a duel (drafted by Beck, entrusted to Rundstedt to deliver), which lapsed when Rundstedt "got cold feet" (Shirer 1960).

[EYEWITNESS] Shirer attended Fritsch's funeral after his death near besieged Warsaw on 22 September 1939 (Shirer 1960 records Fritsch may have deliberately exposed himself to fire — "I shall accompany my regiment only as a target"), calling it "one of the dreariest days... I ever lived through in the capital." Shirer also carries Fritsch's bitter December 1938 letter to Baroness Margot von Schutzbar naming "three battles" Germany needed to win: "1. The battle against the working class—Hitler has won this. 2. Against the Catholic Church... and 3. Against the Jews. We are in the midst of these battles and the one against the Jews is the most difficult" — significant primary-source evidence of the conservative military elite's own antisemitism, relevant to [[appeasement]] and the historiography of officer-corps complicity.

Shirer's verdict on the 4 February 1938 reshuffle matches Kershaw and Evans: "a major turning point... the Nazi revolution... was completed," with the conservative "brake" on Hitler eliminated. He is sharply critical of the officer corps's passivity: "For five years up to this winter day of February 4, 1938, the Army had possessed the physical power to overthrow Hitler and the Third Reich... why did it not attempt to do so?" — quoting Ulrich von Hassell's diary (18 December 1938) recording Fritsch's own fatalism: "This man—Hitler—is Germany's destiny for good and for evil. If he now goes over the abyss... he will drag us all down with him. There is nothing we can do."

## Historiography

Source base: Jodl's diary, the Hossbach/Nuremberg testimony of the participants, and Gestapo file reconstructions — much of it first synthesized by Wheeler-Bennett and Shirer; Kershaw and Evans supply the modern institutional reading (the affair as Hitler's opportunistic capture of the army, not a planned purge). The unresolved question is how far Göring and Himmler manufactured versus merely exploited the scandals; the Fritsch frame-up is documented, the Blomberg marriage's engineering is disputed. Collection coverage: strong (Kershaw, Evans, Shirer).
