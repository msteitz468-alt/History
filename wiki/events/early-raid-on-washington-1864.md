# Early's Raid on Washington (June–July 1864)

Confederate expedition under [[actors/early-jubal-a|Jubal A. Early]], detached by [[actors/robert-e-lee|Robert E. Lee]], that moved down the Shenandoah Valley into Maryland and to the gates of [[places/washington-dc|Washington, D.C.]] in July 1864. Ellsworth frames this as the capital's darkest military hour of the war's final year.

## Narrative

Lee sent Early to clear the Valley, cross into Maryland, and threaten Washington — forcing Grant to detach forces from the Richmond–Petersburg front. After [[events/battle-of-monocacy-1864|Monocacy]] (9 July), Early probed the northern forts. Reinforcements arrived in time; Early withdrew south without taking the city. Skirmishing near Fort Stevens entered Lincoln lore (presidential exposure on the works).

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by / contributed_to:** Lee's strategic diversion under siege pressure; Union overstretch after Overland losses.
- **produced:** Monocacy; temporary Union panic; limited operational relief for Lee.
- **did not produce:** Capture of Washington or forced Union peace.

## Historiography

Scale of the threat is debated (coup opportunity vs. raid). Popular narratives (including Ellsworth) emphasize contingency; operational studies situate Early within Valley logistics and Grant's troop-shuttling capacity.


## Ellsworth Detail (*Midnight on the Potomac*, 2025)

Lee's gamble rested on Conrad's intel that Burnside's corps was with Grant (not defending the capital) and on the Valley opening after Union failure at Lynchburg. Early's column (~10,000) marched >155 miles in nine days without meeting Union troops; crossed the Potomac at Shepherdstown 4–5 July into Maryland, then turned southeast.

After Monocacy, refugees and cut telegraphs/rail made Washington feel besieged; Navy kept a ship ready "for the President's escape." On **11 July** Early saw the Capitol dome from Seventh Street Road—within the District, **<5 miles from the White House**—after marching >260 miles in <19 days in 90° heat. Ellsworth's interpretive climax: Early "blinked"—no general advance that day; 12 July probes at Fort Stevens called off as Grant's reinforcements arrived; two days later Rebels recrossed into Virginia. Lincoln on 12 July stood on Fort Stevens parapet in stovepipe hat until surgeon Crawford three feet away was hit in the leg.
