# 1991 and the End of the Cold War

> **Transition page** (CLAUDE.md standing list: "1991 and the end of the Cold War").
> Marks the discontinuity between the [[cold-war|Cold War]] and the
> [[contemporary|Contemporary]] period.

## Narrative

The collapse of communism in Eastern Europe (the **[[revolutions-of-1989|Revolutions of
1989]]**) and the dissolution of the **Soviet Union (1991)** ended the bipolar order and
opened the [[contemporary|Contemporary]] period. The diplomatic-political story — narrated in
the **[[leffler-westad-cold-war-v3|Cambridge History of the Cold War]]** — centres on
**Mikhail Gorbachev**: his reforms (*perestroika*, *glasnost*), his abandonment of the
**Brezhnev Doctrine** (letting Eastern Europe go without force), the failure of the
**[[soviet-war-in-afghanistan-1979|Afghan war]]**, the **reunification of Germany (1990)**,
and the Soviet collapse (December 1991). The volume stresses the **contingency and speed** of
this "incredibly swift transition," framing the end less as a Western "victory" by
[[containment]] than as a transformation driven from within the Soviet system. In [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s framing,
1989–1991 is less a triumph of one ideology than a hinge in the long material story:
it coincided with the maturing of the post-1970 wave of "**new globalization**"
(Tyrrell) and the decisive **East Asian ascent** that was recentering the world
economy (Selden; see [[east-asia-modern]], [[transpacific-economy]]). Roshwald marks
it as the moment the **nation state** achieved near-planetary acceptance with "the
final collapse of Soviet internationalism" (see [[nationalism]]).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the structural exhaustion of the Soviet command economy and the
  unsustainable costs of superpower competition; reform (perestroika) and its
  unintended consequences.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the brief **unipolar moment** of US hegemony; the spread of the
  [[nationalism|national state]] to the former Soviet space — and renewed conflict
  along the seams of the former multinational empires (the Balkans, the Caucasus; see
  [[genocide]]).
- `enabled:` **hyper-globalization** and the deeply integrated, single global economy
  of the [[contemporary|contemporary period]]; the consolidation of the
  [[great-divergence|Great Divergence]]'s partial reversal as China rose.

## Historiography

Selden notes that while the US–Soviet conflict ended in 1989–1990, "the primary rift
in East Asia, pivoting on the US–China clash, was bridged beginning in the 1970s" — so
the region's decisive turning point preceded the global one. CWH VII, published 2015,
writes the post-1991 world as the still-open culmination of the
[[great-acceleration|Great Acceleration]] and the [[anthropocene|Anthropocene]].

For the global Cold War whose end this marks, see
**[[westad-cold-war|Westad's *The Cold War: A World History*]]** (the Soviet collapse as contingent, not a
simple American "victory") and **[[judt-postwar|Judt's *Postwar*]]** on 1989 in Europe.
