# The First Crusade (1095–1099)

## Narrative

The **First Crusade** was proclaimed by **[[urban-ii|Pope Urban II]]** at the council
of **Clermont** on **27 November 1095**, the climax of his year-long progress through
France. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Jonathan Riley-Smith, Ch. 14) shows that
the *casus belli* — the Seljuk Turkish occupation of Asia Minor and a Byzantine appeal
that reached Urban at Piacenza in March 1095 — was the occasion rather than the deep
cause: Urban proposed something far more ambitious than the defence of Byzantium,
namely a **war of liberation** with two aims, the freeing of the **eastern Christians**
and of the **place of Jerusalem** from Muslim rule. He attached to it the novel
apparatus of the [[crusades|crusade]]: a **vow**, the wearing of the **cross**, and an
**indulgence** remitting the penance due for sin — making the expedition a penitential
armed pilgrimage.

The response vastly exceeded expectation. A disorderly **"People's Crusade"** under
Peter the Hermit set off early, massacring Jewish communities in the **Rhineland**
(Speyer, Worms, Mainz) in 1096 before being destroyed in Anatolia. The main armies of
the princes — Godfrey of Bouillon and his brother Baldwin, Raymond of Toulouse,
Bohemond of Taranto, Robert of Normandy, and others — assembled at **Constantinople**,
swore (uneasily) to restore former imperial territory to **Alexios I**, crossed into
Asia Minor, took **Nicaea (1097)**, won at **Dorylaeum**, and endured the gruelling
siege of **Antioch (1097–98)**, which Bohemond seized and kept. Pressing south, the
crusaders stormed **Jerusalem on 15 July 1099**, massacring much of its population, and
established the Latin states of the East. The victory — the one unambiguous success in
the whole history of crusading — seemed to contemporaries a manifest miracle.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the [[gregorian-reform|reform papacy's]] readiness to authorise
sanctified violence and its search (after the breach with the empire) for lay military
support — the *milites sancti Petri*, the Norman alliance, and the [[just-war|just-war]]
theology of Anselm of Lucca later codified by Gratian.
`caused_by:` the Seljuk conquest of Anatolia after [[battle-of-manzikert-1071|Manzikert
(1071)]] and the consequent Byzantine appeal.
`contributed_to:` the pilgrimage tradition and the powerful idea of liberating the Holy
Sepulchre, already present in papal circles since Gregory VII's plan of 1074.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **[[crusader-states|crusader states]]** — the Kingdom of Jerusalem and
the counties/principalities of Edessa, Antioch, and Tripoli.
`produced:` the launching of the [[crusades|crusading movement]] as an enduring
institution of Latin Christendom.
`produced:` the **1096 massacres** of Rhineland Jewry — a landmark in the deteriorating
position of European Jews (see [[ashkenazi-jewry]]).
`enabled:` a long-running antagonism with [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] (the broken
promises over reconquered territory) and with the Islamic Near East.

## From Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Causes and Clermont

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] joins remote causes — **pilgrimage** to Jerusalem (surge in the 10th–11th c.; Cluniac organisation; overland route after Hungarian conversion; Seljuk disruption after Manzikert 1071 and Turkish capture of Jerusalem the same year) and **holy war** in Spain/Sicily (Reconquista; Alexander II 1063; Gregory VII’s Spanish expeditions and abortive idea of knights for the Eastern emperor) — with the proximate contingency that **Alexius I**, not in perilous crisis in 1095 but wanting fresh troops to clear Anatolia, sent envoys to **Piacenza (March 1095)** seeking knightly warriors framed as reopening pilgrim routes. “What Alexius wanted was Western mercenaries; what he got was the First Crusade.” Clermont’s **indulgence** remitted **penance** for those going solely from devotion — not forgiveness of sins without confession — though preaching could imply full remission. Motives mixed and partly unknowable (demographic pressure, 1094 flooding, 1095 drought/famine, younger sons, lordly influence, land prospects, armed pilgrimage, penance). Background restraints: [[peace-and-truce-of-god|Peace and Truce of God]].

### People’s Crusade and princely recruitment

**Peter the Hermit** preached within weeks of Clermont; by Cologne (April 1096) ~10,000, then ~20,000 including knights. Indiscipline: killed **4,000** in one Hungarian town; savaged and burned Belgrade; escorted to Constantinople (1 August 1096); Alexius shipped them to Anatolia where they were disastrously defeated **21 October**. Peter later entered Jerusalem with the main army after surviving desertion. Princes: Raymond of Toulouse, Robert of Normandy, Godfrey of Lower Lorraine and Baldwin, Bohemond of Taranto; Urban appointed the bishop of Le Puy legate-leader.

### Rhineland and Bohemian Jewish massacres (1096) — full record

Three German crusading armies raised to fight the “infidel” in the East began by **slaughtering Jews in the West**:

- At **Worms (May 1096)** they killed **all the Jews in the ghetto** and **even those given sanctuary by the Catholic bishop**.
- At **Mainz**, a massacre lasting **several days**.
- Further massacres at **Ratisbon (Regensburg)**, **Neuss**, **Wevelinghofen**, and **Xanten**.
- In Bohemia, they **exterminated Jews at Prague**.

Despite attempts of local bishops to protect Jews, “barbaric savagery was carried out in the name of the cross.” Contemporaries saw **divine judgement** when these crusaders met disaster in Hungary. Motivations Logan names: **greed, hatred**, and “unbelievers” close at hand.

### Main armies, Anatolia, Outremer foundations, Jerusalem

Main forces used pilgrim routes (Hungary/Balkans or Adriatic–Dyrrachion); arrived Constantinople by **May 1097** (total maybe **>60,000**); Alexius extracted oaths to restore former imperial lands (Raymond demurred, took modified oath) and ferried them across by end of May. **Nicaea** fell **19 June**; Turkish defeat **1 July** opened Anatolia. **Baldwin** entered **Edessa 6 February 1098**, became count by March — first crusader state — then left the crusade. **Antioch** siege **21 Oct 1097–3 June 1098** (secure 28 June); legate bishop of Le Puy died afterward. At **Maarat** hungry victors **ate victims** — memory lasting among Arabs. **Jerusalem** entered **14 July 1099** (Logan’s date) via siege-tower under Godfrey; crusaders **butchered Muslims and Jews of every age including children and infants**; an observer reports wading **ankle-deep in blood** in the Temple, then thanksgiving at the Holy Sepulchre.

## Historiography

NCMH IV's Riley-Smith embeds the First Crusade firmly in the **eleventh-century
reformation**, treating it less as a sudden eruption of religious violence than as the
logical outgrowth of reform-era ideas about authorised, penitential war — a reading
(the "pluralist" school he helped found) that stresses the crusaders' sincere
penitential motives over the older economic/younger-son explanations.

**Byzantine and Muslim perspectives** have been systematically underrepresented in
Western historiography. The Dumbarton Oaks volume edited by Laiou and Mottahedeh
(2001) addresses this gap. Giles Constable's contribution documents that the crusades
were always experienced from multiple vantage points — Alexios Komnenos's distrust
of crusading armies is preserved in Anna Komnene's *Alexiad*; the Jews who suffered
the 1096 Rhineland massacres have their own Hebrew chronicles; and the rulers of
Edessa, Antioch, Aleppo, Damascus, Cairo, and Baghdad each had distinct attitudes
reflected in their own sources. George T. Dennis's essay in the same volume argues
that the Byzantines regarded the crusade as essentially incomprehensible — they
understood pilgrimage and they understood warfare, but not the combination — and
abhorrent in its implications (the crusader ideology of "exalting" Christianity by
force, and monk-warriors like the Templars, were alien to Byzantine theology).
See [[concepts/crusade-historiography]] and [[concepts/byzantine-just-war]].

Logan quotes **Runciman** on crusade triumphs as faith without wisdom and “a long act of intolerance in the name of God, which is the sin against the Holy Ghost,” noting not all concur but the issues cannot be neglected. Logan himself argues crusades were **marginal** to medieval Europe’s central lines (possibly more central to Middle East history) while papal holy war deeply affected church life by **sanctifying armed force** after early Christian pacifism and limited Augustinian just war. Further reading map: Runciman, Riley-Smith, Tyerman, Asbridge, Hillenbrand, Kedar.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Paul M. Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014)]] retells the First Crusade as medieval Muslims experienced it — a multi-front Mediterranean crisis, not a Jerusalem-only pilgrimage story.

**Structural setting.** For Syrians the Frankish invasion was not the first foreign conquest of the age: the **Great Saljuqs** had already twice overrun Syria, and “the manner of their arrival and governance actually helped the Franks more than it hindered them.” Cobb’s counterfactual is blunt: had the decades before 1097 gone differently, “it is unlikely that the Franks would have ever made it to Jerusalem.” By the time the Franks reached northern Syria, Saljuq politics there were “so poisonous that a concerted response… was impossible.” Seen from Syria, “the road to Jerusalem in 1099 begins in Aleppo in 1064” (Mirdasid–Turcoman opening, then Tutush’s appanage, then Ridwan of Aleppo vs Duqaq of Damascus with atabeg Tughtakin). Near-simultaneous leadership collapse — Nizam al-Mulk and Malikshah dead (1092), 1094 as “the year of the death of caliphs and commanders” (ʿAbbasid, Fatimid, and Badr al-Jamali) — left “the entire chessboard… swept of all its pieces” on the eve of invasion.

**Anatolian approach.** After Sicily and Toledo, Frankish armies with Byzantine aid entered a “stateless” Anatolian frontier (*Bilad al-Rum*) of **Saljuqs of Rum** (Qilij Arslan at Nicaea) and **Danishmendids**. The 1096 “People’s” wave was destroyed near Kibotos; Qilij Arslan then left for a Malatya dispute with the Danishmendids. From April 1097 the main host (modern estimate ~**75,000** plus Byzantines — hedge) took Nicaea and routed him; at **Dorylaeum** (July) a joint Saljuq–Danishmend force was overwhelmed when a second Frankish contingent arrived — for Ibn al-Qalanisi a “shameful calamity to the cause of Islam.” Baldwin of Boulogne, invited by the Armenian Tʿoros, created the **county of Edessa** by March 1098 — “the first of the more or less Frankish states… in the Islamic Near East” — while the main army assembled before Antioch.

**Syrian and Fatimid calculus.** Relief coalitions around Antioch collapsed in personal spite (Yaghi-Siyan, Ridwan, Karbuqa, Sukman); Fatimids negotiated a separate peace at the siege, “relieving [the Franks] of a threat from Egypt for the time being.” After Antioch, no Muslim chronicler left a detailed account of the march south — Cobb relies primarily on Frankish narratives for that leg. Local accommodation eased the road: Shayzar’s lord **Sultan** (Banu Munqidh) supplied markets and guides rather than fight; Homs and Hama followed; only Hisn al-Akrad offered serious resistance. The Fatimid offer of unarmed pilgrimage access to Jerusalem (recently retaken from the Saljuqs, Sept 1098) was refused. On **7 June 1099** the Franks encamped before a modest city (~**20,000–30,000**; garrison “perhaps one thousand”). The sack — al-Aqsa killings “improbably” numbered in the thousands, synagogue burned, Dome of the Rock stripped — became the atrocity yardstick set earlier at **Maʿarra** (“They did there like what they had done at Maʿarra”). Al-Afdal’s relieving army was routed near Ascalon with “no intelligence” of the Frankish approach.

**Emic causation and aftermath.** Cobb’s dominant Muslim line of reasoning (if one must be singled out): Frankish *jihad* against Islam began in the **1060s** on many Mediterranean fronts, and success should be blamed on a **divided umma**. A minority strand (al-ʿAzimi) echoes pilgrim-obstruction motives Urban II is said to have aired; Cobb notes al-ʿAzimi may have recycled Frankish lore after the fact. Jerusalem’s loss was “above all a local, Levantine calamity” and a blow to Fatimid authority more than a pan-Islamic apocalypse. Ramadan 1099 refugees pleaded in Baghdad; a caliphal mission aborted amid civil war — “The rulers were all at odds with one another… and so the Franks conquered the lands.” Jerusalem remained Frankish nearly a century as “a prize not so much the product of divine will or Crusader fervor as of **Fatimid vacillation and the disunity of a distracted Saljuq elite**.” The 1101 Anatolian reinforcement wave was wiped out by prepared Saljuq–Danishmend–Ridwan cooperation, denying the land route for decades.

## From Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (Ch. LVIII)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]]; not asserted as modern fact.

- **Peter the Hermit.** After the Turkish conquest of Jerusalem, Peter of Amiens returns “an accomplished fanatic”; Urban receives him as a prophet; barefoot preaching with crucifix and ass moves palace and cottage — “eloquence of tears and visions” outdoing Athenian oratory.
- **Gregory conceived, Urban executed.** Gregory VII already designed arming Europe against Asia (~50,000 under St. Peter’s banner); “the glory or reproach of executing… this holy enterprise, was reserved for Urban the Second” amid schism and investiture strife; Greek ambassadors at Piacenza; Clermont decides on Urban’s native soil.
- **Plenary indulgence.** At Clermont Urban proclaims plenary indulgence and full remittance of penance for those enlisting under the cross — converting the penance system into holy war; Gibbon stresses that cold modern philosophy cannot feel the impression on a sinful fanatic age.
- **Mixed motives.** All were prompted by enthusiasm; yet “in many it was not the sole, that in some it was not the leading, principle of action.” Secondary motives: glory, Asian wealth, escape from feudal and ecclesiastical tyranny, debt suspension, outlaw impunity; fashion and example as a whirlpool.
