# The Saxon Wars (772–804)

The **Saxon wars** were the longest, bloodiest, and most ideologically driven of
[[charlemagne|Charles the Great]]'s campaigns — more than three decades of intermittent war
to subdue and forcibly Christianise the pagan Saxons between the Rhine/Weser and the Elbe.

## Narrative

Per [[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Janet Nelson]], the 775 campaign set the recurring
template: two armies, forced river crossings, sequential negotiation with the three Saxon
peoples (Westphalians, Angrarians, Eastphalians), and hostage-taking as the mark of victory.
At **Paderborn (777)**, the first Frankish assembly held on Saxon soil, the fidelity oath was
extended for the first time "to his sons and to the Franks."

Major reversals punctuated the war. At the **Süntel disaster (782)** Frankish *missi* charged
without scouting because "they feared [Count] Theoderic would get the glory," and were "killed
almost to a man." The notorious **massacre at Verden (782)** — 4,500 reportedly executed —
Nelson treats sceptically (the round figure "could well have resulted from a scribe … adding a
zero"), and she argues the brutal *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* may have been a
**response** to revolt rather than its cause. The submission and baptism of the resistance
leader [[widukind]] at Attigny (785) prompted the annals' premature verdict "**then all Saxony
was subjugated**" — before the great revolt of 793.

From **795** Charles turned to **deportation**: "a new strategy centred on the taking of
hostages and captives, and the rounding-up of deportees." The 799 Paderborn transfer settled
Saxons across the realm, redistributing their land "among his faithful men … bishops, priests,
counts and … vassals." A later petitioner's gloss survives: "*transmigratio* was a euphemism
for *deportatio*." Yet the **Capitulare Saxonicum of 797** softened the regime — equal fines
for Saxon and Frank, compensation over death penalties, no tithe. The wars are conventionally
dated to a close in **804**.

## Causal Analysis

- `contributed_to: [[charlemagne]]` — Frankish expansionism and the drive to a defensible,
  Christian eastern frontier.
- `caused_by:` the **ideology of forced conversion** — Nelson stresses that "Charles and his
  advisers were driven less by practical considerations than by ideology," especially in the
  *Capitulatio*'s death penalties for paganism.
- `concurrent_with:` endemic frontier raiding in both directions; Nelson cautions that
  archaeology shows Saxons and Franks "difficult to distinguish in the field," so the war was
  not a clash of sharply separate peoples.

## Consequence Analysis

Saxony was permanently incorporated into the [[carolingian-empire]] and Latin Christendom,
its resisting aristocracy selectively removed by deportation and replaced or co-opted under
Frankish counts and bishops. The strategic logic, Nelson notes, was elite-decapitation:
Charles "wanted to deprive the land of its elite," not to punish the population collectively.

## Historiography

The Saxon wars are documented almost entirely from the **Frankish side** — the *Annales regni
Francorum* and its Reviser, the capitularies, and later Saxon writers (Widukind of Corvey,
tenth-century) who retrospectively recast their ancestors' subjugation as a founding ordeal. No
contemporary Saxon voice survives. The two enduring debates are over **scale and causation**:

- **The Verden massacre (782).** [[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Nelson]] joins a
  sceptical tradition questioning the figure of 4,500 executed — "suspiciously large, and its
  tidiness increases suspicion" (a possible scribal extra zero) — though the brutality of the
  campaign is not in doubt.
- **The *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae*.** Nelson argues its death penalties for paganism may
  have been a **response** to revolt rather than its cause, and reflect ideology more than
  practical calculation — reversing the older assumption that Frankish severity simply provoked
  resistance.
- **Forced conversion.** Modern scholarship (Alcuin himself protested coerced baptism and
  tithing) reads the religious programme as exceptionally harsh even by Carolingian standards;
  Nelson cautions against treating "the Saxons" and "the Franks" as sharply distinct peoples,
  since archaeology shows them "difficult to distinguish in the field."

The collection covers the wars well through Frankish narrative and capitulary sources (NCMH II;
Nelson 2019) but is, like the sources themselves, thin on the Saxon experience except where
charters and a rare petition let individual deportees be glimpsed.
