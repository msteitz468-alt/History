# The Battle of Marathon (490 BCE)

At **Marathon**, in north-east Attica, the citizen hoplites of [[athens|Athens]] and
**Plataea** defeated the seaborne invasion force of [[achaemenid-empire|Darius I]] under
**Datis and Artaphernes** — the victory that repulsed the **first Persian invasion of
Greece** and became, for fifth-century Greeks and for later Europe, the symbol of "free men
pitted against an oriental despotism." [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 9, N. G. L.
Hammond) reconstructs the campaign.

## The campaign

After consolidating the European [[thrace|satrapy of Skudra]] (Mardonius, 492) and exacting
**earth and water** from the islands (Aegina and many mainlanders medized), Darius sent
**Datis the Mede** and his nephew **Artaphernes** with a fleet Hammond reckons at ~300
triremes plus horse-transports, ~25,000 infantry and ~800 cavalry — c. 90,000 persons in all
(the figure of the contemporary **Simonides**). In a **bold new strategy** (credited to
Darius), the fleet struck **out across the open Aegean** "through the islands" rather than
hugging the coast: Naxos was burned, Carystus reduced, and **Eretria betrayed and sacked
after a six-day siege**, its people deported. Guided by the ex-tyrant **Hippias**, the
Persians then landed on the sheltered beach of **Marathon** — flat ground ideal for cavalry,
close to their Eretrian base.

## The battle

Athens marched out at once (Miltiades' proposal), took position blocking the roads to
Athens, and was joined by the **full Plataean levy of 1,000**; a runner (**Philippides**) was
sent to Sparta, which by religious law could not march until the full moon. For days the
armies faced each other. The decision to engage hung on a **5–5 split of the ten generals**,
broken by the vote of the **polemarch Callimachus** (and the four pro-engagement generals
ceded their days of command to **[[miltiades|Miltiades]]**).

On the **17th of Boedromion**, with the Persian cavalry away (the cry "the cavalry are away"),
Miltiades attacked: he **thinned his centre and strengthened the wings**, ran the last ~140 m
through the arrow-barrage, and crashed into the Persian line. The Persian **wings collapsed**;
their strong **centre** (Persians and Sacae) broke the Greek centre and pursued — but the
victorious Greek wings **wheeled inward and took the centre from the rear**. The Persians
fled to their ships through the Great Marsh; **seven ships were captured**, Callimachus and
Aeschylus' brother Cynegeirus among the Greek fallen. The Persian fleet then rounded Sunium
toward an undefended Athens (the disputed **shield-signal** suggesting treachery), but the
Athenians **marched the ~40 km back the same day** and reached the city first; the fleet
withdrew. **192 Athenian dead** lie under the Great Mound (the Soros); Persian dead "about
6,400." The Spartan vanguard arrived after the battle, inspected the field, and praised the
Athenians.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: **Persian vengeance** for Athenian and Eretrian help in the
[[ionian-revolt-499bce|Ionian Revolt]] — above all the **burning of [[sardis|Sardis]]** — and
Darius' wider drive of westward expansion, with the restoration of the tyrant **Hippias** as
a lever.
contributed_to: the **medizing collapse** of 491 (the islands and Aegina submitting) which
brought Persia's orbit to the mainland and made resistance urgent.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **repulse of the first invasion** and a vast surge of **Athenian
self-confidence** — Marathon became the founding glory of the democracy (the cult at the
Great Mound, the Stoa Poikile painting, the *Marathonomachai*).
enabled: the **stiffening of Greek will** before Xerxes' far greater invasion of 480; the
brief political supremacy of **[[miltiades]]** (and his ruinous Paros expedition the next
year). Hammond stresses it "affected Persia less deeply" — the loss of 6,000 men "beyond its
western frontier" did not shake the empire, and merely made **Darius more determined** to
conquer Greece outright.

## Historiography

The narrative is **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]** (Book VI), defended by Hammond as
reliable **contemporary history** checked by veteran audiences, supplemented by **Aeschylus'
Persae**, the lost Stoa Poikile painting (via Pausanias), the **Soros** excavation and its
Persian arrowheads, the commemorative tetradrachms, and the Persepolis tablets (confirming
**Datis**). The chief disputes are the **role of the cavalry** (absent from Herodotus'
battle, explained by Hammond as away watering, not absent from Marathon), the **command
structure** (polemarch vs. generals), and Grote's attempt to **re-date** the Aegina war —
which Hammond rejects, since Herodotus *and* Thucydides anchored events to Marathon.

### Osborne (2000)

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed., 2000)]] (Ch. 7, Kallet) corroborates
Herodotus's figures — about 6,400 Persian dead against 192 (of 10,000) Athenian dead —
while flagging them as Herodotus's own reckoning rather than an independently verified
count. Osborne stresses the victory's significance for Athenian self-image above its strategic
weight: it was the **first time other Greeks took serious notice of Athens**, a city whose
archaic-period impact on the wider Greek world had been minimal, and it fixed itself so deeply
in Athenian identity that **Aeschylus's own epitaph** celebrated his part in the Marathon
victory without a word about his tragedies.

### Hall (2014)

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]: after Carystus, Eretria fell after seven-day siege through betrayal; temples sacked, inhabitants enslaved eastward; Persian fleet sailed to Marathon with ex-tyrant Hippias hoping for reinstallation as Persian vassal. ~9,000 Athenians camped near Heracles sanctuary; Phidippides sought Sparta but Carnea delayed them until full moon; 1,000 Plataeans aided Athens; Persian numbers exaggerated but likely ≥2:1 advantage; command rotated among ten Cleisthenic tribal *stratêgoi*; Datis re-embarked cavalry believing Athenians would not fight. Tactics: charge across ~1 mile; center thinned, wings strengthened; Persians broke center (Aristides/Themistocles’ tribes) but wings enveloped; Persians bogged in marshes; 192 Athenians including polemarch Callimachus vs traditional 6,400 Persian dead; fleet failed to beat army back to Athens. From Persian perspective not a major catastrophe. Post-Marathon ostracisms of Pisistratid/Alcmaeonid associates and Medism accusations reflect demotic critique of elite eastern luxury as well as treason fear after Hippias at Marathon.

### CHGRW contribution

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|Whitby (CHGRW I.3)]]:
Marathon reconstruction problems — Herodotus compatible with Nepos’ Miltiades but seems
ignorant of local topography; open questions on Persian cavalry location and why Athenians
attacked before Spartan help; Hodge’s geography supports older hypothesis that cavalry had
already embarked and Athenians struck for fear of treachery in the city. Wheeler (I.6): no
detailed Greek battle account exists before Herodotus on Marathon — a Greek–barbarian clash
outside agonal rules and steeped in Athenian propaganda; Aristophanes *Vesp.* 1085 veterans
equate fighting and pushing (“we pushed them… until evening”) as audience recognition of
*othismos*, not tactical precision. van Wees (I.9): Marathon’s 9,000 Athenian hoplites refute
claim that lower-status hoplite recruitment is only classical — far exceeds highest property
classes alone.
