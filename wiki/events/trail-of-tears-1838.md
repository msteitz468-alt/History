# Trail of Tears (Cherokee Removal, 1838–1839)

## Narrative

Forced removal of the bulk of the [[cherokee-nation]] from the Southeast to [[indian-territory]] after the Treaty of [[new-echota]] (December 1835). Cherokees called the winter migration the **“trail where we cried.”** Michael D. Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8. Parallel removals struck [[creek-muscogee|Creek]], [[choctaw-nation|Choctaw]], [[chickasaw-nation|Chickasaw]], and [[seminole]] nations under the same [[indian-removal-act-1830]] regime; this page centers the Cherokee National Party experience.

### Legal prelude

Georgia required non-Indians in the Cherokee Nation (except federal officials) to register and swear allegiance. Missionaries Samuel Worcester and Elizur Butler (ABCFM) refused for a test case; convicted to four years hard labor. **[[worcester-v-georgia-1832]]** (March 1832): Marshall held Georgia jurisdiction null and void—Cherokee Nation retained sovereignty outside enumerated surrenders. Court had no enforcement power; Georgia rejected the decision and continued legal harassment. Principal Chief John Ross led a publicity blitz and majority policy of refusal to negotiate, relying on American sense of justice; hoped 1832 election would remove Jackson.

### Treaty of New Echota (Dec 1835)

A small number of well-educated, highly acculturated Cherokees—**Elias Boudinot**, **John Ridge**, **Major Ridge** best articulating the view—concluded Ross was naive: American racism and economic ambition, and a political order committed to removal, meant only safety lay in flight; essence of nation is people not land. Despite the Ross government and national policy, Boudinot, the Ridges, and about **100** like-minded Cherokees signed a removal treaty at Boudinot’s home in New Echota (Cherokee capital). Ross petition: virtually the entire population opposed; signers had neither official standing nor authority—**yet the Senate ratified**. Terms: Cherokees out of the eastern nation by spring 1838.

### Forced roundup, stockades, and march (atrocity-dense)

Treaty Party migrated in 1837, joining Old Settlers west of the Arkansas River—together ~**6,000**. Bulk of the nation under Ross: passive resistance; Ross certain American public would prevent forced removal. **June 1838 deadline: no preparation.**

U.S. troops and Georgia militia entered the nation, **rounded up the people, often at gunpoint**, and herded them to **temporary stockades** built to hold them until they were shipped out. Suffering from **exposure and a shortage of food and water**, bereft of possessions and in dire need, **many people sickened and died in the camps**. Ross then negotiated that the Cherokee government organize and conduct its own removal.

During the winter of **1838–39**, under conditions so horrendous the Cherokees termed it the **“trail where we cried,”** they struggled toward western homes. The **“National Party”**—some **15,000** Cherokees led by Ross—made the journey.

### Aftermath in the West

Western tension: Old Settlers had decades of their own institutions; Treaty Party feared retribution; Nationals expected to dominate via constitutional government and majority. **Summer 1839:** Boudinot and the two Ridges were killed. Ross always claimed the government had not ordered their execution despite capital-crime framing of unauthorized treaty; deaths precipitated **civil strife with further bloodshed lasting seven years**. In **1846** three factions made peace, forming the basis for a united Cherokee Nation in Indian Territory.

Eastern Band: >100 families had taken 640-acre reserves as state citizens under 1817/1819 treaties; harassment drove many back, but several western North Carolina families held allotments **exempt from the 1835 removal treaty**, forming the **Eastern Band of Cherokees** in the Smoky Mountains (ongoing).

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: Treaty of New Echota signed by unauthorized Treaty Party and Senate ratification despite national opposition
- caused_by: [[indian-removal-act-1830]] and Jackson Administration refusal to enforce protective treaty provisions against Georgia
- contributed_to: by Georgia legal assault (jurisdiction extension, property stripping) after [[worcester-v-georgia-1832]] went unenforced
- preceded_by: Cherokee constitutional government (1827–28) and civilization peak that failed to stop removal
- part_of: [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880]]

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: mass mortality in stockades and on the winter trail; forced transfer of ~15,000 Nationals to [[indian-territory]]
- produced: Treaty Party executions (1839) and seven-year Cherokee civil strife; 1846 reunification
- contributed_to: cotton-belt consolidation on seized eastern lands
- enabled: later Indian Territory nation-building, missions, and slaveholding planter economy among western Cherokees
- concurrent_with: other southern and northern removals under the same federal policy

## Actors

John Ross (National Party / Principal Chief); Treaty Party (Elias Boudinot, John Ridge, Major Ridge); Old Settlers in the West; U.S. Army and Georgia militia; missionaries Worcester and Butler; [[jackson-andrew]] administration (policy frame; Van Buren presidency during 1838 roundup).

## Historiography

Death-toll estimates have varied; Washburn & Trigger note that some historians exaggerated Cherokee removal death figures from unfamiliarity with primary sources (citing Prucha)—a **historiographical caution about inflated numbers**, not a denial that camps and trail killed many. Moral and legal condemnation of forced removal is not seriously contested among modern historians; factional Cherokee politics (Treaty Party rationale vs National resistance) and comparison with Choctaw (~2,500 dead), Creek winter march, and Seminole War require multi-sided treatment. Atrocity documentation is first-class historical content: stockades, gunpoint roundups, exposure, food/water shortage, winter march naming. Key guides: Foreman, McLoughlin, Perdue, Wallace *Long, Bitter Trail*, Green *Politics of Indian Removal*.
