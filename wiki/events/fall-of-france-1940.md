# Fall of France (May–June 1940)

Rapid German conquest of France and Low Countries using innovative mobile warfare.

## Narrative

See detailed tactical analysis at [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/second-world-war/fall-of-france-1940]].

German forces bypassed Maginot Line via Ardennes, encircled Allied armies in north, leading to Dunkirk and French surrender.

## Causal Analysis

Allied complacency, poor coordination, German combined arms (Panzers + air + infantry), Manstein plan, Guderian's drive.

## Consequence Analysis

Britain isolated but defiant; US involvement accelerated; Germany turned east; end of Third Republic. `produced:` the [[franco-german-armistice-1940]] and [[vichy-france]] — though Paxton (1972) stresses the armistice was a choice, not a necessity: the North African option was viable (Noguès's forces "burning" to fight, the fleet intact, the *Massilia* actually sailing), and "there were valid alternatives to an armistice… they were Hitler's nightmare." Paxton also rejects treason/Fifth Column explanations of the defeat itself: thin Meuse defenses, slow communications, obsolete tactics, sagging morale. `enabled:` the [[national-revolution-vichy]] and Free France ([[de-gaulle-charles]]).

## Historiography

Older accounts leaned on treason/Fifth Column and material-inferiority explanations for France's collapse; Paxton (1972) and, more recently, Jackson and Frieser argue against inevitability — Allied forces were not materially outmatched (roughly comparable divisional counts, Allied artillery superiority, tank parity), and the defeat is better explained by doctrine (dispersed vs. concentrated armor), coordination failures, and reaction speed than by any structural inferiority. Evans (2008) stresses the razor-thin contingency of the campaign (a captured invasion plan forcing the switch to the Ardennes "sickle cut") alongside genuine command failures. Sources: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]], [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]], [[paxton-vichy-france-1972]].

## From Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

The original invasion plan literally fell into Allied hands (a staff officer's forced landing in Belgium, January 1940), easing Hitler's switch to [[manstein-erich-von|Manstein's]] Ardennes "sickle-cut," demonstrated to Hitler 17 February 1940. Evans stresses the Allies were not materially inferior: ~3,000 modern French tanks to ~2,500 German, 103 Allied divisions to 93 German; the decisive asymmetries were concentration and command. Kleist's Ardennes column — 134,000 men, 1,222 tanks, ~40,000 vehicles, "the greatest traffic jam known to that date in Europe," crews on "panzer chocolate" amphetamines for three sleepless days — crossed the Meuse on 13 May behind an eight-hour, 1,000-plane bombardment; Reynaud phoned Churchill on the 15th: "We have been defeated." Tanks reached the Channel 20 May. The Dunkirk halt order (Hitler's, on Rundstedt's advice and [[goring-hermann|Göring's]] boast that the Luftwaffe would finish the job) let ~340,000 troops escape on 860 vessels. [[petain-philippe|Pétain]] argued the armistice as "the necessary condition of the durability of eternal France"; it was signed in Foch's railway carriage at Compiègne on Hitler's order. ~120,000 French dead, ~1.5 million POWs; German dead under 50,000. Evans gives the *exode* its weight — 6–8 million refugees (Lille 200,000 → 20,000; Chartres 23,000 → 800), Irène Némirovsky's "chaotic multitude trudging through the dust" — and Hitler's pre-dawn Paris tour of 28 June 1940 with Speer ("It was the dream of my life to see Paris"). Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

**Anti-inevitability thesis.** Paxton and Hessler (citing Jackson and Frieser) argue the German victory was not foreordained by material weakness: the Allies fielded 94 French, 10 British, 8 Dutch, and 22 Belgian divisions against 134 German divisions, with Allied artillery superior and tank strength roughly at parity (some Allied tanks superior individually). The defeat is better explained by doctrine and coordination failures — French tanks were dispersed as infantry-support cover rather than concentrated, air-ground coordination lagged, and Allied reaction speed could not match German tempo — compounded by Allied disunity: Belgian and Dutch neutrality held until the moment of invasion, and Lord Gort's British Expeditionary Force mounted a solitary counterattack before withdrawing on 28 May, an act "the French have always considered... a betrayal."

**Manstein/Guderian mechanics.** Ten Panzer divisions drove through the Ardennes to Sedan, crossing the Meuse on 13 May — two days ahead of French expectations — while French high command spent three critical days misreading the main German thrust as coming through Belgium. De Gaulle's piecemeal armored counterattacks were, in his own later judgment, "always one jump too late." Hitler's pause before Dunkirk allowed the evacuation of roughly 200,000 British and 130,000 French troops between 28 May and 3 June.

**Armistice dynamics.** Pétain's push for an armistice was driven substantially by fear of social revolution should the war continue; Reynaud resigned as premier on 16 June rather than preside over capitulation. Paxton and Hessler's verdict: France's defeat came "in a bare six weeks."

**Phony War color.** Before the German blow fell, the Western Allies were, if anything, more eager to open a front against the USSR than against Germany — plans circulated for a Caucasus oil-bombing campaign and a Balkan front, and a scheme to aid Finland against the Soviets in the Winter War evolved into the Norway intervention plan, forestalled by the German invasion of Denmark and Norway on 9 April 1940 (Denmark fell after just 13 casualties). Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
