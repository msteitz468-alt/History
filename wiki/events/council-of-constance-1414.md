# Council of Constance (1414–1418)

## Narrative

The **Council of Constance**, opened in **1414** under the patronage of **Emperor Sigismund**, ended the **[[great-schism-1378|Great Schism]]** by securing the removal of rival papal claimants and the election of **Martin V** (1417). It also tried and executed **[[hus-jan|Jan Hus]]** (1415) and issued the conciliar decrees *Haec sancta* and *Frequens* that became touchstones of [[conciliarism]].

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[great-schism-1378]]` — dual/triple papacy after 1378
- `preceded_by:` Council of Pisa (1409), which added a third claimant rather than healing the split
- `contributed_to: [[conciliarism]]` — theory that a general council could act superior to a pope in emergency

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` restored single line of Roman popes (Martin V); martyrdom of Hus and stimulus to Bohemian dissent
- `enabled:` temporary conciliar constitutional claims; later conflict with papal restoration (Basel etc.)
- `followed_by:` fifteenth-century popes-and-councils struggles (Logan ch. 17)

## Historiography

Classic tension: was Constance primarily a successful reunification council, a constitutional revolution for the church, or both? Hus's trial is a separate moral and legal controversy. Logan narrates Constance as the resolution of the schism road that ran through Pisa.

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Summons: reform, not schism resolution

Contra a common claim that emperor-elect **Sigismund** forced a reluctant John XXIII to call Constance, Logan holds Sigismund mainly prevailed on the **place** (Constance in lands he controlled). The council was summoned (John’s formal call 9 Dec 1413; opened **5 Nov 1414**) to complete [[council-of-pisa-1409|Pisa]]’s unfinished **internal reform**, not to resolve the schism—that turn was unforeseen.

### Scale and duration

Constance sat continuously over **three and a half years** with forty-five full sessions and countless committee meetings; composition broader than Pisa (remote British Isles and Scandinavia, Silesia, Transylvania, later Iberia; Eastern observers from Greece and Constantinople)—“the greatest representative assembly of the Middle Ages,” Logan allows, may be justified.

### Pierre d’Ailly and voting by nations

**Pierre d’Ailly** virtually took control: rejected Italian bishops’ memorandum (7 Dec 1414) limiting the council to confirming Pisa; put church unity with reform on the agenda; urged John to abdicate as good shepherd. On **7 Feb 1415** Constance organized into four nations (English incl. Scots/Irish; French; German incl. Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, Denmark; Italian); later cardinals and Spanish formed “nations”; voting by head within nations, by nation in general sessions—to prevent Italian numerical domination.

### John XXIII: flight and deposition

On **2 March 1415** John publicly promised genuine abdication if Benedict XIII and Gregory XII also renounced; German, French, and English nations demanded immediate resignation; on **20 March** he fled Constance in disguise; an armed force of 300 returned him; on **29 May 1415** the council deposed him without his answering “highly inflated charges”; he later ratified by resigning and in 1419 paid homage to the new pope, who made him a cardinal.

### Gregory’s resignation; Benedict’s deposition; *Frequens*; Martin V

On **4 July 1415** Gregory XII’s representatives formally convoked Constance as a general council and submitted his resignation. Spanish kings and Scottish legates abandoned Benedict XIII (13 Dec 1415); he was formally deposed **16 July 1417** and died 23 May 1423 still claiming legitimacy. Stalemate over reforming before electing broken by **Henry Beaufort** (early Oct 1417): promulgate agreed reforms, elect a pope, defer other reforms. On **9 Oct 1417** the council by its own authority issued five reforming decrees including ***Frequens***—councils five years after Constance, then after seven years, then every ten years. Electoral body (23 cardinals + six per nation) elected **[[martin-v|Odo Colonna as Martin V]]** on **11 Nov 1417**. “The schism was truly over.”

### Effect on the medieval papacy

Logan rejects calling Constance “the end of the medieval papacy” as exaggeration: the 1418 papacy differed from 1378; nations questioned the identity of the pope not the institution; conciliar superiority claims persisted into the fifteenth century—but conciliarism did not foment sixteenth-century changes; after Constance the principal challenge was reform and revitalizing Christian spiritual life.
