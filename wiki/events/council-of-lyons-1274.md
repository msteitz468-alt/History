## Narrative

Convened by Pope Gregory X at Lyon in 1274, the Second Council of Lyon sought to reunite the Latin and Greek churches, coordinate a new crusade to the Holy Land, and pursue internal church reform. Byzantine Emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos, seeking Western backing against the expansionist ambitions of Charles of Anjou in the Balkans and Mediterranean, sent representatives who accepted union with Rome and the disputed *Filioque* clause (the Western addition to the Nicene Creed asserting the Holy Spirit's procession from the Son as well as the Father). The agreement was reached under strong political pressure on the Byzantine side rather than genuine theological consensus, and it was met with fierce resistance from much of the Byzantine clergy and populace, who regarded the concession as a betrayal of Orthodox doctrine and practice.

## Causal Analysis

The union was `caused_by` Michael VIII's urgent need for Western diplomatic cover against Charles of Anjou's threatened invasion, and was `enabled` by Gregory X's genuine ambition to restore Christian unity ahead of a planned crusade.

## Consequence Analysis

The council `produced` a formal but deeply unpopular union that `contributed_to` internal Byzantine political and religious turmoil throughout Michael VIII's remaining reign; the union `enabled` a temporary respite from Western threats but collapsed almost immediately after Michael VIII's death in 1282, when his successor Andronikos II repudiated it. The episode `contributed_to` cementing the *Filioque* as the emblematic, irreconcilable point of the broader Latin-Greek schism polemic.

## Historiography

The council is a central case study in Byzantine-Latin relations historiography for the limits of politically-driven religious reunion; Byzantine sources (notably later chroniclers hostile to Michael VIII) emphasize the union's unpopularity and its role in undermining his domestic legitimacy, while Latin sources of the period present the union more straightforwardly as a diplomatic achievement.
