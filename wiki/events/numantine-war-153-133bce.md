# The Celtiberian Wars and the Fall of Numantia (153–133 BCE)

The long, brutal Spanish wars of 153–133, remembered for the extraordinary
resistance of the Celtiberian town of **Numantia** and ended by its destruction at
the hands of **[[scipio-aemilianus|Scipio Aemilianus]]**. Treated in
[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 5 (Harris); paired in those decades with the
[[viriathus|Lusitanian war of Viriathus]].

## Narrative

After the Gracchan settlement of the 170s, fighting resumed c. 154 when the
Lusitanians invaded the Roman province and the **Aravaci** (Celtiberians) rebelled
over a fortification dispute. Polybius calls the Celtiberian conflict **"the fiery
war"** for its violence; awareness of it caused the **recruiting crisis at Rome in
151**. A sequence of commanders failed or resorted to atrocity (L. Lucullus
massacred the surrendered Vaccaei of Cauca, 151; Galba massacred Lusitanians, 150).
Roman generals repeatedly made treaties with the Celtiberians that the **Senate
repudiated** — Marcellus (151), Q. Pompeius (139), and most notoriously
**Hostilius Mancinus** (137), who surrendered his army on oath only to have the
treaty disavowed and himself handed over naked to the Numantines.

By 134 only the Aravaci of **Numantia** held out — with perhaps 8,000 fighting men
in 144. Rome turned to **[[scipio-aemilianus]]**, conqueror of Carthage, elected
consul (134) despite the legal bar. Refusing to assault, he raised a force that
swelled to 60,000, ravaged the Vaccaei, and **closely besieged Numantia** for
months. After frightful suffering the survivors surrendered in summer **133**;
Scipio chose fifty for his triumph, sold the rest, and razed the town.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **Roman drive to expand** (Harris) compounded by **provincial
misgovernment** — the *avaritia* and treaty-breaking of governors, which the
*lex Calpurnia* of 149 created the first standing *repetundae* court to check.
Celtiberian "raids" into Roman territory were often pretexts.
contributed_to: the **terrain and semi-pastoral mobility** of the Celtiberians, and
the **declining quality and discipline** of a Roman army no one wished to serve in.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **fall of Numantia (133)** and the near-completion of the conquest of
Spain (the far north left untouched by design).
enabled: at Rome, the war's recruiting crises, atrocities, and humiliations
(Mancinus) fed the political pressures that erupted in the **Gracchan crisis** —
itself reserved to CAH IX. Among those present at the siege were **Polybius,
C. Gracchus, Jugurtha, and Marius**.

## Historiography

**Appian** (*Iberica*) is the principal narrative from 154; **Polybius** (an
eyewitness at Numantia) and the Livian epitomes supplement. Schulten's
excavations at Numantia are the archaeological backbone. The wars are central to
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]: Harris reads the relentless Roman pressure,
pretextual casus belli, and massacres as evidence of systemic aggression, against
which the resistance of Numantia became a Spanish national symbol.
