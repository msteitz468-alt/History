## Narrative

In 399 BCE, [[actors/socrates|Socrates]] was formally charged before an Athenian jury of several hundred citizens with impiety (failing to acknowledge the city's gods and introducing new divinities) and with corrupting the young. The accusers — Meletus, backed by the politically prominent Anytus and the orator Lycon — brought the case in the fraught atmosphere following the restoration of Athenian democracy after the brief, bloody rule of the oligarchic Thirty Tyrants, several of whose leading members (notably Critias) had been associated with Socrates. Socrates's defense, as reconstructed in Plato's *Apology*, mounted a defiant rather than conciliatory case, and the jury convicted him by a relatively narrow margin. Under Athenian procedure, Socrates proposed his own counter-penalty (reportedly public maintenance rather than a fine or exile), a response the jury took as provocative, and a larger majority then voted for the death sentence. Execution was delayed several weeks by the ritual absence of the state ship on its annual mission to Delos, during which Socrates remained in prison (declining an opportunity to escape, as dramatized in Plato's *Crito*) before drinking the hemlock draught, an episode narrated in the *Phaedo*.

## Causal Analysis

The trial was caused_by the formal impiety and corruption charges but contributed_to significantly by the political trauma of the Thirty Tyrants' recent rule and Socrates's known associations with several of its members, even though the amnesty following the democratic restoration formally barred prosecution for prior political conduct.

## Consequence Analysis

Socrates's execution produced a foundational trauma and touchstone for the subsequent Athenian philosophical tradition, directly shaping [[actors/plato|Plato's]] turn away from Athenian democratic politics and profoundly influencing the entire subsequent trajectory of Western political philosophy's treatment of the relationship between the individual conscience and the state.

## Historiography

The trial is known almost entirely through Socrates's students, chiefly Plato (*Apology*, *Crito*, *Phaedo*) and, in a markedly different and more pragmatically apologetic vein, Xenophon (*Apology*, *Memorabilia*) — both fundamentally sympathetic sources with their own philosophical agendas, meaning no neutral or hostile contemporary account survives. Historians debate how far Plato's *Apology* represents Socrates's actual courtroom speech versus a later literary and philosophical reconstruction, and the precise motives of the historical Anytus and Meletus remain genuinely uncertain given the loss of any prosecution-side source.
