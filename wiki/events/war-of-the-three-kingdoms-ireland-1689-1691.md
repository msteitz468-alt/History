## Narrative

The Irish dimension of the Williamite Revolution was the most intense and destructive theatre of warfare to visit Ireland between the Cromwellian conquest and the 1798 rebellion. As Robert Armstrong writes in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], the conflict brought "the true scope and horror of contemporary European warfare" to Ireland: it was simultaneously a dynastic war between two claimants to three thrones, a confessional conflict between Catholic and Protestant political orders, and a European great-power struggle between Louis XIV of France and the League of Augsburg.

### Origins: James II's Irish Programme (1685–1688)

James II succeeded to the Irish, English, and Scottish thrones in February 1685. His programme of "Catholicization" — extending civil and military office to Catholics — differed in degree across the three kingdoms but was most thoroughgoing in Ireland. Richard Talbot, created Earl of Tyrconnell in 1685 and appointed Lord Deputy in November 1686, systematically replaced Protestant army officers with Catholics, purged Protestant sheriffs and justices of the peace, recalled borough charters, and reissued them with Catholic-majority appointees. By 1688 the Irish army was predominantly Catholic.

The strategic import of Tyrconnell's changes was profound: where Protestants had "far more soldiers and soldier-like men" before 1685, disarming the Protestant militia and arming Catholic recruits transferred the capacity for organised violence. As Armstrong notes, Irish-language poetry confirms that "it was the disarming of the Protestants and the recruitment of Catholic troops which gave the greatest satisfaction."

Tyrconnell and his associate Richard Nagle pushed for repeal of the Cromwellian land settlement, arguing that nothing could sustain Catholicism in Ireland "but to make Catholicks there considerable in their fortunes as they are considerable in their number." James resisted wholesale reversal, but his delay on land questions alienated Catholic supporters without reassuring Protestants.

### Protestant Mobilisation (December 1688–March 1689)

On 7 December 1688 — a month after William of Orange's English landing — a body of Protestant apprentice boys shut the gates of Derry against an approaching royal garrison, initiating what would become a months-long siege. Similar armed Protestant associations formed at Enniskillen and in Sligo and Cork. Protestant resistance was initially framed in paradoxical terms: resisting the king's Catholic deputies while claiming loyalty to James himself. As Armstrong observes, this mirrors the "Anglican revolution" in England, which sought to remove James's ministers rather than James himself, before hardening into full-scale Williamite allegiance.

James arrived in Ireland in March 1689, welcomed by enthusiastic Catholic crowds as his army engaged the besieged Protestant population of Derry.

### The Patriot Parliament (May–July 1689)

James summoned an Irish parliament which assembled on 7 May 1689 — the first since 1666. Its composition was overwhelmingly Catholic: only six Protestants were returned to the Commons. Despite its later reputation as a distraction from the military effort, the parliament was constitutionally active, passing approximately thirty-five pieces of legislation:

- **Act of Recognition**: Proclaimed James's "just and most undoubted right" in terms drawn from indefeasible hereditary monarchy.
- **Act declaring the English Parliament cannot bind Ireland**: Repudiated Westminster legislative authority over the Irish kingdom — a constitutional statement that would echo through the eighteenth century.
- **Act of Attainder**: Condemned over 2,000 named individuals (principally Protestant landowners who had fled) as traitors, providing the legal mechanism for reversing the land settlement.
- **Act for Liberty of Conscience**: Enacted James's preferred policy of religious toleration, maintaining the Protestant establishment while guaranteeing Catholic free practice and ordering payment of tithes only to clergy of one's own persuasion.

The parliament *did not* repeal Poynings' Law (James refused), nor did it secure the wholesale land reversal many deputies wanted. James feared alienating the Catholic "new interest" — proprietors who had purchased land under the 1660s settlement — and his need for English and Scottish support constrained his Irish concessions.

### Military Campaign

**July 1689**: Relief of Derry by sea — described by J.G. Simms as "a near-run thing" — ended the siege and defeated the first Jacobite offensive. Jacobite defeat at Newtownbutler followed.

**August 1689**: Marshal Schomberg landed with approximately 20,000 English and allied troops at Bangor, nearly collapsing the Jacobite position in Ulster. Schomberg's subsequent advance stalled at Dundalk through logistical failures, disease, and poor weather; perhaps half his army was lost to sickness without major battle.

**1 July 1690 (Battle of the Boyne)**: Approximately 60,000 troops on both sides assembled on the River Boyne. William was struck by a cannon shot but survived ("a difference of inches separated a superficial wound from the death of the leader of an international coalition"). William's army — which included 13,000 Dutch troops, 7,000 Danes, and experienced French Huguenot officers — defeated the Jacobites in what was not an "overwhelming victory" (Simms), as the defeated forces escaped largely intact. James fled to France.

The Boyne's real significance was political: it delivered Dublin and gave the Williamites control of the east. Yet the war continued for fifteen more months.

**Battle of Aughrim (12 July 1691)**: The decisive engagement. St Ruth, the French commander, was killed by a cannon shot on the field. An estimated 7,000 Jacobites died — compared to perhaps 1,000 at the Boyne — as cavalry failures and command collapse turned battle into rout. Galway surrendered soon after.

**Siege of Limerick (1690–1691)**: Limerick was held twice against Williamite assault. Its fortifications, heavily strengthened during 1690–1, exemplified "advanced techniques of fortification and siegecraft" that made the city strategically vital. William's commander Ginkell entered the 1691 campaign under pressure to end the war quickly, taking Athlone (12,000 cannonballs in ten days) and breaking the Shannon line before Aughrim.

**French Intervention**: Eight significant French convoys supplied the Jacobite cause in 1689–91. The only major naval engagement at Bantry Bay (May 1689) was indecisive. France's failure to fully exploit its period of naval superiority (notably the Anglo-Dutch defeat at Beachy Head, the very day William was wounded at the Boyne) has been debated by historians, attributed partly to Louis XIV's land-war priorities and limited understanding of naval strategy.

### Treaty of Limerick (3 October 1691)

Negotiations were conducted hastily. Ginkell admitted he could not take Limerick by assault with the campaigning season ending. The final terms included:

- **Civil articles**: Catholics to enjoy such religious privileges as they had under Charles II (Article One, later stripped out by the Dublin parliament in 1697); all those who had borne arms and their dependants in five western counties to retain property and follow their professions on swearing allegiance to William and Mary (Article Two); general amnesty for wartime actions (Article Six).
- **Military articles**: The Jacobite army (up to 19,000, in what became known as the "Flight of the Wild Geese") could transfer to French service; Ginkell even offered shipping.

Patrick Kelly identifies the Treaty as "the central document of the revolution in Ireland" — since the Irish revolution produced no Declaration of Rights or Claim of Right equivalent. Its reputation as a "broken treaty" began immediately: the "missing clause" of Article Two (extending terms to civilians in Jacobite quarters, dropped in scribal error and restored by William but deliberately removed by the 1697 Dublin parliament) and the subsequent Penal Laws enacted from 1695 onwards made the treaty's Catholic provisions effectively null.

## Causal Analysis

The war was caused_by: [[restoration-ireland-1660-1688]] and specifically by Tyrconnell's acceleration of Catholic political power under James II, which triggered Protestant armed resistance and invited William's intervention. It was simultaneously an episode in the War of the League of Augsburg (concurrent_with): Louis XIV's support for James was "the principled upholding of a lawful monarch" but also a strategic bid to divert British military power from the continental war. The war was enabled_by: the Irish Catholic exile community on the Continent, which supplied trained officers and officers' networks for Jacobite recruitment.

## Consequence Analysis

The war produced: [[protestant-ascendancy]] in its definitive eighteenth-century form. Catholic landownership, which had partially recovered during the Restoration to perhaps one-third of profitable land, was permanently consolidated at that or lower levels. The Penal Laws (1695–1709), enacted by the Dublin Protestant parliament over William's personal objections, created the "confessional state" that defined Irish society until Catholic Emancipation (1829).

The Flight of the Wild Geese — c.19,000 soldiers entering French service — maintained Irish Catholic military presence in Europe and Irish exile political networks for two further generations, sustaining Jacobite hopes until Culloden (1746) effectively ended them.

The war also enabled: the transformation of the Irish parliament into a permanent biennial institution, as described under [[early-hanoverian-ireland-1690-1750]], since the Williamite government's chronic financial dependence on parliamentary supply forced regular sessions and gave Protestant Irish politicians leverage they had not previously held.

## Historiography

J.G. Simms (*Jacobite Ireland*, 1969; *War and Politics in Ireland 1649–1730*, 1986) established the modern scholarly framework. Subsequent military histories by Piers Wauchope (*Patrick Sarsfield and the Williamite War*, 1992), Richard Doherty (*The Williamite War in Ireland*, 1998), Pádraig Lenihan (*1690: Battle of the Boyne*, 2003), and John Childs (*The Williamite Wars in Ireland*, 2007) are all primarily military narratives. Armstrong notes the relative neglect of economic, social, and religious dimensions of the period.

The term "revolution" for 1688–91 is contested in the Irish context: "some historians have denied the applicability of the term altogether; others have seen a multiplicity of revolutions tumbling one after another." The Patriot Parliament of 1689 attracted renewed attention in the nineteenth century through Thomas Davis's *The Patriot Parliament of 1689*, published posthumously under that title, reflecting its later nationalist significance.
