## Narrative

In 945, the Buyid (Buwayhid) military commander Ahmad ibn Buya entered Baghdad and took direct control of the Abbasid capital, which had been wracked for decades by factional military strife and the progressive erosion of caliphal authority over the wider Islamic world. The reigning Abbasid caliph, al-Mustakfi, was compelled to grant Ahmad ibn Buya the title *Mu'izz al-Dawla* ("Fortifier of the State") and was deposed and blinded shortly afterward in favor of a more pliable successor. From this point the Abbasid caliphs retained their religious and symbolic primacy as heads of Sunni Islam, but real political and military power in Iraq passed to the Buyid amirs, a Zaydi/Twelver Shia dynasty from the Daylam region of northern Iran, who ruled "in the name of" a series of largely powerless caliphs for more than a century.

## Causal Analysis

The conquest was `enabled` by decades of prior fragmentation of Abbasid central authority among rival regional military strongmen (Turkish, Daylamite, and other commanders), and was `contributed_to` by the Buyids' prior consolidation of a strong regional power base in western Iran and Khuzistan.

## Consequence Analysis

The event `produced` a lasting structural separation between religious and political authority in the central Islamic lands — caliphal legitimacy without caliphal power — and `enabled` the entrenchment of Shia political dominance (the Buyids) over a Sunni-headed caliphate, a configuration that shaped subsequent Islamic political theory on the relationship between the caliphate and military rulers (later formalized under the sultanate model with the Seljuks).

## Historiography

The Buyid takeover is a standard case study in Islamic historiography and political theory for the divergence of religious and temporal authority; sources are predominantly later Arabic chronicle traditions (Miskawayh, Ibn al-Athir), and modern scholarship (e.g., work synthesized in the Cambridge History of Iran and Cambridge History of Islam) treats the Buyid period as a formative precedent for the later, more famous separation of caliph and sultan under the Seljuks.
