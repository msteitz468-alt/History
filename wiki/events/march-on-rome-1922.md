# The March on Rome (27–31 October 1922)

The event through which Mussolini became prime minister of Italy — and,
per Paxton ([[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004]]), the most successful
propaganda myth in fascist history: "The 'March on Rome' was a gigantic
bluff that worked, and still works in the general public's perceptions
of Mussolini's 'seizure of power.'"

## Narrative

Through 1922 the squadristi escalated from raids to open supplantation of
the state: Fiume retaken 3 March; Ferrara and Bologna assaulted in May,
their socialist city governments driven out; Cremona occupied 12 July; a
"column of fire" through the Romagna reached Ravenna 26 July; Trent and
Bolzano "Italianized" in early October. Italy had had no effective
government since February — Luigi Facta's caretaker cabinet had lost its
majority on 19 July. A socialist general strike of 31 July (the "strike
for legality") collapsed quickly, "inflating Mussolini's appeal as a
bulwark against revolution."

At the Fascist Congress in Naples (24 October) Mussolini ordered the
Blackshirts to seize public buildings, commandeer trains, and converge on
Rome under a quadrumvirate representing Fascism's strands: Italo Balbo
(squadrist boss of Ferrara), General Emilio De Bono, Michele Bianchi
(ex-syndicalist), and the monarchist Cesare Maria De Vecchi. Mussolini
himself stayed in his Milan newspaper offices, "not far from a possible
Swiss refuge in case things went wrong."

The march itself was militarily contained. Facta, with the king's
approval, reinforced the Rome garrison with five Alpine battalions and
ordered the trains stopped: about 400 police halted trains carrying some
20,000 Blackshirts at Civita Vecchia, Orte, and Avezzano. The roughly
9,000 who got through formed "a motley crowd at the gates of Rome" on the
morning of 28 October — "poorly armed, wearing makeshift uniforms, short
of food and water, and milling about in a discouraging rain." A
contemporary judgment Paxton quotes: "In ancient and modern history,
there was hardly any attempt on Rome that failed so miserably at its
beginning."

The decision was made elsewhere. King **Victor Emmanuel III** refused at
the last moment to sign Facta's martial-law decree, declined Salandra's
attempt at a conservative government without Mussolini, and offered
Mussolini the prime ministry directly. Mussolini arrived in Rome on
30 October **by railway sleeping car**, calling on the king in morning
coat and black shirt: "Sire, forgive my attire, I come from the
battlefields" — said, Paxton notes, "mendaciously." Only on 31 October,
with Mussolini already in office, were the Blackshirts — now fed and
given dry clothes — granted a "compensatory parade" through Rome (which
still produced 7 deaths and 17 injuries) before being sent home in fifty
special trains.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the deadlock of the liberal state — the November 1919
  election had produced an unbridgeable PSI–Popolari majority; Giolitti's
  inclusion of the Fascists on his 1921 "National Bloc" list was "the
  first of several crucial steps by which the Italian Establishment tried
  to coopt Fascist energy and numbers for their own survival."
- `caused_by:` the king's refusal to use available force. Paxton's best
  reconstruction (no archival trace survives): a private warning —
  probably from Marshal Armando Diaz — that troops might fraternize with
  the Blackshirts; alternatively fear that his Fascist-sympathizing
  cousin, the duke of Aosta, might claim the throne. "It was not
  Fascism's force that decided the issue, but the conservatives'
  unwillingness to risk their force against his."
- `enabled:` by two years of squadrist violence tolerated by police,
  army, and prefects — Fascist Italy was "more violent than Nazism on its
  way to power" (plausible estimates: 500–600 Fascist and ~2,000
  anti-Fascist dead, 1920–22).
- `contributed_to:` (as myth) the German Left's fatal passivity in
  1932–33 — both SPD and KPD braced for a Nazi *coup* that never came,
  because the Italian precedent had taught them to expect one.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Mussolini's premiership — followed by two years of
  quasi-parliamentary rule, the Acerbo law (1923), the April 1924
  election, the Matteotti murder, the militia consuls' ultimatum of
  31 December 1924, and the 3 January 1925 speech opening the
  dictatorship. "By early 1927 Italy had become a one-party dictatorship."
- `produced:` the founding liturgy of the regime: 28 October became a
  national holiday and (from 1927) the first day of the Fascist calendar;
  the 1932 Mostra della Rivoluzione Fascista enshrined the march's
  "martyrs."
- `enabled:` the template misreading — "Even the most scrupulous authors
  refer to their 'seizure of power,' but that phrase better describes
  what the two fascist leaders did after reaching office than how they
  got into office" ([[generic-fascism]]).

## Paxton & Hessler (2011): mechanics and verdict

Paxton and Hessler's textbook account corroborates the demythologized
reading with its own emphasis on the political theater: at the Fascist
congress in Naples (October 1922) Mussolini organized the march under a
quadrumvirate — Balbo (representing the veterans), Bianchi (the
syndicalist left), De Bono (the army), and De Vecchi (the monarchists) —
who set the march for the night of 27–28 October. Police stopped the
squadristi trains before they reached the capital, and only around 9,000
poorly armed marchers actually reached Rome, in the rain, badly
outnumbered by the troops already stationed inside the city. Facta
prepared a martial-law decree, but Victor Emmanuel III refused to
countersign it; Giolitti and Salandra each tried to co-opt Mussolini into a
coalition government, but he refused anything short of the premiership
itself, arriving in Rome by sleeping car on the morning of 30 October — the
"march" proper was staged for the cameras only on 31 October, after he was
already in office. Paxton and Hessler's verdict: the march was
"constitutional in form, force in substance" — Mussolini "made normal
government impossible until the political leaders bought him off." Source:
[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## Payne's account (1995)

[[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne]] independently corroborates and
sharpens the demythologized reading: "There was not the slightest danger of
a violent Fascist coup against the government itself" — no plan existed, the
army and police outnumbered the ~26,000 assembling Blackshirts, and General
Pugliese stood ready to defend Rome on royal order. "The March on Rome had
been a sort of pronunciamiento, or political imposition, but not a violent
coup d'état... Mussolini came to power legally, heading a normal multiparty
parliamentary coalition" — only three of his thirteen other cabinet members
were Fascists. Payne's squadrismo figures frame the run-up: Fascist records
show 463 Fascists killed 1919–22, Salvemini estimated ~900 Socialists killed
by Fascists, total political-violence deaths approaching 2,000; membership
had exploded from 20,000 (end of 1920) to 187,588 (May 1921) as the movement
became a rural mass force under the provincial *ras*. The dictatorship
followed only after the Matteotti crisis, in the 3 January 1925 speech ("I,
and I alone, assume the political, moral, and historical responsibility for
all that has happened") and the 1925–28 *leggi fascistissime* — of which,
Payne notes, only the 1928 elevation of the Grand Council directly violated
the constitution. The Grand Council mechanism thus preserved would depose
Mussolini himself on 24–25 July 1943 (Grandi's motion, 19–7).

## Sternhell's account (1994)

[[sternhell-birth-fascist-ideology-1994|Sternhell/Asheri]] agree the march
"was not the result of a coup d'état but the outcome of a political process
lasting several months," but shift the explanation from conservative
calculation to ideological penetration: the authorities' collapse is
explained "neither by the weakness of the state... nor by Mussolini's
ability to maneuver" but by the "comprehension and even sympathy" — the
ideological "impregnation" — of politicians, intellectuals, and elites.
Squadrist violence is correspondingly downplayed as the operative cause. On
the eve of the march, Camillo Pellizzi's *Gerarchia* article could already
state the full doctrine ("fascism is the affirmation of the principles of
tradition, hierarchy, authority, and individual sacrifice"): "The philosophy
of fascism was thus fully elaborated even before the movement came to
power." The two later near-collapses (Giolitti's blocked anti-Fascist
coalition, 1922; the Matteotti crisis, defused by Croce's Senate vote of
confidence, 26 June 1924) are read the same way. See [[sternhell-thesis]].

## Historiography

The demythologized account is now standard (Lyttelton, *The Seizure of
Power*, is Paxton's "most penetrating analysis in any language"; Tasca's
1938 ex-socialist account remains valuable). The open question is the
king's motive, on which no document survives — Diaz-warning vs.
Aosta-rivalry theories both circulate. The event anchors the wider
historiographical point that fascism reached power everywhere through
conservative invitation, never through insurrection
([[fascism-and-the-political-left]] Position 1's central evidence;
[[generic-fascism]]). The page now rests on Paxton (2004) and Payne (1995),
which agree on all essentials.

## Griffin (2007): Year I of a New Temporality

*Modernism and Fascism* adds the temporal reading: the regime made 1922 "Year I of the Fascist Era," a parallel calendar on the French Revolutionary model — "a profoundly mythic will to create a new type of state... in which chronos will be suspended and historical time will literally be made anew." The March's success turned, per Griffin's Paxton reading, on "a nomic crisis of national proportions" convincing Victor Emmanuel III to appoint Mussolini, with a second constellation after the Matteotti Crisis (late 1924) legitimizing the totalitarian turn. Five months after the March, the Nationalist Association merged with the PNF, bringing Rocco, Federzoni, and Corradini alongside ex-syndicalists Panunzio, Lanzillo, and Orano — Rocco justifying the corporatist state via Vico's *corsi e ricorsi*. The 1932 decennial Mostra della Rivoluzione Fascista (≈4 million visitors) ritualized the March as the founding Aufbruch — Schnapp: "a rally of the living dead... where history's victims are forever present to each other." (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
