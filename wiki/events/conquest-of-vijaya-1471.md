## Narrative

After a Cham army besieged the Vietnamese garrison at Hue in 1470, [[le-thanh-tong|Le Thanh Tong]] personally organized and led a large-scale punitive expedition, publishing detailed campaign orders and a vernacular manual of drill and discipline used to train the army en route — the campaign doubled as an extended training exercise for his forces. Rendezvousing with his army in Nghe An in early 1471, he was dismayed to find undisciplined "crowds" rather than a trained force and spent weeks imposing strict drill before advancing.

Moving through modern Quang Nam, Le Thanh Tong devised a pincer plan: his main force advanced down the coast while his right wing moved through the mountains to lay an ambush and his left wing landed by sea behind the Cham army. The Cham force was surrounded and scattered. Le Thanh Tong pursued and captured the Cham king at his capital, Vijaya (in modern Binh Dinh Province). A Cham general fled south and established a rump successor state at modern Phan Rang, more than 250 kilometers further south; rulers in the Central Highlands and along the coast immediately south of Binh Dinh submitted to Le Thanh Tong as vassals. The captured Cham king, embarked with his numerous wives (requiring an additional boat) for transport to Dong Kinh, reportedly died of illness en route.

The conquered territory — modern Quang Nam, Quang Ngai, and Binh Dinh — was annexed and organized as Quang Nam Province, garrisoned and staffed with newly appointed officials. In late 1471, the Le court selectively populated the new province with marginal and undesirable groups: descendants of Ming collaborators, unreliable upland chieftains, unsatisfactory royal slaves, criminals, resident Chams and Laotians, and people of mixed Ming-local ancestry — alongside officials being punished or reassigned for poor performance elsewhere (e.g., coastal officials who failed to repair typhoon-damaged dikes). Many Cham people remained in the newly annexed territory and adopted Vietnamese names; an edict in 1472 restricted such names to three characters, and officials were separately warned against illegally concealing enslaved Chams.

## Causal Analysis

The campaign was `caused_by` the immediate provocation of the 1470 Cham siege of Hue, but `contributed_to` by a much longer-running structural conflict dating back to Ho Quy Ly's initial 1402 annexation of the same territory (later lost back to Champa during the Ming interregnum) and the failure of the 1446 Le expedition to establish a durable vassal relationship. It `enabled` the permanent fragmentation of Champa into weaker rump polities that never again threatened Dai Viet as a unified power.

## Consequence Analysis

This campaign is a central episode — arguably the decisive one — in [[nam-tien|Nam Tien]], the long Vietnamese southward expansion: it converted Champa from a rival kingdom capable of besieging Hue into a set of fragmented, vassal, and increasingly marginal successor states. It `produced` the new administrative unit of Quang Nam Province and set the template (deliberate settlement of undesirables and demoted officials as frontier pioneers) that would recur in later phases of southward expansion. It also `contributed_to` Le Thanh Tong's broader domestic project of channeling the martial energy of the Thanh Hoa elite into frontier campaigns rather than court factionalism.

## Historiography

Taylor draws on Le-dynasty court annals for the campaign's operational detail (Le Thanh Tong's personal drilling of troops, his vernacular training manual, the pincer maneuver) and treats this material as more reliably contemporary than earlier reigns' hagiographic material, given the density of surviving edicts from Le Thanh Tong's own reign. The event is conventionally treated in Vietnamese national historiography as the decisive blow that "ended" Champa; Taylor's account supports this in territorial-political terms while noting that smaller Cham successor polities persisted for generations afterward at Phan Rang and elsewhere.
