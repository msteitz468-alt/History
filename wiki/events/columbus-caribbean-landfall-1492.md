On the morning of 12 October 1492 (23 October in the Gregorian calendar), Christopher Columbus went ashore on the island the indigenous Lucayans called Guanahaní, which he renamed San Salvador ("Holy Savior"). This landing, the culmination of his westward Atlantic crossing in search of Asia, initiated the first sustained contact between Europeans and the peoples of the Americas and triggered consequences of hemispheric and global scale.

## Narrative

Columbus's fleet of three ships — Niña, Pinta, and Santa María — had departed from Palos de la Frontera on 3 August 1492 under the patronage of the Spanish Crown (Ferdinand and Isabella, though the political entity of "Spain" as such barely existed). After a crossing via the Canary Islands, Columbus made landfall at an island in the Bahamas on 12 October.

Columbus kept a running journal (diario) of his first voyage. The original was lost; the surviving version is a copy transcribed by Bartolomé de las Casas around 1530. The Las Casas manuscript consists of seventy-six large-sized paper folios in small cursive shorthand. The most complete modern scholarly edition is Oliver Dunn and James E. Kelley Jr.'s 1989 exact transcription and translation. The diario is unusual for its time in including descriptions of local inhabitants and environments — ships' logs typically recorded only navigational data.

Upon landing, Columbus took possession of the island for the Spanish Crown and encountered the indigenous Lucayan population. He noted that they were of good stature, went naked, were welcoming, and brought gifts including cotton thread and parrots. He observed that some bore marks of wounds, which the Lucayans explained as inflicted by people from neighboring islands who came to take them captive — an observation Columbus immediately interpreted through his preconception that he was near the territory of the Grand Khan of China, whose army he assumed was raiding these "simple" people.

Columbus renamed the island San Salvador. It is known by that name today, and a substantial number of European artifacts — pottery, metals, and glass beads — recovered from archaeological excavations at the Long Bay site are indicative of a Spanish visit that may date to this encounter, though firm attribution is complicated by the possibility of later Vespucci expedition contacts.

Twelve other islands, and even Florida, have been proposed as the site of the first landfall. The sailing directions and island descriptions in the diario do not strongly support other candidates (Keegan 1992), and San Salvador (Long Bay) remains the most plausible identification.

## Causal Analysis

**Primary causes:**

- *preceded_by*: [[columbian-exchange-1492]] — the decade of prior European Atlantic navigation (Portuguese southward along Africa, Columbus's theory of a westward route to Asia)
- *caused_by*: Columbus's conviction, drawn from his reading of Ptolemy, Marco Polo, and Pierre d'Ailly, that Asia could be reached by sailing west — a conviction based on his systematic underestimation of the circumference of the Earth and overestimation of Asia's eastward extent
- *contributed_to*: Spanish Crown patronage motivated by competition with Portugal and the desire for direct access to Asian spice and luxury trade after the Ottoman Empire disrupted overland routes

**Secondary causes:**

- The timing of the voyage in late summer 1492 allowed Columbus to exploit the northeast trade winds for a westward passage — an atmospheric route he had observed on his earlier voyages to the Canary Islands and West Africa.
- The expulsion of Moors from Granada (January 1492) and the political consolidation of Castile and Aragon freed resources and royal attention for overseas patronage.

## Consequence Analysis

The landfall at Guanahaní initiated a cascading sequence of consequences:

1. **Immediate:** Columbus continued through the Bahamas, Cuba, and Hispaniola over the following months, encountering the Lucayan, Cuban, and Hispaniolan peoples; collecting gold specimens and indigenous captives; and constructing a mental geography that identified these islands as Asian lands near the Grand Khan's territory.

2. **Short-term:** The Golfo de las Flechas incident (Samaná Bay, Dominican Republic) — when Columbus was attacked near the end of his first voyage — he labeled the aggressors "Carib," initiating the cannibal myth that would serve to justify indigenous enslavement. Columbus's reports prompted a second voyage (1493) with 1,500 men, the establishment of colonies, and the beginning of systematic exploitation.

3. **Long-term:** The landfall activated what became known as the [[columbian-exchange-1492]] — the hemispheric transfer of peoples, diseases, animals, and plants — and triggered the [[indigenous-caribbean-demographic-collapse]] that virtually destroyed Lucayan society within a generation and devastated Greater Antillean populations within decades.

4. *enabled*: [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]] and subsequent conquest of the Americas.

## Actors

**Columbus:** At the time of the landfall, Columbus was 41 years old, a Genoese-born mariner working under Castilian patronage. He had been promised the title "Admiral of the Ocean Sea" and 10% of the profits from all voyages to the lands he reached, plus 25% of profits from expeditions he directed. He was convinced throughout his first voyage that he was approaching China and the Grand Khan's territory. He did not understand the languages of any peoples he encountered, and multiple passages in the diario acknowledge this directly ("Also I do not know the language, and the people of these islands do not understand me nor do I, nor anyone else I have with me, them" — Dunn and Kelley 1989:183).

Columbus's interpretive framework was medieval European: he expected the Grand Khan to have a standing army, metal weapons, and cities; the "simple," unarmored peoples he encountered in the Bahamas and Cuba were therefore, to his mind, not the Grand Khan's subjects but rather peoples oppressed by the Khan's soldiers — whose raiding for slaves he identified behind every wound and every indigenous warning about "Caribes."

**Lucayans:** The indigenous inhabitants of the Bahamas were descended from Taíno-related peoples who had colonized the Bahamas from Hispaniola beginning around AD 700. See [[lucayans]] and [[lucayan-colonisation-bahamas-700ce]]. They initially received Columbus with gifts and curiosity; multiple passages in the diario describe friendly exchanges of goods. Columbus noted their capacity for conversion and labor from the first day: "they could be subjected and made to do whatever one might wish" (12 October 1492; Dunn and Kelley 1989:65).

## The Diario, Canibals, and the Construction of "Carib"

The diario is the founding document of the Carib controversy (see [[kalinago-origins-and-carib-identity]]). The word "Carib" (in variants: Caribes, Caniba, Canima, Cannibales) appears ten times in four distinct contexts, all of which Keegan (1996a, 2007) identifies as unreliable evidence for an actual indigenous people called Carib:

1. **Caniba** = the subjects (gente) of the Grand Khan, identified by Columbus with the Mongol military.
2. **Caribes** = mythological beings in Lucayan cosmology that came from the otherworld to consume the dead and bring them to final rest.
3. **Carib Island** = a mythical all-male island in the indigenous insular cosmological triad (Carib:Guanín:Matininó).
4. **Cannibales** = Columbus's general term for the enemies of the peoples he encountered in the Bahamas, Cuba, and Hispaniola.

The most significant textual evidence for the revisionist position is that when Columbus sailed east toward what indigenous captives told him was the territory of the "Carib," the Lucayans on his ship became frightened — because Columbus was sailing east, while he believed the Caniba (Grand Khan) lay to the *west*. The geographic contradiction reveals that two entirely different referents were being conflated. As Keegan argues, Columbus was confused by indigenous descriptions of cosmological beings (the Caribes to the east in indigenous mythology) and superimposed onto them his preexisting expectation of Asian political geography.

Friar Ramón Pané, writing c. 1496–1497 in Macorix territory on Hispaniola, confirmed that the indigenous peoples initially identified the Spanish themselves as Cannibales — which makes sense if the Cannibales/Caribes were mythological beings rather than known neighboring peoples.

## Historiography

Columbus's diario has generated an enormous scholarly literature. The core problem is that the original is lost and the Las Casas transcription is itself a secondary document with demonstrable anachronisms and interpolations, identified by David Henige (1991) and Keegan (2007). The diario cannot be treated as a straightforward primary source.

**First-landfall debate:** Beginning with Washington Irving's biography of Columbus (1828), dozens of scholars have proposed different first-landfall sites. Thirteen islands and Florida have been seriously proposed. The historiographical consensus since the 1980s has shifted back toward San Salvador (Watlings Island), supported by computer modelling of the sailing directions (National Geographic, 1986), though the debate has never been definitively resolved. Keegan (1992) provides the most comprehensive defence of the San Salvador identification.

**The cannibal myth:** The term "cannibal" derives directly from "Canibales" in Columbus's diario. Peter Hulme's *Colonial Encounters* (1986) provided the foundational postcolonial analysis demonstrating that the cannibal label served specific colonial ideological functions — particularly the legal justification for enslaving "Carib" peoples under a papal bull permitting the enslavement of cannibals. The critical literature (Hulme 1986, Whitehead 1984, Keegan 1996a, 2007) has established broad scholarly consensus that the cannibal attribution tells us more about European colonial categories than about indigenous practice.

**Columbus's self-promotion:** Columbus's son Ferdinand promoted his father's discoveries to maintain family concessions from the Spanish Crown. Columbus had been promised 10% of profits from all American voyages and governance titles. He reportedly died in poverty on 20 May 1506 after the Crown stripped the family of most concessions. Carl Sauer (1966:29) quoted the Spanish humanist Ramón Iglesia describing Columbus's diario as "the promotion literature of a tourist board, which he did with Italian exuberance."
