## Narrative

The Locarno Agreements, concluded on October 16, 1925 at the Swiss resort of
Locarno on Lake Maggiore, opened what Paxton calls the "years of
conciliation" (1924–1929) following the "years of coercion" (1919–1924) that
culminated in the French occupation of the Ruhr — see
[[ruhr-occupation-crisis-1923]]. Mutual exhaustion drove the shift: France had
discovered it could not coerce Germany alone, and Germany had learned that
passive resistance was self-destructive to its own currency and economy.

Political change enabled the settlement. British and French voters
repudiated the wartime hard-liners at the turn of 1924: Ramsay MacDonald
became Britain's first Labour prime minister, and a new center-left French
leadership under Premier Édouard Herriot and Foreign Minister Aristide
Briand took office. Briand, who directed French foreign policy from April
1925 to his death in January 1932, came to personify the conciliation era as
Poincaré had personified coercion — though Paxton notes that "on closer
inspection, Briand actually conceded very little in private negotiation"
because he needed to placate a nationalist parliamentary majority.

Deadlock loosened once German Foreign Minister Gustav Stresemann proposed a
mutual Franco-German guarantee of the Rhine frontier. Briand and British
Conservative Foreign Secretary Austen Chamberlain welcomed the proposal, and
the parties met at Locarno in October 1925. The core provision was a
Franco-German pledge to maintain the frontier set by the Treaty of Versailles
(see [[treaty-of-versailles-1919]]); Britain and Italy promised to intervene
if either France or Germany crossed that frontier by force, or if Germany
remilitarized the Rhineland. Crucially, Germany's *eastern* frontiers were
left unguaranteed — Germany subsequently signed only arbitration (not
territorial-guarantee) treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia, prompting
France to conclude more binding mutual-assistance treaties with the Little
Entente states (Czechoslovakia, Romania, Yugoslavia) as a hedge.

Each party gained and yielded something. Germany renounced forcible recovery
of Alsace-Lorraine or unilateral Rhineland remilitarization — acts beyond its
military capacity in any case — in exchange for separation of the eastern
frontier question from the western, French support for German entry into the
League of Nations (achieved 1926), and an accelerated Allied Rhineland
withdrawal (completed in 1930 rather than the scheduled 1935). France
renounced armed intervention in Germany (its 1920–1923 efforts having proven
counterproductive) and accepted Germany as an equal diplomatic partner.
Britain ended its post-1919 isolation by extending a continental guarantee —
but a symmetrical one, applying equally against French as against German
aggression, so as not to alarm a British public wary of being "dragged into a
war by France." This symmetry dissolved the French hope (pursued in vain by
both Briand and Poincaré) of a one-sided British defense guarantee.

Contemporary reaction was euphoric: Austen Chamberlain and Briand reportedly
wept with joy at the signing; Mussolini kissed Mrs. Chamberlain's hand; crowds
danced in the square; the *New York Times* headline read "France and Germany
Ban War Forever" and the *London Times*, "Peace at Last" (Paxton, quoting Jon
Jacobson, *Locarno Diplomacy: Germany and the West, 1925–1929* (1972), p. 3).
Stresemann and Briand jointly received the 1926 Nobel Peace Prize — "for that
spirit," Paxton writes, "rather than for the agreements' concrete terms." The
same optimism produced the Kellogg-Briand Pact of August 27, 1928, in which
the major powers, the United States, and Japan renounced "war as an
instrument of national policy," with no enforcement mechanism attached.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by`: the mutual exhaustion following the Ruhr crisis of 1923 (see
  [[ruhr-occupation-crisis-1923]]) — French inability to coerce Germany
  unilaterally, and German recognition that passive resistance had been
  ruinously self-destructive.
- `contributed_to`: the 1924 electoral defeats of hardline governments in both
  Britain (Conservative loss to Labour) and France (defeat of the
  "horizon-blue Chamber" bloc), which brought in leadership disposed toward
  conciliation.
- `caused_by`: Stresemann's direct proposal of a mutual Rhine guarantee,
  which Paxton frames within Stresemann's broader — and, per his private
  papers, more cynical than his public reputation suggests — strategy of
  dismantling the Versailles system by stages ("First we must get the
  throttler from our throat"; then pursue eastern revision by *finassieren*,
  "being crafty").

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: German admission to the League of Nations (1926); early
  completion of Allied Rhineland withdrawal (1930 instead of 1935).
- `enabled`: the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) and the broader "new diplomacy"
  optimism of the later 1920s.
- `contributed_to`: a false sense of durable Franco-German settlement that
  masked the underlying disparity between 60 million Germans and 40 million
  French, and did nothing to resolve Germany's unguaranteed and (per
  Stresemann's private intentions) still-contested eastern frontiers.
- `preceded_by` (not caused by): the failure of the World Disarmament
  Conference (1932–1933) and the collapse of the Young Plan settlement (1929)
  — Locarno's conciliatory spirit did not survive into the 1930s, and its
  optimism proved, in Paxton's assessment, misplaced.

## Historiography

Paxton is explicit that Stresemann's reputation as an internationalist
conciliator required revision once his private papers became available after
World War II: a September 7, 1925 secret memorandum to the exiled Prussian
crown prince laid out a staged timetable for dismantling Versailles,
including recovery of Danzig, the Polish Corridor, parts of Upper Silesia,
revision of the Czechoslovak frontier, and eventual Anschluss with Austria —
aims Paxton stresses must nonetheless be sharply distinguished from Hitler's
by their exclusively German-speaking scope, absence of overt racist doctrine,
and lack of any parallel intent to display force. Key source: Hans W. Gatzke,
*Stresemann and the Rearmament of Germany* (1954). General surveys cited:
Sally Marks, *The Illusion of Peace: Europe's International Relations,
1918–1933* (2nd ed., 2003); Jon Jacobson, *Locarno Diplomacy* (1972).
