# The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)

The treaty signed on **30 January 1902** in London between [[japan|Japan]] and the British Empire —
the first alliance between a European great power and a non-Western state on equal terms, and the
strategic enabler of Japan's victory in the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]]. From
Donald Keene's [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002|*Emperor of Japan: Meiji and His World*]]
(chapters 49–51), which provides the fullest account of deliberations on both sides.

## Narrative

The origins of the alliance lay in the aftermath of the [[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Triple
Intervention (1895)]]: by forcing Japan to disgorge the Liaodong Peninsula and then leasing it
themselves (Port Arthur and Dairen), Russia had made itself Japan's primary rival. The Russians
also signed a **secret treaty with China (1896)** during the coronation of Nicholas II — concluded
by Foreign Minister Lobanov-Rostovskii with Li Hung-chang — providing for mutual defence against
Japan and authorising a Russian railway through Manchuria to Vladivostok. By the late 1890s Japan
faced a steadily expanding Russian presence it could not oppose unaided.

Japan had two strategic options:

1. **The Itō option**: Concede Manchuria to Russia in exchange for Russian recognition of
   Japan's primacy in Korea (a "Manchuria for Korea" swap).
2. **The alliance option**: Join with a major European power to contain Russian expansion.
   France (allied to Russia) was unavailable; Germany was unpredictable; Britain — with its
   own concerns about Russia in China — was the natural partner.

A **pro-Japanese mood in Britain** after Japanese troops rescued British legation personnel
besieged by Boxers in 1900 created the opening. Hayashi Tadasu, appointed Japanese minister to
London in 1900, concluded that Britain was the only viable ally against Russia. In April 1901
he voiced this view to Lord Lansdowne, who agreed in principle. Keene records that as early as
1895, Fukuzawa Yukichi had written an editorial proposing an Anglo-Japanese alliance, while
Joseph Chamberlain had informally raised the idea with the Japanese minister in London.

### The Terms

After prolonged negotiation, the two sides agreed on six points:

1. An **open door** to be maintained in China.
2. No further acquisition of Chinese territory beyond existing treaty rights.
3. **Japan's special interests in Korea** explicitly recognised (Japan had "greater interests
   there than any other country").
4. If one ally were **attacked by a single power**, the other would preserve neutrality; if a
   **third power** joined the enemy, the other would enter the war as a belligerent.
5. The Anglo-German agreement on China (1900) to remain in force.
6. The alliance restricted to the **Far East**.

The critical fourth clause was the alliance's core: it guaranteed Japan that if it fought Russia
alone, Britain would stay neutral; if France (Russia's ally) joined in, Britain would enter the
war against France. This effectively neutralised the threat of a renewed Triple Intervention.

### Ratification

Itō Hirobumi — then in Russia pursuing the "Manchuria for Korea" alternative — sent a
telegram objecting to the treaty's wording and urging further consideration, noting possible
German objections and the still-open Russian negotiating track. Emperor Meiji, however, having
been briefed that the cabinet and genrō majority approved, told Prime Minister Katsura that
"in view of the treaty's approval by both the cabinet and the genrō, he thought that Japanese
consent could not be delayed." On 12 December 1901 Japan's acceptance was telegraphed to Hayashi.
Hayashi and Lansdowne signed the treaty in London on **30 January 1902**; it was announced
publicly on **12 February**.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895]]` — the Triple Intervention that forced Japan
to yield Liaodong, only for Russia to seize it, hardened Japanese public opinion against Russia
and made a strategic counter-alliance necessary.

`contributed_to:` the **Boxer Rebellion (1900)** and Japan's prominent role in the eight-nation
relief force, which demonstrated Japanese military competence and reliability to London.

`caused_by:` Russian expansion — the Trans-Siberian Railway, the Russo-Chinese secret treaty (1896),
and the occupation of Manchuria during the Boxer suppression — which threatened British and
Japanese interests alike and supplied the common threat to unite them.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced: [[russo-japanese-war-1904]]` — the alliance removed the risk of a Franco-German
intervention repeating the Triple Intervention; Japan could fight Russia without a multi-front
crisis. Hayashi himself believed the alliance was the decisive factor in Japan's victory.

`produced:` Japan's First World War participation (August 1914) and acquisition of former
German colonies in the South Pacific and German holdings in Shandong — a direct consequence
of the treaty obligation when Britain entered the war.

`produced:` immense prestige and emotional significance: Keene records that the joy felt by
the Japanese — "from the emperor on down" — at being recognised as "an equal partner of the
strongest nation in the world, a nation that in the past had humiliated Japan again and again"
was profound.

`enabled: [[japanese-imperialism]]` — the confirmed great-power status the alliance conferred
lowered barriers to future Japanese expansionism in Asia.

## Actors

- **Hayashi Tadasu** — Japanese minister to London; the principal Japanese negotiator who
  identified Britain as the necessary ally and drove the diplomacy.
- **Lord Lansdowne** — British Foreign Secretary; counter-signatory.
- **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** — opposed or at least delayed the alliance, favouring
  a Russian settlement; his telegram of objections was overridden by the emperor and genrō
  majority.
- **[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]** — gave decisive authorisation, citing the cabinet and
  genrō consensus: "Japanese consent could not be delayed."
- **Katsura Tarō** — Prime Minister at the time of ratification; pressed the case against
  Itō's hesitation.

## Historiography

Keene presents the alliance as the pivotal outcome of the post-1895 strategic reorientation,
casting the Boxer Rebellion as its direct cause and the Russo-Japanese War as its direct
product. The alliance is treated in Japanese historiography both as a diplomatic triumph
(recognition as a world power) and, in retrospect, as an early step toward the expansionism
that would culminate in 1945. The emotional weight Keene assigns to Meiji-era pride in the
alliance reflects the contemporary Japanese sense that the unequal-treaty era was definitively
over.
