# The Scramble for Africa

The **Scramble for Africa** was the rapid conquest and **partition of almost the entire continent** by the
European powers between roughly **1881 and 1914** — the territorial core of the **[[imperialism|New
Imperialism]]**. In a single generation Africa went from largely independent to almost wholly colonized,
its map redrawn by Europeans who, as **[[reader-africa-biography|Reader]]** stresses, neither invited nor
consulted a single African ruler. It is the African chapter of the wider
[[imperialism|New Imperialism]] and a CLAUDE.md standing transition-context for the continent.

## The Berlin Conference (1884–85)

The **Berlin Conference**, convened by Bismarck, set the ground rules for partition — "effective
occupation," free-trade zones, and the recognition of claims — entirely among Europeans. Reader's verdict
is mordant: the proceedings, "larded with expressions of good intent," were "a backstreet game of poker
with marked cards," with **no Africans present**. It legitimized **Leopold II's** seizure of the
**[[congo-free-state|Congo Free State]]** and triggered the rush of flag-planting that followed.

## How partition was done: the deceptive treaty

Reader's central mechanism is the **fraudulent concession-treaty**. Treaties were drafted in European
languages, full of terms (land "ownership in perpetuity," "protection") with **no counterpart in African
understanding**, and signed by rulers who trusted that they could always expel the interlopers later — and
could not. His case study is **Lewanika** of the **Lozi** (Barotseland, western Zambia): persuaded by the
missionary Coillard and the company agent **Lochner** — who "shamelessly misrepresented" Cecil Rhodes's
**British South Africa Company** as the arm of Queen Victoria — Lewanika signed away 200,000 square miles of
trading and mineral rights for promises of schools, railways and protection that were **never honoured**.
Barotseland became "a stagnant labour pool," its king demoted from "King" to "Paramount Chief." The same
pattern (Lobengula and the Rudd Concession in Matabeleland) recurred across the continent.

## Drivers

- **Great-power rivalry and the New Imperialism** (see [[imperialism]]): national prestige, strategic
  competition over the route to India and the Nile, and the logic of pre-emptive claiming.
- **The "tools of empire"** ([[industrialization|industrial technology]]): steamships, railways, the
  telegraph, **quinine** (against malaria), and the **Maxim gun**, which made conquest cheap and
  one-sided.
- **The [[southern-african-mineral-revolution|mineral revolution]]** and the ambitions of capitalists like
  **Rhodes** ("Cape to Cairo"), whose chartered company governed and conquered on Britain's behalf.

## Resistance

African societies resisted widely — from the Lozi's legal protests to armed defence — and not always in
vain: **Ethiopia under Menelik II destroyed an Italian army at Adwa (1896)**, the one major state to defeat
the Scramble and remain independent. But against industrial firepower most resistance was crushed (see
[[imperialism]] on the technological asymmetry; Omdurman, 1898).

## Consequences

- Nearly all of Africa partitioned into colonies with **arbitrary borders** cutting across peoples — a
  legacy that outlived the empires.
- **Colonial extraction and labour economies** (the [[southern-african-mineral-revolution|compound and
  migrant-labour systems]]; the Congo's rubber atrocities; the [[late-victorian-famines-1876|production of
  famine]] elsewhere).
- The grievances and structures against which mid-twentieth-century **[[decolonization|decolonization]]**
  would mobilize.
