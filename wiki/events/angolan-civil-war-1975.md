## Narrative

Angola's civil war ran in four phases from 1974/75 to 2002, a 27-year conflict pitting the Soviet- and Cuban-backed MPLA (People's Liberation Movement of Angola, founded 1956 among the Mbundu ethnic group and led by [[neto-agostinho|Agostinho Neto]]) against the US- and South African-backed FNLA (Bakongo, north, led by Holden Roberto, formed 1961) and UNITA (National Union for the Total Independence of Angola, Ovimbundu base in south-central Angola, led by [[savimbi-jonas|Jonas Savimbi]]). By McCoy's cited figures, the war ultimately killed roughly 800,000 people, displaced 4 million (out of a population of about 8 million), left 15 million landmines that had produced approximately 88,000 mine casualties by 2014, and reduced postwar life expectancy to 47 years by 2002.

In March 1961, FNLA fighters killed roughly 750 white settlers along a 400-mile front; Portuguese air reprisals sent an estimated 150,000 African refugees fleeing. Congo's leader Mobutu Sese Seko backed the FNLA as the "sole representative" of Angolan nationalism from 1963. Beijing sent about 300 Chinese instructors to train FNLA forces in Congo from 1964, withdrawing in October 1975 to redirect aid toward Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

In January 1975 the Ford administration's 40 Committee, with Kissinger's approval, authorized $300,000 to the FNLA and $100,000 to UNITA; after the fall of Saigon in April 1975, Ford authorized a hundredfold increase to $32 million (July 1975) under Operation IAFeature, deploying 83 CIA officers to distribute some 25 tons of weapons. South Africa's intelligence service BOSS formed an arm's-length alliance with the CIA in Angola from October 1974; in November 1975 South Africa's Operation Savannah sent a 2,500-strong armored column under Commandant Jan Dirk Breytenbach some 600 miles toward Luanda.

The Soviet Union began airlifting an estimated $300 million in arms to the MPLA from 5 November 1975, and Cuba flew roughly 650 special forces to Luanda along with an artillery regiment and 4,000 additional troops by ship. At the "Battle of Death Road" (Quifangondo) on 10 November 1975, Cuban troops firing Soviet 122mm rocket launchers routed a joint FNLA/Congo/South African-backed force approximately 15 miles from Luanda, just before Angolan independence on 11 November 1975, effectively eliminating the FNLA as a contender. On 23 November 1975 at the Battle of Ebo, 70 Cuban special forces destroyed 8 South African armored cars and killed 80 UNITA guerrillas, forcing a South African retreat; South Africa withdrew from Angola by March 1976, by which time Cuban troop strength had risen to 36,000 and the MPLA controlled Luanda, the Cabinda oil enclave, and sufficient military capacity to hold the state.

CIA officer John Stockwell, who headed Operation IAFeature, resigned in April 1977 and later testified to the Senate Intelligence Committee that CIA covert operations had "ranged from the felonious, to violent, to depraved," estimating the Angola task force alone cost 10,000 lives. In 1976, the US Congress passed the Clark Amendment, cutting off covert Angola aid for a decade.

In the war's second phase (1977–88), the formally announced Reagan Doctrine (February 1985) and Congress's repeal of the Clark Amendment (July 1985) allowed CIA aid to UNITA to rise to $50 million annually, later including 100 TOW missiles and 300 Stinger missiles. Savimbi, whose public image was managed by consultant Paul Manafort, met Reagan in the Oval Office on 30 January 1986; UN Ambassador Jeane Kirkpatrick called him "one of the few authentic heroes of our time," while the Congressional Black Caucus called him a "surrogate" for apartheid South Africa. Four days after that meeting Reagan's State of the Union address articulated the Reagan Doctrine, pledging support for "freedom fighters" in Afghanistan, Angola, Cambodia, and Nicaragua.

The war's decisive military turning point came at the siege of Cuito Cuanavale (October 1987–March 1988), where roughly 6,000 South African troops and 65,000 UNITA guerrillas attacked Angolan and Cuban forces defending the town (Cuban strength eventually reaching 55,000). By McCoy's cited figures, the siege cost 4,700 Angolan and 86 Cuban dead, 150 armored vehicles, and 12 MiG jets; South Africa's final major attack on 23 March 1988 was repulsed, and South Africa subsequently withdrew from Angola. Nelson Mandela later called Cuito Cuanavale "a victory for all Africa" that "destroyed the myth of the invincibility of the white oppressor." The New York Accord (December 1988), negotiated by Cuban delegate Jorge Risquet against US assistant secretary of state Chester Crocker, provided for South African withdrawal, an end to UNITA support, and Namibian independence in exchange for a phased Cuban withdrawal of 50,000 troops within two years; Namibian independence followed in 1990, and an Angolan civil-war truce was reached in June 1989, though the war later reignited.

In the war's final phase (1991–2002), Savimbi lost the 1992 presidential election to the MPLA's José Eduardo dos Santos and resumed fighting, funding UNITA through "blood diamonds" mined in the diamond-rich northern provinces after President Clinton's 1993 executive order banned US arms sales to UNITA. Savimbi was killed by government forces in February 2002 (shot 15 times), ending the war; his body was displayed on national television. Dos Santos ruled Angola from 1979 to 2017, his regime devolving into a patronage kleptocracy; his daughter Isabel dos Santos, who chaired the state oil company Sonangol and became Africa's richest woman, was later indicted for fraud (2020) and sanctioned by the UK as a "notorious kleptocrat" (2024), by which point roughly 53% of Angolans lived in extreme poverty.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by`: the collapse of Portuguese colonial rule (1974–75) without an agreed transfer of power among the three liberation movements.
- `contributed_to`: superpower and regional Cold War patronage (Soviet/Cuban support for the MPLA; US/South African support for FNLA and UNITA), which sustained and internationalized what began as a domestic succession conflict.
- `part_of`: the broader Cold War proxy conflicts of southern Africa, alongside the Mozambican Civil War and Namibia's independence struggle.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: Namibian independence (1990), secured as part of the New York Accord settlement.
- `contributed_to`: entrenchment of an MPLA one-party kleptocracy under dos Santos after 1979.
- `enabled`: [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis|McCoy's]] broader periodization argument that southern Africa's 1975 battles (Quifangondo, Ebo) opened "the final, destructive period of the global Cold War" — this is McCoy's interpretive framing, not settled historiographical consensus, and his comparison of Cuito Cuanavale to Dien Bien Phu as the "death knell" of European colonialism in Africa should likewise be read as his own comparative judgment.

## Historiography

Alfred W. McCoy's *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]* (2024) is the primary source for this page's figures, drawing on declassified US records, Cuban and South African military accounts, and secondary scholarship; casualty and cost figures throughout are attributed to McCoy's synthesis rather than presented as uncontested consensus totals. McCoy's argument that Cold War patronage produced structurally convergent kleptocracies in Angola (MPLA/dos Santos) and Congo (Mobutu) despite opposed ideologies is his own causal inference, not a proven finding.
