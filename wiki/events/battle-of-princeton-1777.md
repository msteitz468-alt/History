# The Battle of Princeton (3 January 1777)

> **⚔️ Staff-college tactical analysis:** see **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/battle-of-princeton|Princeton — Tactical Analysis]]** in the warfare hub for the full order of battle, the phased conduct of the action, and the principles-of-war assessment.

The second blow of the **Trenton-Princeton campaign** that completed the American revival. After
[[battle-of-trenton-1776|Trenton]], [[cornwallis-charles|Cornwallis]] advanced with 8,000 men to
trap Washington against the Delaware, boasting he would "bag the Old Fox in the morning."

## Narrative

Rather than fight or retreat downriver, [[washington-george|Washington]] **slipped his army around
Cornwallis's flank by a silent night march** — leaving campfires burning and a small party patrolling
at Trenton — and fell on the British rear at Princeton at dawn. Three British regiments, contemptuous
of American fighting quality, chose to stand; in sharp fighting (in which Brigadier General **Hugh
Mercer** was mortally wounded and Washington personally rode forward to rally the line) they were
driven back toward New Brunswick, losing ~276 to about 65 Americans. Washington then withdrew to the
defensible hills of **Morristown** to rebuild.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Cornwallis's **aggressive pursuit** and the opening it left, exploited by Washington's
  bold flank march on local intelligence.
- `contributed_to:` British **contempt** for the American army after 1776, which left them flat-footed.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the abandonment by Howe of most of his **New Jersey cantonments**, ending the threat to
  Philadelphia and reviving the cause — a performance even the British called a "prodigy of
  generalship."
- `produced:` a new respect among the enemy: "they are now become a formidable enemy" (Lt. Col.
  Harcourt).

## Historiography

Washington's own dispatches and British officers' letters (including the often-quoted Harcourt assessment of a newly "formidable enemy") document the battle closely, and it is frequently treated as the clearest tactical example of Washington's generalship — a night march executed with a deception (burning campfires) that succeeded completely. Historians debate how much of the Trenton-Princeton campaign's psychological impact on Congress, the army, and European observers reflects genuine strategic weight versus the mythologizing that began almost immediately in American memory. Casualty figures and some tactical details (the exact circumstances of Mercer's death) vary somewhat between sources.
