# Repeal of the Judiciary Act of 1801 (1802)

## Narrative

In the closing weeks of the Adams administration, the Federalist Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating new circuit courts and judgeships filled with "midnight" appointees. Upon taking office, Jefferson and the Republicans viewed the Act as a last-minute partisan fortification of the judiciary. In his first annual message (December 1801) Jefferson called attention to the unnecessary offices. Senator John Breckinridge introduced repeal legislation. After intense debate (Giles, Wright, and others on the Republican side; Bayard, Gouverneur Morris, and others on the Federalist), the repeal passed the Senate in February and the House in early March 1802. The new judgeships were abolished and the displaced judges left without positions.

The Federalists denounced the repeal as a violation of the Constitution's guarantee of judicial tenure during good behavior and an assault on judicial independence. Republicans argued that Congress had plenary power to create and abolish inferior courts and that the 1801 Act had been an unnecessary and expensive political job.

The Supreme Court (with Marshall) later upheld the repeal in *Stuart v. Laird* (1803) while deciding *Marbury v. Madison* in a manner that asserted judicial review without directly confronting the repeal.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Partisan composition of the lame-duck 1801 judiciary legislation + Republican electoral victory of 1800 interpreted as mandate to dismantle Federalist institutional entrenchment.

**contributed_to:** Jefferson's strict-construction and economy principles; widespread Republican resentment of Federalist judges' behavior during the Sedition Act prosecutions.

## Consequence Analysis

- Institutional: Established that Congress could restructure the lower federal judiciary.
- Political: Deepened partisan hatred; Federalists portrayed repeal as revolutionary destruction of the Constitution.
- Constitutional: Set stage for *Marbury* (judicial review announced) while the Court prudently accepted the repeal.
- Long-term: Contributed to the image of Jefferson as hostile to an independent judiciary (a charge he and Malone dispute in nuance).

## Historiography

Malone (Vol. 4) gives the fullest account from the administration's perspective, showing Jefferson's measured approach and the political context. Modern views see the episode as a formative test of the separation of powers and the first major instance of partisan "court packing" reversal.

**Sources:** Malone, *Jefferson the President: First Term* (1970); Annals of Congress; *Stuart v. Laird*.
