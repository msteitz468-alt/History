# The Plague of Cyprian

## Narrative

A devastating epidemic that broke out in **Ethiopia in 250 CE**, spread to Roman Egypt the
following year during the reigns of **Decius (249–251)** and **Gallus (251–253)**, and
ravaged the [[roman-empire|Roman Empire]] for roughly two decades (250–270 CE). At its
height (250–266) up to **5,000 people a day** were reportedly dying in Rome, depleting the
army and causing severe labour shortages; **Emperor Claudius Gothicus** himself died of it
in 270 CE. It struck during the worst phase of the
[[crisis-of-the-third-century|Crisis of the Third Century]].

It is named for **St. Cyprian, bishop of Carthage**, whose vivid account (*De Mortalitate*)
describes bowel dissolution, continuous vomiting, eyes "set on fire from the force of the
blood," and loss of gait, hearing, and sight. [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]]
cites **Kyle Harper**, who argues the symptoms "sound more like... yellow fever or Ebola
than smallpox" — distinguishing it from the earlier [[antonine-plague-165|Antonine
Plague]].

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` an unidentified pathogen out of Ethiopia, spread along trade and troop
movements. `concurrent_with: [[crisis-of-the-third-century]]` — the two reinforced each
other.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` mass mortality, labour shortage, and a weakened army at the worst possible
moment, `contributed_to: [[collapse-of-first-silk-roads-era-250]]`. Socially, it
`enabled:` the relative growth of **Christianity**: pagan society scapegoated Christians,
but Harper argues the plague's psychological toll fell harder on pagans, while Christian
communities responded in a more orderly way — especially in burial — and offered
consolation traditional Roman religion could not.

## Actors

[[roman-empire]] · Christian communities · the soldier-emperors of the crisis.

## Historiography

Known chiefly from **Cyprian's *De Mortalitate***, supplemented by later sources; the
pathogen is unidentified and debated (Harper's yellow-fever/viral-hemorrhagic hypothesis
vs. older smallpox/measles assumptions). Part of the broader scholarly turn — central to
Benjamin's collapse argument — toward **disease as a driver** of the third-century
contraction at both ends of the [[silk-roads|Silk Roads]].
