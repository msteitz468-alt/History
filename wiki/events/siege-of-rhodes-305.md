## Narrative

After his victory at Salamis-in-Cyprus (306) gave the Antigonids command of the
eastern seas, [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I]] resolved to remove the one
remaining obstacle to total Aegean control: the free and prosperous island-state of
**[[rhodes|Rhodes]]**, which had aligned itself with [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy]] and
refused to take part in the Antigonid campaigns against Cyprus and Egypt. In 305–304
[[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]] besieged the city with the most elaborate siege
train the Greek world had yet seen — culminating in the giant *helepolis*
("city-taker") siege-tower. From this celebrated effort he won the surname by which
history knows him, **Poliorcetes, "the Besieger"** (more exactly "taker of cities").

He failed. Ptolemy kept the city supplied by sea (earning, by tradition, his own cult
title *Soter*, "Saviour"), and after a year Demetrius had to come to terms. The
Antigonids recognised Rhodian liberty; Rhodes allied with them on the express
condition that the alliance would never be turned against Ptolemy.

## Consequence Analysis

Will draws the structural lesson explicitly: that the survival of Rhodian freedom
shows the "freedom of the Greek cities" in this period was **not a legal doctrine but
a balance of forces**. The preserved independence underwrote the island's commercial
and naval prosperity for over a century — Rhodes becomes a major maritime power,
arbiter, and anti-piracy force in later chapters (see [[rhodes]]). To commemorate the
raising of the siege the Rhodians erected, in honour of their god Helios, the
**Colossus of Rhodes**, counted among the seven wonders of the world.

## Historiography

Diodorus XX (from [[hieronymus-of-cardia|Hieronymus]]) gives the fullest account,
with Plutarch's *Demetrius*. The siege is a locus classicus for Hellenistic
[[hellenistic-science|siegecraft and military technology]] (treated in CAH VII.1 ch.
9b). See [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]].
