## Narrative

The revolt of Taira no Masakado is conventionally treated as the beginning of the age of warrior dominance in Japanese history. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] dates its beginning to 935: "The revolt in the east began inconspicuously in 935 as an armed squabble among members of a Taira family who had settled in the Kanto area perhaps forty-five years earlier" (p. 653). By that time the Taira descendants of Takamochi — appointed vice-governor of Kazusa around 889 — had become great landowners in Kazusa, Shimosa, and Hitachi.

### Origins and Escalation

Masakado began as one fighter among several in intra-clan disputes over land. Operating from a base in Shimosa, he became the primary military power and "principal arbiter of disputes throughout the southern Kanto area." The disputes centered on resistance to provincial tax exactions. His forces included a permanent bodyguard of 400 mounted warriors (jusha), independent allied warriors (banrui), and provincial peasant foot soldiers.

The court paid minimal attention initially, assessing mild punishment that was promptly canceled in a general amnesty. The situation changed in late 939 when Masakado burned the Hitachi provincial headquarters, seized the official provincial seal, and took the keys to the provincial storehouse — "a direct challenge to its authority or income" that "the inward-looking court at Heian could scarcely ignore" (p. 655).

At the urging of Prince Okiyo, who observed that "the punishment for rebellion in many provinces was no worse than for that in one" (Shomonki, p. 208), Masakado seized all eight Kanto provinces, proclaimed himself the "New Emperor" (shinno), and began establishing a warrior state in Shimosa. He appointed new provincial officials and sent a message to the regent Fujiwara no Tadahira, under whom he had formerly served, proposing in effect a division of Japan between the Fujiwara and his warrior family.

### The Warrior Ideal

Masakado's letter to the regent, preserved in the Shomonki, reveals the earliest articulation of a warrior self-conception. He justified his actions on three grounds: "(1) the precedents of the past, history having recorded earlier instances of usurpation of central authority by arms; (2) Masakado's descent from an emperor, a lineage that could entitle him to exercise sovereignty over the country; and (3) his military power, with which he had been endowed by Heaven, not by any governmental authority" (p. 653). This rationale — ancestry, martial power, divine mandate — prefigures later warrior ideology.

### Suppression

The court adopted a three-pronged response: prayer; appointment of Pursuit and Apprehension Agents (tsuibushi) and dispatch of a court commander, Fujiwara no Tadafumi; and promises of reward to provincial leaders who suppressed Masakado. It was the third prong that worked. In early 940, with Masakado's force reduced from as many as six thousand to a thousand men (the remainder having dispersed back to their agricultural pursuits), he was surprised at his Shimosa base and killed by the joint forces of his cousin Taira no Sadamori and Fujiwara no Hidesato — the latter holding the imperially conferred title of Suppression and Control Agent (oryoshi) of Shimotsuke.

The failure was organizational: "Masakado's lack of a retainership system meant that his coalition force of antigovernment landholders tended to fragment after initial successes had achieved the aims of the landholders" (p. 655). This diagnosis of the revolt's failure is simultaneously the founding argument for what [[bushidan]] organizations would become over the following century and a half.

## Causal Analysis

The revolt was caused by structural conditions, not Masakado's personal ambition alone. The statutory conscript army had been abandoned, responsibility for provincial order ceded to wealthy landholders, and provincial governors were dependent on private warriors. The court had no effective military presence in the east. Masakado was "a professional warrior, a tsuwamono in the contemporary lexicon" (p. 661) who "gathered many fierce tsuwamono as his companions and engaged in battles as his occupation" (Konjaku monogatari-shu, kan 25, story 1). The conditions for revolt were structural; the specific trigger was the escalating Kanto land disputes.

The concurrent piracy of Fujiwara no Sumitomo in the Inland Sea (936–941) was not coordinated with Masakado's revolt, though the court feared otherwise — "buffeted by alarming reports from east and west, the court seems to have been on the verge of panic, fearing that Sumitomo and Masakado were acting in concert" (p. 657). Sumitomo's was an independent phenomenon driven by different social forces (renegade official, pirate networks of coastal fishermen), suppressed separately in 941.

## Consequence Analysis

The revolt's suppression did not restore court authority in the east. Instead: "The revolt and its suppression confirmed the private wealth and military strength of the local leaders" (p. 659). The four men most prominent in the affair — Masakado, Sadamori, Hidesato, and Minamoto no Tsunemoto — "were the ancestors of most of the important warrior leaders in the east during the remainder of the Heian period, and it was from the east that the decisive military power flowed in the fateful twelfth century" (p. 659–60).

The court's dependence on provincial warriors for military force was permanently institutionalized. The chinjufu General title and the oryoshi/tsuibushi titles became the framework through which private warrior power was legitimated as quasi-governmental agency.

## Historiography

The principal primary source is the Shomonki (Story of Masakado's Rebellion), a nearly contemporary account in hentai kambun believed completed a few months after Masakado's death in 940 (translated by Judith Rabinovitch, Sophia University, 1986). It is considered the most reliable account, though it survives as a "unique, single text of uncertain authenticity" (p. 662). The Konjaku monogatari-shu (12th-century tale collection) preserves additional material on Masakado but was compiled over a century after events. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] draws on both alongside Takeuchi Rizo's modern scholarship.

The significance of the revolt is a matter of interpretive debate: "In the opinion of some, [the revolts of Masakado and Sumitomo were] the beginning of the 'middle age' (chusei)" (p. 661). The chapter treats this interpretation cautiously — the revolts "were not revolutionary in effect" — while acknowledging that "at least they were the first major efforts by provincial military leaders to assert their autonomous strength" (p. 661).
