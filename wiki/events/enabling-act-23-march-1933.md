# Enabling Act (Ermächtigungsgesetz), 23 March 1933

## Narrative

The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 granted the Hitler government the power to enact laws without the involvement of the Reichstag for a period of four years (renewable). It effectively ended the Weimar constitutional order and provided the legal basis for the rapid establishment of the dictatorship.

In the negotiations of early August 1932, the prospective ‘deal’ with Schleicher already envisaged: ‘If the Reichstag rejects the enabling act, it will be sent packing (nach Hause geschickt).’ The mechanism was thus contemplated well before power was attained.

Following the appointment of 30 January 1933 and the Reichstag Fire Decree (which suspended civil liberties and allowed mass arrests of Communists and other opponents), the new Reichstag (elected 5 March under conditions of terror and Nazi dominance of the streets) met in the Kroll Opera House. With the KPD deputies arrested or in hiding and the SPD isolated, the necessary two-thirds majority was secured with the support of the Centre Party and other bourgeois remnants alongside the NSDAP and DNVP. The Act passed on 23 March 1933.

Hitler presented it as a temporary measure for national recovery; in practice it was the decisive instrument of Machtergreifung.

## Causal Analysis

The Enabling Act was the direct legislative consequence of the conservative élites’ decision to install Hitler as Chancellor in the belief they could contain him. The Reichstag Fire provided the immediate pretext and atmosphere of emergency. Intimidation (SA presence, arrests) and the exclusion of the largest opposition party (KPD) ensured passage. The Centre Party’s vote was secured by false assurances and the broader conservative desire to see strong government.

From Kershaw: the ‘levered’ nature is clear – Hitler’s movement supplied the mass pressure and parliamentary plurality, but the conservatives supplied the final constitutional fig-leaf and the miscalculation that allowed the Act to be used against them.

## Consequence Analysis

The Enabling Act removed the last institutional restraints on Hitler’s government. Combined with the Reichstag Fire Decree, it enabled the rapid ‘co-ordination’ (Gleichschaltung) of state and society: dissolution or nazification of parties, trade unions, federal states, civil service, judiciary, and cultural institutions. By summer 1933 the one-party state was in place; the conservative ‘allies’ of January were already being sidelined.

It marked the formal end of parliamentary democracy in Germany and the birth of the Third Reich as a personal dictatorship resting on ‘legal’ revolutionary foundations.

## Actors

- **[[actors/adolf-hitler|Adolf Hitler]]**: Demanded and received the Act; used it to dismantle the constitution.
- NSDAP parliamentary leadership (Frick, Göring): Managed the vote.
- **Alfred Hugenberg** and DNVP: Voted in favour, believing it served their ends.
- Centre Party leadership: Voted yes under pressure and illusion of safeguards.
- SPD (Otto Wels): Alone voted no in a courageous last stand.

## Evans (2003): The Vote in Detail

- **Potsdam Day** (21 March, garrison church, anniversary of Bismarck's first
  Reichstag): the Goebbels-staged reconciliation tableau — Hindenburg in
  Field-Marshal's uniform beside the Kaiser's vacant throne, the frock-coated
  Hitler bowing; black-white-red had officially replaced the republican colors
  on 12 March; the SPD declined to attend; Hitler pointedly boycotted the
  Catholic service — "a clear warning to the Church."
- **Quorum manipulation**: Göring, presiding, reduced the quorum from 432 to
  378 by not counting the legally elected KPD deputies — "a high-handed
  decision that had no legitimacy in law whatsoever" (Evans). Only 94 of 120
  SPD deputies could vote (prison, illness, fear).
- **The Centre's capitulation**: under Prelate Kaas the party "had long since
  ceased to be a supporter of democracy"; the 1929 Italian Concordat supplied
  the model; two days of Hitler's assurances (Church rights, the federated
  states, judicial independence) plus "heavy pressure from the Vatican" won
  its votes. Wirth privately: the deputies feared "bloody anarchy" if the Act
  failed.
- **The scene** (Wilhelm Hoegner): "Wild chants greeted us: 'We want the
  Enabling Law!'... In the Kroll Opera it was swarming with armed SA and SS."
  Hitler closed with "May you, gentlemen, now take the decision yourselves as
  to whether it is to be peace or war."
- **Otto Wels**, carrying a cyanide capsule in his waist pocket, gave the
  Republic's epitaph: "In this historic hour, we German Social Democrats
  solemnly profess our allegiance to the basic principles of humanity and
  justice, freedom and socialism. No Enabling Law gives you the right to
  annihilate ideas that are eternal and indestructible." Hitler, replying from
  an advance press copy: "Germany's star will rise and yours will sink...
  Germany shall be free, but not through you!"
- **Vote and afterlife**: 444–94 [CONTRADICTION: Paxton & Hessler give 441–92 — see below] (only the SPD against; the bill would have
  passed even with every SPD and KPD deputy present and voting no). Renewed
  1937 and 1939; made permanent by decree in 1943. Hindenburg was thereafter
  a rubber stamp or bypassed entirely.

## Paxton (2011): Vote Count, Aftermath, and the January 1934 Reichsrat Abolition

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton and Hessler]] give the vote as
**441 in favor to 92 against**, with only the SPD opposed; the KPD's 81 elected
deputies and 12 SPD deputies were already jailed and so could not vote at all.

## Historiography

Kershaw places the Enabling Act within the broader ‘Machtergreifung’ phase (chs. 10–11): the product of élite miscalculation interacting with Nazi ruthlessness and the structural weakness of Weimar institutions. It exemplifies how Hitler’s ‘legal’ path (emphasized since the 1930 Leipzig trial) was used to destroy legality. The Act’s passage with conservative and centrist votes underscores the complicity of traditional élites. Structuralists note the pre-existing presidential government trend (Brüning–Papen–Schleicher) made the final step easier; intentionalists stress Hitler’s unwavering will to total power. Kershaw integrates both via the charismatic leadership framework and ‘working towards the Führer’.

**Grounded in range:** Kershaw, *Hitler 1889–1936: Hubris* (1998), references in ch. 10 to enabling act in the Schleicher negotiations, and the overall analysis of the 1933 consolidation mechanics.

## Shirer (1960) Additions

- Cabinet minutes of 15 March 1933 (produced at Nuremberg) show the two-thirds strategy being engineered in advance: excluding the 81 "absent" Communist deputies, Göring proposing "refusing admittance to a few Social Democrats"; Hugenberg wanted President Hindenburg empowered to co-approve cabinet decrees but was rebuffed by State Secretary Meissner.
- "Day of Potsdam" (21 March 1933), staged for broadcast and photography by Goebbels, deliberately chosen on the anniversary of Bismarck's 1871 Reichstag opening to link the regime with Prussian/Hohenzollern legitimacy — corroborates Evans's account above. French ambassador André François-Poncet's contemporary judgment (quoted by Shirer): the Potsdam "dazzling pledge" dissolved conservative apprehension about Hitler and led them to "concede the full powers he claimed."
- Full text of the Enabling Act ("Law for Removing the Distress of People and Reich") as summarized by Shirer: cabinet given legislative power (including budget, treaties, constitutional amendments) for four years; laws could "deviate from the constitution"; storm troopers lined the Kroll Opera House aisles during the vote.
- [SHIRER-THESIS]: Shirer quotes Alan Bullock's framing device — "The street gangs had seized control of the resources of a great modern State, the gutter had come to power" — but "legally," by parliamentary vote, capturing the "legal revolution" thesis that recurs across this ingest.

Source: [[shirer-rise-fall-third-reich-1960]].
