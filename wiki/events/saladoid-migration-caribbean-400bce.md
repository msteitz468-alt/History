## Narrative

Arawak-speaking communities from continental South America began entering the Caribbean Sea between approximately 800 and 200 BCE, initiating what archaeologists call the Early Ceramic Age. These colonists, broadly identified by their shared ceramic traditions and farming economy, are known as "Saladoid" peoples after the Saladero site on the lower Orinoco River. Their migration from the Orinoco-Amazonia region into the Lesser Antilles and Greater Antilles represents one of the most consequential population movements in Caribbean prehistory.

The centre of dispersal appears to have been the northwest Amazon between the upper Amazon in Brazil and the middle Orinoco in Venezuela. Linguistic evidence suggests that well before 1000 BCE, Arawak communities were moving rapidly through the Negro and Orinoco floodplains and along the Caribbean coast. Saladoid pottery — distinguished above all by white-on-red (WOR) painted designs — was widely distributed along the Orinoco valley and coastal Venezuela before its appearance in the islands.

The earliest known Ceramic Age sites in the Antilles are all located in the **northern** islands: Puerto Rico, Vieques, the Virgin Islands, Montserrat, Antigua, and St. Martin. Radiocarbon dates from these locations cluster between 800 and 200 BCE (2-sigma), a finding that fundamentally overturned the classic "stepping-stone" model. In that model, colonists were expected to have moved island by island northward from Trinidad/Tobago through the Windward Islands before reaching Puerto Rico. Instead, the archaeological evidence shows that the **Windward Islands of the southern Lesser Antilles were sparsely inhabited or uninhabited during this initial expansion**, while the northern islands were fully settled centuries earlier.

The revised model, supported by simulation studies and recent survey work, proposes **multiple, semi-independent migrations** from different communities along the coasts of South and Central America. These communities broadly shared Arawak cultural traditions — settled village life, intensive horticulture, landscape alteration, institutional social ranking by lineage, and a cultural emphasis on long-distance exchange — while adapting to local island conditions.

### Key Sites

**Trants, Montserrat (c. 400 BCE–AD 500):** One of the earliest Ceramic Age sites in the Antilles, located on the windward shore adjacent to Montserrat's airport. The site was a large oval village (approximately 275 × 225 m) with houses and middens surrounding a central plaza, a layout maintained with remarkable continuity for nearly a millennium. The pottery is Saladoid in style (white-on-red, black-on-red, ZIC), with some Huecoid elements. The site had an active lapidary industry using non-local exotic stones (carnelian, amethyst, aventurine, turquoise, jade, serpentine), and complementary exchange with distant sites (carnelian debitage at Trants paired with finished amethyst beads; opposite distribution at Pearls, Grenada). Diet combined terrestrial animals (primarily oryzomyine rodents) with reef fish in roughly equal proportions, typical of early northern Lesser Antillean sites.

**Hacienda Grande, Puerto Rico (c. AD 150–500):** The type-site for the Hacienda Grande pottery style, located on the northeast coast ~25 km east of modern San Juan. A medium-sized circular to oval village (~200 × 150 m), it was the base from which population spread upstream along the Río Loíza. Pottery is thin, well-made Saladoid ware with polychrome painting, ZIC, and modeled zoomorphic lugs; 30% of sherds are decorated. Small three-pointed stones (cemís) appear here, considered precursors to later, more elaborate trigonoliticos. Diet shifted over time from land crabs to marine species.

**Morel, Guadeloupe (c. 400 BCE–AD 1400):** Located directly on the Atlantic coast of eastern Grande-Terre. An early component with mixed Huecoid and Saladoid material culture; four sub-components (Morel I–IV) have long anchored Caribbean ceramic chronologies for the northern Lesser Antilles. Notable for dog burials interred alongside human burials, and for a rich assemblage of semiprecious stone beads and wooden amulets (dog and jaguar figures recovered after a hurricane).

**Golden Rock, St. Eustatius (c. AD 200–850):** Located at the center of the island on prime agricultural land. The site's most remarkable feature is its well-preserved post-holes revealing circular to oval house structures 7–19 m in diameter, compared to Amazonian maloca-style great houses. Marine resources dominated diet (grouper, scad, tuna = 75% of faunal sample), with turtle, rice rat, and agouti also present. Eleven burials excavated in the central plaza area; a child's burial included 81 quartz beads. The Saladoid pottery traditions at this site persisted into the 9th century, later than on other islands.

### The Huecoid Problem

Coexisting with Saladoid assemblages at several sites is a distinct material tradition called **Huecoid** (after Sorcé/La Hueca on Vieques Island). Huecoid pottery is unpainted, with zoned-incised-crosshatch (ZIC) as the primary decorative mode; vessel forms differ from Saladoid; and the lithic assemblages are sufficiently distinct to indicate separate cultural traditions. At the Punta Candelero site in eastern Puerto Rico, Huecoid materials occur without any Saladoid association. Lithic analysis (Rodríguez Ramos 2010) demonstrates independent cultural traditions. Whether Huecoid represents a separate earlier migration, a continuation of an Archaic Age ceramic tradition, or a specialization within Saladoid remains debated. The relationship was one of contemporaneity within a shared exchange network.

## Causal Analysis

**Push/pull dynamics:** The colonists left continental South America in response to a cost-benefit ratio that favoured island settlement. Specific push factors (population pressure, resource competition) remain unclear. Pull factors included the islands' abundant marine resources, tropical horticultural potential, and — once the initial discovery of Grenada enabled visibility along the arc — the effective appearance of a "continent divided by water" (Torres and Rodríguez Ramos 2008).

**Social prerequisites:** Island colonisation was not the free movement of individuals but the organised movement of entire communities replicating economic, social, and political entities. Moore's (2001) demographic simulations show that colony success is greatly enhanced by maintaining ties to at least one parent community. Social distance — the difficulty of assembling the spouse pools and kin networks necessary for viable colonies — was the primary constraint, not transport technology or knowledge of island existence.

**Why northern islands first:** The archaeological evidence falsifies the stepping-stone model. Simulation studies (Callaghan 2013) suggest that direct jumps from South America to the Greater Antilles may have been easier than progressive island-hopping. Multiple independent migration waves from different source communities across the Orinoco-Caribbean coast better fits the data than a single propagule moving sequentially through the Lesser Antilles.

**Arawak cultural predisposition:** Heckenberger (2002) identifies a deep Arawak habitus: a predisposition toward settled village life, large fixed populations, intensive subsistence economies, institutional social ranking by bloodline and birth order, regional integration, and an emphasis on exchange of foreign goods. This cultural template was portable and reproduced across dispersal zones from Amazonia to the Caribbean.

## Consequence Analysis

The Saladoid migration established the demographic and cultural foundation for all subsequent Caribbean Ceramic Age societies. Communities persisted for centuries in stable oval or circular villages with central plazas, developing sophisticated lapidary industries and pan-regional exchange networks that linked islands to each other and to the South American mainland. The shared ceramic iconography — elaborately decorated WOR and polychrome pottery — served as a material expression of social solidarity across 2,500 km of dispersed island settlement, what Keegan (2004) calls a "veneer" that reflected shared needs for regional integration during the risky phase of island colonisation.

The migration brought domesticated plants (manioc, sweet potato, maize, various tropical fruits) and animals (dogs, guinea pigs, hutias) to islands previously inhabited only by Archaic Age forager-horticulturalists. Archaic Age communities continued as independent entities alongside Saladoid settlers for several centuries; by ~AD 200 they appear to have been absorbed or displaced in Puerto Rico, though their dietary preferences (marine mollusks over crabs) and lithic traditions may have contributed to the later Ostionoid ceramic tradition.

After approximately AD 600, the Saladoid "veneer" began dissolving as populations grew, regional identities strengthened, and the economic imperatives of island colonisation gave way to more localised social formations. This is the process documented in `[[post-saladoid-caribbean-differentiation]]`.

## Actors

The Saladoid peoples were not a discrete ethnicity but a broad cultural horizon of Arawak-speaking communities from northern South America (see `[[saladoid-culture]]`). No named individuals or political units are identifiable archaeologically. Archaic Age communities (see `[[archaic-age-caribbean]]`) were present as contemporaries on many islands, with complex relationships of coexistence, interaction, and eventual absorption.

## Historiography

The dominant interpretive framework for most of the 20th century was Irving Rouse's model of a single Saladoid migration proceeding island by island northward from the Orinoco Delta via Trinidad into the Greater Antilles (Rouse 1986, 1992). This model was challenged beginning in the 1980s when radiocarbon dates from northern islands (Puerto Rico, Montserrat, Antigua, St. Martin) proved consistently earlier than those from the southern Windward Islands nearer the mainland.

Luis Chanlatte Baik's identification of Huecoid as a distinct cultural tradition (1981) was the first major assault on a unified Saladoid origin model, though it took two decades to gain full acceptance. Rodríguez Ramos's lithic analyses (2010) ultimately demonstrated that Saladoid and Huecoid represent distinct technological traditions, vindicating the separate-migration interpretation.

The current consensus (Keegan and Hofman 2017; Hofman et al. 2011, 2014) favours multiple semi-independent migration waves from communities across the full extent of the Orinoco-Caribbean coast and possibly Central America, with no single point source and no single directional progression. Callaghan's (2013) computer simulations of Caribbean sailing conditions support the plausibility of long-distance direct crossings rather than stepping-stone progression.

Primary evidence is entirely archaeological: radiocarbon dates from stratified deposits, ceramic typology and compositional analysis, lithic source analysis (Knippenberg 1999, 2006), faunal and botanical remains, stable isotope analysis, and lapidary material sourcing. No written sources exist for this period. The dating method is radiocarbon with 2-sigma calibrated ranges; all dates carry uncertainty intervals of ±100–200 years or more.
