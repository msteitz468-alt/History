# Treaty of Verdun (August 843)

## Narrative

In August 843, after a year of hard diplomacy following the [[battle-of-fontenoy-841|battle of
Fontenoy]] and the **Strasbourg Oaths**, the three surviving sons of [[louis-the-pious]] partitioned
the [[carolingian-empire]] among themselves:

- **[[lothar-i|Lothar I]]** kept the **imperial title** and a long, narrow **Middle Kingdom** running
  from Frisia through Lotharingia and Burgundy to Italy, including the old imperial centres of Aachen
  and Rome;
- **[[louis-the-german|Louis the German]]** received **East Francia**, the lands east of the Rhine
  (plus the bridgehead cities of Mainz, Speyer, and Worms);
- **[[charles-the-bald|Charles the Bald]]** received **West Francia**, the lands west of the Scheldt,
  Meuse, Saône, and Rhône.

Their nephew **Pippin II of Aquitaine** was excluded altogether.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the victory of Charles and Louis at [[battle-of-fontenoy-841]], which discredited the
  unitary model
- `caused_by:` the collapse of the **Ordinatio imperii (817)**, whose principle of indivisible empire
  Frankish custom and aristocratic interest would not sustain
- `contributed_to:` the work of noble *missi* who spent "many months' laborious assessing" of royal
  estates, churches, markets, and mints — for, as Lothar put it, from those resources kings got "the
  wherewithal to reward the men who followed them loyally." NCMH II stresses Verdun was a **trade-off
  between the competing interests of the Carolingians and of their men**, not a tidy geographical split.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the threefold political division that underlies the long-term emergence of **France** (West
  Francia) and **Germany** (East Francia)
- `produced:` the politically incoherent **Middle Kingdom**, which fragmented further after Lothar's death
  (855) — its northern part becoming **Lotharingia/Lorraine**, the perennially contested borderland between
  the French and German worlds
- `enabled:` a generation of further "ephemeral maps" of the Carolingian *regna* (redivisions and mergers
  843–888) down to the final fragmentation of 888

## Historiography

NCMH II (Nelson, Ch. 4) resists the nationalist teleology that reads Verdun as "the birth of France and
Germany": in 843 these were Frankish *regna* defined by dynastic and aristocratic bargaining, not nations.
The treaty itself does not survive; its terms are reconstructed from Nithard and the annal traditions, whose
separation (the western *Annals of St-Bertin* vs. the eastern *Annals of Fulda*) is itself a symptom of the
political divergence Verdun set in motion.
