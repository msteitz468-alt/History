# The Pacific War and the Occupation of Japan (1941–1945; Occupation 1945–52)

The catastrophic climax of [[japanese-imperialism|Japanese imperialism]] — Japan's war
against China, the United States, and the British Empire — ending in the **first outright
defeat in [[japan|Japanese]] history** and the transformative Allied Occupation. The
Asian–Pacific theater of the Second World War. From Mason & Caiger's
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (ch. 17).

## Narrative

**War in China (from 1937).** The "China Incident" (a clash near Peking, July 1937)
escalated into a full, undeclared war; Japan took the cities (the **sack of Nanking**, late
1937, tens of thousands of civilians slaughtered) but could not defeat the Nationalists, who
withdrew to Chungking — "neither won nor lost." Konoe's **"New Order in East Asia"** (1938)
masked occupation with the rhetoric of pan-Asian cooperation.

**The road to Pearl Harbor.** Bogged down in China and blaming Western aid to it, Japan
moved into **Indochina (1940–41)**; the US responded with a **freeze and oil embargo** that
threatened to strangle the war machine within two years. Unwilling "to negotiate the
withdrawal of an unbeaten army," Japan's leaders (now under General **Tōjō**) chose war.

**The Pacific War (1941–45).** The carrier strike on **Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941)** and
a lightning offensive overran Malaya, **Singapore**, the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies,
and Burma within five months — the **Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere** at its widest
(mid-1942 to mid-1943). The tide turned at **Midway (June 1942)**; thereafter US naval and
submarine power, island by island (Guadalcanal, Saipan, Okinawa), strangled Japan's
shipping and bombed its cities. The **atomic bombs on Hiroshima (6 Aug.) and Nagasaki (9
Aug. 1945)**, and the Soviet attack in Manchuria, brought **Emperor Hirohito's** decision to
accept the Potsdam surrender — announced by radio on **15 August 1945**.

**The Occupation (1945–52).** Under **General Douglas MacArthur** (SCAP), the Allies
(overwhelmingly American) ruled indirectly through the Japanese government for nearly seven
years: a **Purge** of ~200,000 from public life and the war-crimes execution of Tōjō and
six others; demilitarization; and sweeping reforms of **land** (tenants made owners),
**education**, labor, and **big business** (the zaibatsu). The new **Constitution of 1947**
vested sovereignty in the people, made the emperor a **"symbol of the state,"** and — in the
famous **Article 9** — had Japan "forever renounce war." From 1948, with the Cold War and
China's communist victory, US policy pivoted to rebuilding Japan as a capitalist ally; the
**Korean War (1950)** boom sped recovery, and the Occupation ended with the San Francisco
peace treaty (1951; effective April 1952).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **unwinnable China war** and the **US oil embargo (1941)** — the choice
  between humiliating withdrawal and a gamble on quick victory.
- `contributed_to:` [[japanese-imperialism|ultranationalism]] and the **autonomy of the
  military** in policymaking; the belief that Japanese spirit could overcome American
  industrial might (a fatal "inadequate appreciation of the will of foreign peoples to
  resist").

## The home front and the road to war (Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 6)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v6-twentieth-century|Cambridge History Vol. 6]] (Gordon Berger,
ch. 2) shows that the war abroad was preceded by the building of a **mobilized home front**:
the [[japanese-militarism|collapse of party government]] after 1932, the **National
Mobilization Law (1938)**, the rise of "reformist" bureaucrats bent on a planned war economy,
and the dissolution of the political parties into the single **Imperial Rule Assistance
Association (IRAA, 1940)**. Berger stresses that the decisions of 1940–41 — the Axis alliance,
the move into Southeast Asia for oil and strategic materials, and ultimately war with the
Anglo-American powers — were driven by the army's quest for **continental autonomy** and the
"golden opportunity" that the German conquest of Western Europe seemed to open in the colonial
south. Alvin Coox's detailed operational chapter (ch. 7) traces the war itself from "Tora!
Tora! Tora!" through Midway to Okinawa and Japan *in extremis*.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **devastation of Japan** (a quarter of urban housing destroyed; the atomic
  bombings) and the **end of its empire**.
- `produced:` the **Occupation reforms and the 1947 constitution** — a remade, demilitarized,
  democratic Japan in which, paradoxically, the **imperial institution survived** and helped
  reconcile the nation to defeat.
- `enabled:` the **acceleration of decolonization** — Japan's conquests had shattered Western
  colonial prestige, and the regimes it sponsored became "part of the early history of new
  nations" (Indonesia, Burma, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaya).

## Significance

The war is the great rupture of modern Japanese history — the collapse of the imperial
project built since Meiji — and the prelude to Japan's postwar reinvention as a peaceful
economic power. Mason and Caiger stress the irony that the **emperor-centred ideology** the
militarists had stoked became the instrument of **peaceful surrender and occupation**, and
that defeat opened the way to reforms (land, democracy) that prewar reformers had sought in
vain.

## Pacific Islanders in the War (Bennett & Poyer, Cambridge History of the Pacific Ocean, Vol. 2)

*Material from [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023]], ch. 55 (Judith A. Bennett and Lin Poyer), which focuses on Islander experience — complementing the Japan-centred narrative above.*

### Strategic Geography and Islander Exposure

The war's effects "differed greatly, depending on whether islands became sites of combat
or of rear-line support. Almost all of Polynesia remained behind the front lines … a marked
contrast to the battlegrounds of much of Micronesia and western Melanesia, where people
suffered deprivation, fear, and loss." Japan launched its December 1941 attacks "from
Micronesian bases" against US holdings at Hawaii, Wake, Guam, and the Philippines.

By mid-1930s Japan had shifted from economic development of the Mandate Islands to
fortification: the Imperial Japanese Navy base at Chuuk was strengthened; Marshall Islanders'
land was confiscated and communities relocated to make way for airfields; Saipan and Tinian's
military infrastructure was developed. "Most Islanders began the war confident in Japanese
military superiority and willing to support the war effort."

### The Southwest Pacific (Papua, New Guinea, Solomons)

European settlers and colonial officials fled before the Japanese advance, "leaving local
people to face the new occupation force. The abandonment made a lasting impression, cemented
when incoming Japanese emphasized their own power by humiliating or executing POWs and other
remaining whites." Initial Japanese commanders paid for goods and labour, "implemented
pro-Japanese schooling and propaganda, and dismantled colonial and Christian authority" —
but by September 1942 the Japanese were in retreat and relations deteriorated sharply, with
"confiscations, forced labour, and reprisals against any sign of aiding the enemy."

Allied forces in the Melanesian region numbered "at least a million Americans, nearly half a
million Australians, and smaller numbers from New Zealand, Fiji, Tonga, and other Pacific
Islands, supported by tens of thousands of civilian Indigenous labourers." In New Guinea,
"thousands of people [moved] from combat zones or base construction areas"; food supplies
were reduced for years after. New Guinea, Bougainville, New Britain, New Ireland, and parts
of the Solomons "saw significant population drops, as the vulnerable died from malnutrition
and disease, and fewer children were born."

### The Central Pacific (Micronesia)

The Allied strategy called for a US Navy-led "second road to Tokyo" through the central
Pacific, starting with Tarawa (November 1943), then Kwajalein, Eniwetok, and Majuro
(early 1944). "Nearly 200 Marshallese died at Kwajalein, where some who had been employed
by the Japanese military fought alongside them."

Islands not invaded — Truk/Chuuk Lagoon, Maloelap, Mili, and Babelthuap — "were heavily
bombed and blockaded into starvation, conditions that in some places lasted for the eighteen
months until the end of war." In Palau, some 5,700 Micronesians lived under siege alongside
29,700 Japanese Army and Navy personnel. Nauruans deported to Chuuk (1943) "suffered
extreme privation … the Japanese regarded them as enemies."

The Chamorros of Guam, the only pre-war US territory in Micronesia, "maintained strong
passive resistance during the occupation, despite an intensive Japanization programme." On
the eve of the American invasion (July 1944), Japanese forces committed "killings, rapes,
and several massacres." After liberation, Guam's rapid development of airfields and harbours
"signalled a change in Guam's relationship with the USA, as military landholdings, employment,
and governance dominated civilian life." Planes from Guam, Saipan, and Tinian facilitated
the bombing runs of August 1945, including the Hiroshima and Nagasaki atomic bombings
launched from Tinian.

### Islander Labour

"The aspect of war that affected the largest number of Islanders was military labour."
ANGAU in New Guinea employed more than 45,000 workers at peak, with in some villages "80
per cent or more of the men … taken." Solomon Islands Labour Corps men "organized strikes
in response to combat conditions and low wages." Pacific carriers on the Kokoda Track campaign
— carrying supplies to the front and bringing out wounded — faced "overwork, lack of food,
exposure to the elements, and disease," with hundreds dying of disease and accident. Allied
military records note carriers' "crucial role" in Papua-New Guinea: Japanese movements were
"limited by scarcity of carriers … as their relationships with local people deteriorated."

The Free French Pacific Battalion totalled by end of 1943 "1,200 Europeans, nearly 1,600
Melanesians, and 300 Tahitians." The Fiji Defence Force and Solomon Islands Defence Force
served in the Solomons. As many as a thousand Islanders trained as coastwatchers along
New Guinea and the Solomons. The 28th (Māori) Battalion — never short of recruits, all-
volunteer throughout — "completed the war as New Zealand's most-decorated battalion," with
the Victoria Cross awarded posthumously to 2nd Lt. Moana-Nui-a-Kiwa Ngarimu at Tunisia.
"The service of more than 8,500 Fijian men in the Fiji Force helped Fiji emerge from colonial
subordination" (Ravuvu).

### Racial Dynamics

The war transformed race relations in profound ways. Japanese conquest had "dissolved the
myth of white superiority." Meeting Black American servicemen "as wealthy and skilled as
whites offered Islanders a view of a future in which skin colour did not determine the limits
of occupation, wages, or status." Colonial authorities "worried that the friendly and generous
Americans would cause discontent and pressure for political change after the war." Some groups
in the British Solomons and the Gilberts "expressed a desire to have the USA as the government."

### Physical Destruction and Aftermath

"Repeated bombing, base construction, and ground combat destroyed tree crops, gardens, taro
pits, and fishing areas." Peleliu's civilians were evacuated before the US invasion, but
"the damage to their home island was severe and irrecoverable." Residents returned to "a
barren landscape made dangerous by unexploded ordnance, with nothing left of the five
villages, the community houses, piers, boathouses, and cemeteries they had known." Unexploded
ordnance continued killing and wounding into the twenty-first century. As late as 2009,
President Toribiong of Palau requested UN help for clearing Peleliu ordnance.

### Decolonisation Trajectories

Whereas colonial territories occupied by Japan reverted to their "owners," Japan's Micronesian
possessions became a US strategic trust territory, with US Navy military government in place
until 1951. "On Guam, 1,350 families lost their land and homes to military use by 1947.
Bikini and Enewetak became sites of nuclear weapons testing (1946–58) with damaging and
virtually eternal harm to the people, lands, and seas." Related: [[pacific-nuclear-testing]].

Most British Pacific Islands moved towards independence; French holdings became more integrated
into the metropole. New Zealand expanded formal education and localised services in Western
Samoa, which gained independence in 1962. Australia's PNG policy shifted given the "Fuzzy
Wuzzy Angel" image of New Guinea carriers; PNG gained independence in 1975.

Bennett and Poyer conclude: "it is no small irony that the island states' diplomatic and
economic trump card in this ocean of 'peace' has been and remains their potential value in
other nations' wars and preparations for war."

### Memory and Forgetting

"The major combatants, victors and losers, victims and heroes, are commemorated at
battlefields, cemeteries, and war memorials … [but] for Islanders, this was 'someone else's
war.'" Japanese bone-collecting expeditions have continued across Pacific islands since the
1950s. On Guam, Liberation Day (July 1944) expresses strong US patriotism; in the Northern
Marianas, which was part of the Japanese Empire, commemoration has been "problematic."
Issues of collaboration — Chamorro interpreters who worked for Japanese on Guam, "women of
war" in brothels — are "not yet dealt with in official narratives or public commemorations:
making history 'as much about forgetting as it is about remembering.'"

War tourism has become significant: Chuuk (Truk) Lagoon is now a world-famous diving site
for shipwrecks; Bikini Atoll nuclear test range is a UN World Heritage Site; the Kokoda
Trail is "sacred ground" to Australians whose interests shape land use along the trail.

## Historiography

Accounts of the Pacific War have traditionally been written as an operational and
strategic contest between the United States and Imperial Japan — the "great battles"
narrative of Midway, Guadalcanal, Leyte Gulf and the island-hopping campaign. Bennett
and Poyer (in [[hattori-samson-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v2-2023|*The Cambridge History
of the Pacific Ocean*, Vol. 2]]) represent a now-dominant revisionist current that
recentres the war on the Islanders who lived in the combat zones: their labour,
displacement, famine, and the lasting political consequences (accelerated
[[pacific-decolonisation|decolonisation]], new infrastructure, and the entrenchment of
US strategic basing). Their key intervention is on **memory** — the insistence that for
Pacific Islanders this was often "someone else's war," and that commemoration is "as
much about forgetting as it is about remembering," with collaboration, sexual violence,
and Indigenous suffering still poorly integrated into official narratives. This sits
alongside the Japanese-side and Allied-strategic literatures rather than replacing them;
the dispute is one of emphasis and scale (whose war, told from where) rather than of
basic fact.
