# Putumayo Atrocities

The systematic enslavement, torture, and mass killing of Indigenous peoples — above all the
Bora, Huitoto (Murui-Muinanɨ), Andoke, and Ocaina — in the Putumayo region of the upper
Amazon by the Peruvian Amazon Company (the Casa Arana of Julio César Arana) during the
height of the [[amazon-rubber-boom|rubber boom]], c. 1900–1912.

## Narrative

To meet the labor demand of wild-rubber extraction, the company ran a regime of
[[debt-peonage]] enforced by armed overseers (*muchachos* and Barbadian recruits): floggings,
mutilation, starvation, and murder produced a demographic catastrophe in which tens of
thousands of Indigenous people died. Exposure came through the journalist Walter Hardenburg
and an investigation led by the British consul **Roger Casement** (1910–1911), whose report
to a British parliamentary inquiry made the Putumayo an international scandal.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[amazon-rubber-boom]] — the extractive frontier economy and its insatiable
  labor demand.
- `caused_by:` [[debt-peonage]] — the *habilitación* system that bound and brutalized
  workers.
- `contributed_to:` weak state presence in a contested, ill-defined borderland.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` near-destruction of several Putumayo peoples and lasting demographic collapse.
- `enabled:` an early landmark in international humanitarian and (later) Indigenous-rights
  advocacy; the scandal damaged the rubber companies even before Asian plantation rubber
  collapsed Amazonian prices.

## Actors

The Casa Arana (Peruvian Amazon Company) · Roger Casement and the British inquiry · the
Bora, Huitoto, Andoke, and Ocaina peoples.

## Historiography

The Putumayo became a touchstone case in the study of frontier violence and "the political
economy of terror" (Michael Taussig), debated as both an aberration and a logical extreme of
the rubber labor regime.
