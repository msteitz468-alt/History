## Narrative

The first humans to reach the Caribbean islands arrived in the fifth millennium BCE. The oldest radiocarbon dates come from the Vignier III site in Haiti (4510–4350 BCE), the Angostura and Maruca sites in Puerto Rico (c. 4000 BCE), and an uncalibrated date of 3220 BCE for the Levisa site in Cuba. All of the earliest sites are located on Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico. Jamaica, despite abundant raw materials for flaked-stone tools and over 150 years of archaeological prospecting, shows no evidence of human occupation before approximately AD 500 — an absence Keegan and Hofman attribute to maritime conditions rather than archaeologists' failure to find sites.

Keegan and Hofman collapse the traditional Lithic Age / Archaic Age distinction (based on the transition from flaked-stone to ground-stone technologies) into a single **Archaic Age**, arguing that flaked-stone tools persisted throughout the entire precolonial sequence and that pottery — traditionally the defining marker of the Ceramic Age — was in use at Archaic Age sites as early as 2600 BCE. The "three Ages = three migrations" model underlying Rouse's framework is substantially rejected on current evidence.

### Flaked-Stone Complexes (from c. 5000 BCE)

The oldest Caribbean sites are characterized by flaked-stone macroblades struck from prismatic cores of fine-grained chert (Cuba, Hispaniola) or chalcedony (Puerto Rico). The diagnostic tool is the unretouched macroblade, sometimes backed and tanged. These were not big-game hunting tools — no large-game remains have been found in Caribbean sites, and there was no megafauna comparable to continental settings. Tools were likely used for woodworking, forest penetration, and processing perishable materials.

The type assemblage is from the **Barrera-Mordán sites** in the south-central Dominican Republic (c. 4000 BCE), a cluster of four named sites (Barrera, Mordán, Casimira, Las Alejandrinas) within 100 meters of each other. These are lithic workshops or quarries — they do not exhibit evidence of permanent habitation. The quality of chert raw material proved attractive for hundreds of years, making chronological assignment from lithic type alone unreliable. Strata at the site also contained pottery, complicating the clean sequence.

Rouse (1992) classified these assemblages as the Casimiroid series with three subseries: Casimiran Casimiroid (Cuba through Hispaniola, 4000–2000 BCE), Redondan Casimiroid (Cuba, 2000 BCE to AD 500), and Courian Casimiroid (all Hispaniola, 2000 BCE to AD 500). These chronological slots were assigned to fill the taxonomy rather than derived from established dates.

### Ground-Stone Complexes and the Archaic Age Proper (from c. 3000 BCE)

Ground-stone tools — pestles, mortars, manos, grooved axes, stone balls — appear across the Greater Antilles and northern Lesser Antilles from about 3000 BCE. The conventional view attributed this to a separate migration of Ortoiroid peoples from Trinidad carrying this technology north through the Lesser Antilles. This view has been substantially undermined:

1. There are **no definite Archaic Age sites in the Windward Islands** south of Guadeloupe, despite systematic surveys — the route the migration was supposed to take.
2. Tool distributions suggest **west-to-east movement**, not the expected south-to-north direction.
3. Richard Callaghan's voyaging simulations show that **direct crossing of the Caribbean Sea** from South or Central America is not only feasible but easier than navigating between Lesser Antilles islands.
4. Ground-stone tool technology is so simple it may have developed independently or arrived through exchange rather than population movement.

The **Banwari Trace site** in Trinidad (c. 5000 BCE) is the oldest Archaic Age site excavated in the Caribbean region. Located on a hill above wetlands in southwestern Trinidad, it is a shell midden approximately 30 meters in diameter and over 2 meters tall. Tools include ground-stone pestles, mortars, manos, anvils, and grooved axes — many shaped through use from water-rounded stones. The site shows a shift from terrestrial to marine resources through time, partly reflecting Trinidad's separation from the mainland as the Gulf of Paría opened. One small potsherd was found on the surface; a second in upper excavation levels. Starch-grain analysis at the nearby St. John site revealed evidence for maize as early as 7,700 BP.

### Archaic Age Pottery (from c. 2600 BCE)

The "Pre-Arawak Pottery Horizon" — small quantities of pottery in sites with predominantly Archaic Age tool kits — has been documented across Cuba (earliest date 2160 BCE), the Dominican Republic (El Curro site, 1450 BCE), Haiti (Source Pascade II site, 1090 BCE), Puerto Rico (Angostura site, 2700–1800 BCE; Paso del Indio, 2630 BCE), and St. Thomas (870 BCE).

The pottery is distinctive: small to medium globular bowls and boat-shaped (navicular) vessels, manufactured by coiling, usually under 1 cm thick. Vessel shapes mimic gourds (cross-cutting a gourd yields a round vessel; a transverse cut yields an oval). Decoration includes incised, punctate, and modeled designs, and paint in red, pink, white, and black. Unlike later Saladoid pottery, which has a highly formalized grammar, Archaic Age pottery represents a period of experimentation. Rodríguez Ramos (2010) argues these vessels replaced gourd containers for serving wet foods in ceremonial and social contexts — pots as tools performing functions previously served by other containers.

Starch-grain analysis of Archaic Age grinding tools (Pagán Jiménez 2013) found evidence for virtually all the cultigens traditionally attributed to Ceramic Age introductions — including maize, sweet potatoes, and manioc — present in Puerto Rico by at least 1300 BCE.

### European Encounters with Archaic Communities

It has generally been believed that Archaic Age practices survived in western Cuba until after Columbus's arrival (1492–1493). This belief is based on reports by Bartolomé de las Casas, who identified the Guanahatabey (Guanahacabibes) of western Cuba as distinct from other Cuban societies. However, las Casas and Velázquez apparently never visited the western part of the island; their reports lack direct observation. Present archaeological investigations find no Ceramic Age materials in western Cuba, but the negative evidence is not conclusive. The Guanahatabey may represent the terminal phase of Archaic Age communities (Kozlowski's "Carnero Culture") or may reflect ceramic communities whose traces have not been found.

## Causal Analysis

The initial colonization of unoccupied islands requires no cause beyond the capacity for open-water voyaging and motivation (population pressure, resource competition, exploration). The technology of choice was dugout canoes without sails — Callaghan (2001) concluded that rafts would be carried away by winds and currents, and the Spanish reported no sails in the Caribbean at contact. The most likely source area for the earliest flaked-stone complex colonists is the Isthmo-Colombian region (northwestern South America / Central America), based on lithic comparisons and Callaghan's voyaging simulations, though Belize also shows material culture affinities.

## Consequence Analysis

The first colonists encountered islands that were significantly different from today: coastal marine habitats were recently formed and transitional; eustatic sea level was still stabilizing. The Greater Antilles between 7000 and 5000 BCE experienced warmer and moister climates than the preceding Pleistocene, and forests had replaced dry-adapted woodlands.

Large and unwary terrestrial fauna — giant flightless owls, tortoises, sloths, iguanas, manatees, monk seals, crocodiles, and diverse rodents — were heavily exploited. Cunningham (1997) estimated that 90% of the fauna of the Greater Antilles went extinct shortly after humans arrived. The sloth and manatee were likely extirpated by Archaic Age hunters.

Increased charcoal in sediment cores from multiple islands (Puerto Rico, Haiti) from c. 5300 years ago onward signals intensive anthropogenic burning of large tracts of land, transforming natural habitats into anthropogenic landscapes before the Ceramic Age began.

## Actors

The Archaic Age peoples of the Caribbean are known only through their material remains — lithic workshops, shell middens, ground-stone tool assemblages, and scattered pottery. They were organized in small, mobile communities (Rouse's reconstruction of band-level organization based on ethnographic analogy with South American lowland groups), though recent investigations at Maruca and Angostura (Puerto Rico) demonstrate that at least some communities had a more sedentary existence and more complex social organization than the band model assumes. Seasonal mobility between resource concentrations appears to have been common, but long-term ties to particular locations are also documented (e.g., recurrent abandonment and reoccupation at Plum Piece, Saba).

## Historiography

The earliest Caribbean inhabitants have received less archaeological attention than Ceramic Age communities, in part because most comprehensive studies were conducted by Cuban and Dominican archaeologists working within a historical-materialist framework — their work published in Spanish and thus largely excluded from North American synthetic literature.

The traditional framework of three distinct ages (Lithic, Archaic, Ceramic) reflecting three separate migrations is being dismantled by: (1) calibrated radiocarbon dating showing chronological overlap where discrete sequences were assumed; (2) Archaic Age pottery documented across multiple islands well before the Ceramic Age; (3) starch-grain evidence for Archaic Age cultivation of Ceramic Age-attributed cultigens; (4) the absence of Archaic Age (Ortoiroid) sites in the southern Lesser Antilles — the supposed migration corridor; and (5) voyaging simulations showing that direct Caribbean crossing was easier than island-hopping through the Windwards.

The continuing challenge is that most excavated sites are lithic workshops lacking the full range of cultural activities, making generalization difficult. Faunal remains are poorly preserved. Dating is complicated by mis-cross-dating, marine reservoir effects, and selective reporting.
