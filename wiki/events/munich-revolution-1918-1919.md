# Munich Revolution and Räterepublik (November 1918 – May 1919)

## Narrative

The German Revolution reached Bavaria early. On 7 November 1918 the House of Wittelsbach (rulers for over seven centuries) fell; Kurt Eisner (Jewish, Independent Social Democrat/radical) became Minister President of a provisional National Council government. The barracks Hitler returned to on 21 November were already under soldiers' councils. By spring 1919, after Eisner's assassination (21 February), Bavarian politics descended into chaos. In April a Soviet-style councils republic (Räterepublik) was proclaimed in Munich; for its last two weeks it was under direct Communist leadership looking to Moscow.

The "liberation" of Munich by Reichswehr troops and Freikorps formations in late April–early May 1919 was accompanied by bloody repression. Hitler, still in the army, witnessed the turmoil at close quarters in Munich (epicentre) and was later involved in the regimental commission examining "revolutionary occurrences" — his "first more or less purely political activity."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the broader German collapse (armistice request 3 Oct, Wilson notes demanding removal of autocratic rulers, mutinies at Kiel and Wilhelmshaven, spontaneous soldiers'/workers' councils). In Bavaria: long-standing particularism + war weariness + hunger + the sudden removal of the monarchy. `contributed_to:` the weakness of moderate forces and the rapid radicalization to USPD then Communist leadership after Eisner's death.

`preceded_by:` [[events/hitler-pasewalk-1918]] and the armistice.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the traumatic environment in which Hitler returned as a 29-year-old without prospects and resolved on a political mission. The "passing rule of the Jews" (Eisner, later Räterepublik leaders) became a lifelong fixation.

`enabled:` Hitler's retention by the Reichswehr as informant/educator against Bolshevism; the Lechfeld speeches; attendance at the DAP meeting. The bloody "liberation" also seeded the Freikorps/paramilitary culture that later fed the SA and the 1923 putsch milieu.

`followed_by:` [[events/hitler-dap-entry-1919]], rise of völkisch right in Bavaria, [[beer-hall-putsch-1923]].

Hitler's own *Mein Kampf* account is "mere page," laconic, and "has the air of a fabrication" (claims he "did nothing," invented arrest story). The gulf between the drama and his later minimization suggests deliberate obfuscation of any embarrassing role.

## Historiography

Kershaw (Range 01, Ch. 4) emphasizes the "messy, largely spontaneous and uncoordinated" character of the revolution nationally and locally; it arose from disaffection and demand for peace/end to misery, not solely "hard-core left-wing revolutionaries." The Räterepublik was short and chaotic. Hitler's minimization in *Mein Kampf* is highlighted as suspicious; patchy evidence suggests he remained in Munich through much of the period. The events "deeply scarred his psyche."
