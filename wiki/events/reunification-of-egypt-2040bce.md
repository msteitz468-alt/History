# Reunification of Egypt under Mentuhotpe II

The military reunification of Upper and Lower Egypt under the Theban king
[[mentuhotpe-ii|Nebhepetre Mentuhotpe II]] (c. 2040 BCE), ending the
[[first-intermediate-period|First Intermediate Period]] and founding the
[[middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]]. Egyptian tradition ranked it with the
[[unification-of-egypt-3100bce|original unification]] and the New Kingdom's start:
a Nineteenth-Dynasty temple list names **Menes, Nebhepetre, and Amosis** together
as the founders of the Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XX, W. C. Hayes).

## Narrative

By the late Eleventh Dynasty the Theban [[mentuhotpe-ii|Mentuhotpe II]] had
inherited a southern kingdom reaching to Abydos. In his **fourteenth regnal year**
a "rebellion of This" — an attempt by Heracleopolitan supporters to recover the key
Thinite nome — reopened the war. This time the Theban offensive was decisive:
Asyut, the loyal northern bastion of the Heracleopolitans, fell; the fighting moved
through Middle Egypt; and around 2040 BCE **Heracleopolis itself fell** and the Two
Lands were reunited under one crown. Mentuhotpe marked the achievement in his
titulary, changing his Horus name first to **Netjeryhedjet** and finally to
**Sematawy — "Uniter of the Two Lands."** A mass grave of **sixty soldiers** slain
attacking a fortress, found above his Deir el-Bahri temple, is a vivid relic of the
wars of the reign — "the best preserved of all Middle Kingdom bodies." The
[[sources/shaw-oxford-history-ancient-egypt-2000|Oxford History]] (Callender) adds that the
Herakleopolitan king **Merykara died before** Mentuhotpe reached his capital, and that
recent excavations at the Ihnasya el-Medina (Herakleopolis) cemetery show its funerary
monuments "were literally hacked to pieces" in the early Middle Kingdom — physical evidence
of the victors' settling of scores.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** the long [[first-intermediate-period|Theban–Heracleopolitan
  conflict]] over the Thinite nome, reignited by the "rebellion of This."
- **contributed_to:** Theban military energy and the solid southern loyalty kindled
  when the Heracleopolitans earlier desecrated the holy Thinite cemeteries (a
  blunder their own *Instruction for Merykare* laments).
- The collapse of central authority that made the conflict possible was itself the
  legacy of the [[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|structural fall of the Old Kingdom]].

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** the [[middle-kingdom-egypt|Middle Kingdom]] — a re-centralized
  Egyptian state, governed from Thebes by appointed Theban officials, that
  recovered the prosperity, art, and monumental building of the Old Kingdom.
- **enabled:** the suppression (begun here, completed by [[senwosret-iii|Senwosret
  III]]) of the hereditary nomarchies, and the renewed reconquest of **Lower
  Nubia**.
- **produced:** the elevation of the Theban war-god **Mont**, and soon **Amun**, to
  the head of the Egyptian pantheon — the cult that would dominate Egypt for the
  next millennium and a half.

## Actors

[[mentuhotpe-ii|Nebhepetre Mentuhotpe II]] and the Theban Eleventh Dynasty; the
Heracleopolitan kingdom (its last king unnamed); the nomarchs of **Asyut**
(irreconcilable, removed by force) and **Hermopolis/Beni Hasan** (conciliated and
left in place).

## Historiography

The reunification is reconstructed almost entirely from **provincial tomb
biographies and royal monuments**, not narrative history — the Theban king-lists,
the Inyotef stelae, the *Instruction for Merykare* (from the losing side), and the
graffiti of Mentuhotpe's officials. The exact sequence and the precise regnal year
of Heracleopolis's fall are inferred. Dates follow the conventional Egyptian
chronology of CAH (1971) and are approximate. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
