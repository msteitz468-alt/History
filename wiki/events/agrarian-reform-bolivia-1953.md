# Bolivian Agrarian Reform of 1953

The agrarian reform decreed on 2 August 1953 in the wake of the Bolivian National
Revolution of 1952, which abolished the servile labor obligations of the
[[hacienda-system|hacienda]] and redistributed estate land to the indigenous and mestizo
peasantry. It was one of the most far-reaching land reforms in twentieth-century Latin
America and a turning point in the relationship between Andean indigenous peoples and the
nation-state.

## Narrative

The 1952 revolution, led by the Movimiento Nacionalista Revolucionario (MNR), rested in
part on armed peasant and miner mobilization. Universal suffrage (ending literacy
restrictions that had excluded most indigenous people) was followed by the 1953 reform,
which broke up highland estates, ended *pongueaje* (unpaid personal service), and organized
the rural population into peasant unions (*sindicatos*).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the 1952 revolution and the peasant militancy that underwrote it.
- `contributed_to:` long-standing pressure on the [[hacienda-system]] and indigenous
  community land loss across the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` dissolution of the highland hacienda and a re-peasantization of land tenure.
- `enabled:` the rise of a unionized, politically mobilized peasantry — the ground from which
  later [[katarismo|Katarista]] and indigenous movements grew, even as the reform's
  assimilationist *campesino* (peasant) framing later drew indigenous critique.

## Actors

The MNR state · highland peasant unions · the displaced landlord class.

## Historiography

The reform is debated as both a genuine social revolution and an instrument of state
incorporation that recast indigenous people as a deethnicized "peasantry" — a framing
[[katarismo]] would later contest.
