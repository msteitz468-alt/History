# Battle of Nineveh (627)

The **Battle of Nineveh** (December 12, 627) was the decisive engagement of the Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628. The Byzantine victory broke the power of the Sasanian dynasty, ended the long-running war, and restored the pre-war borders. However, the mutual exhaustion of the two empires left both highly vulnerable to the subsequent [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Arab-Muslim conquests]].

## Narrative

In 627, Emperor [[heraclius|Heraclius]] launched a daring counter-offensive into the Sasanian heartland of Mesopotamia. Leading a multi-ethnic force, including a large contingent of Göktürk allies, Heraclius invaded Assyria. The Sasanian general Rhahzadh pursued the Byzantine army with a hastily raised force.

The two armies clashed near the ruins of ancient Nineveh on December 12, 627. The battle was fought in dense fog, reducing the effectiveness of Persian archery and leading to hand-to-hand combat. According to traditional accounts, Heraclius fought personally in the vanguard, killing Rhahzadh in single combat. The Byzantine forces routed the Persians, who retreated to the royal palace at Dastagird.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[successors-of-justinian|Sasanian-Roman War of 602–628]]`: The battle was the culmination of Heraclius's long campaign to force the Persian court to negotiate.
- `contributed_to: [[heraclius|Heraclius's Offensive Strategy]]`: Heraclius's bold decision to bypass Sasanian strongholds in Anatolia and march directly into the Mesopotamian plain forced the Sasanians to fight a defensive action on their own territory.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[heraclius|Sasanian Collapse]]`: Following the battle, Heraclius plundered the Sasanian royal palace at Dastagird. King Chosroes II was overthrown and executed by his son, Kavadh II, who immediately sued for peace. The Sasanians returned all conquered Byzantine territories (Syria, Palestine, and Egypt) and restored the True Cross.
- `contributed_to: [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Arab-Muslim Conquests]]`: The war left both the Byzantine and Sasanian states financially ruined, demographically depleted, and militarily exhausted. When the [[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Arab-Muslim armies]] invaded in the 630s, the Sasanians collapsed entirely within a decade, and the Byzantines lost their newly restored southern provinces.

## Historiography

Contemporary Byzantine accounts are found in the *Chronicon Paschale* and the poems of George of Pisidia, alongside later accounts by Theophanes the Confessor. Modern historians (such as Andrew Louth) note that the battle highlights the strategic brilliance of Heraclius, who took great risks by marching into the Sasanian heartland but succeeded in ending centuries of Sasanian-Roman rivalry.
