# The Gundovald Affair

The **Gundovald affair** (c. 582–585) was the attempt of a pretender — **Gundovald**,
who claimed to be an unrecognised son of **[[chlothar-i|Chlothar I]]** — to seize a kingdom
in southern Gaul with Byzantine backing and the support of disaffected Frankish dukes. It
is the central political episode of Books VII–VIII of
**[[gregory-of-tours-history-of-the-franks-thorpe-1974|Gregory of Tours']]** *Histories*
and the fullest case study available of a Merovingian succession crisis, royal legitimacy,
and the limits of kingship.

## Narrative

Gundovald had been raised in the East and "came from Constantinople" at the invitation of
**Guntram Boso**, who "had visited Constantinople with the express purpose of inviting
Gundovald to come to Gaul" — Byzantine money and policy stood behind the bid (see
[[byzantine-empire]]). King **[[guntram|Guntram]]** scorned his claim, sneering that "this
man had two fathers, a weaver and a miller!"

The death of **[[chilperic-i|Chilperic I]]** in 584 created the opening. The dukes
**Mummolus** (Guntram's former patrician) and **Desiderius** harboured and promoted
Gundovald, who "was raised up as King on a shield" at Brives-la-Gaillarde — and, in an omen
Gregory relishes, "as they carried him round for the third time, he stumbled." He seized
the late Chilperic's daughter Rigunth's treasure, took oaths at Angoulême and Périgueux,
and forced his way into **Toulouse**, where Mummolus "boxed the [bishop's] ears." His chief
allies were the warrior-bishop **Sagittarius** (promised the see of Toulouse) and the dukes
Bladast and Waddo.

Guntram raised "the whole army of his realm" under the command of **Leudegisel** and
besieged the rebels in **Comminges (Convenae)** from the beginning of Lent 585. Gundovald
had tricked the townsfolk into sheltering his army and then locked them out. As the siege
ground on, his own captains — Mummolus, Sagittarius, Chariulf and Waddo — swore "they would
break their alliance with Gundovald and hand him over." Lured outside the gate, Gundovald
was pushed into a ravine; "Boso threw a rock at him and hit him full in the head. Gundovald
fell and as he fell he died." His body was abused, dragged through the camp, and left
unburied. **Comminges was then sacked**, its inhabitants slaughtered and the town burned,
"leaving there nothing but the bare earth." Despite their oaths, **Mummolus was run through**
by Leudegisel's men and **Sagittarius beheaded** as he fled.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[chilperic-i]]` — Chilperic's assassination (584) removed the Neustrian
  king and created the southern vacancy the pretender exploited.
- `contributed_to: [[byzantine-empire]]` — Constantinople financed and dispatched Gundovald
  as an instrument against Frankish power, making this also an episode of Byzantine western
  policy.
- `contributed_to: [[merovingian-franks]]` — the structural fragility of Merovingian
  succession, in which an unrecognised royal son and ambitious dukes could mount a credible
  kingship, enabled the revolt.
- `concurrent_with: [[brunhild]]` — Guntram suspected Brunhild of complicity; she later
  "cleared herself of the accusation on oath," but the affair deepened inter-court mistrust.

## Consequence Analysis

The crushing of the revolt **consolidated Guntram's authority** over Aquitaine and the
south and eliminated a generation of over-mighty dukes (Mummolus, Desiderius's circle). It
intensified suspicion of **[[brunhild|Brunhild]]** and her Austrasian court, feeding the
diplomacy that produced the **Treaty of Andelot (587)**. The episcopate was purged of
Gundovald's partisans at the **Council of Mâcon** — Faustianus of Dax deposed, Ursicinus of
Cahors given three years' penance — demonstrating the Church's entanglement in the dynastic
wars (see [[merovingian-episcopate]]).

## Actors

- **[[guntram]]** — suppressed the revolt; arbiter of the Frankish south.
- **[[chilperic-i]]** — his death triggered the crisis.
- **[[childebert-ii]]** / **[[brunhild]]** — the Austrasian court, suspected of complicity.
- **[[byzantine-empire]]** — the pretender's sponsor.
- **[[merovingian-episcopate]]** — bishops on both sides, judged at Mâcon.

## Historiography

Gregory is the principal source and an interested one: hostile to the rebels, partial to
Guntram, and silent on much of the Byzantine dimension, which modern scholarship has
reconstructed from coinage and Eastern sources. The affair is treated as a key test case in
the secondary literature on Merovingian kingship and legitimacy
([[geary-before-france-germany-1988|Geary]];
[[effros-moreira-oxford-handbook-merovingian-world-2020|the Oxford Handbook]]), especially
on whether shield-raising and royal descent, rather than recognition by the existing kings,
could confer legitimacy.
