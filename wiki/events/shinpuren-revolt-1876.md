# Shinpūren Revolt (1876)

The Shinpūren revolt (*Shinpūren no ran*, also called the Keishin-tō uprising) was a short-lived but dramatic samurai insurrection in Kumamoto, Kyūshū, on the night of October 24–25, 1876. Led by **Ōtaguro Tomoo** (1835–1876) and some 180 men of the radical anti-Westernization *Shinpūren* faction, it was the first of three near-simultaneous samurai revolts in the autumn of 1876 — the others being the Akizuki uprising in Fukuoka and the Hagi revolt in Yamaguchi — and served as the immediate trigger for the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877.

## The Shinpūren

The Shinpūren (*Keishin-tō*, loosely "Righteous Wind Association") was the most extreme of several factions among disaffected samurai in Kumamoto. Their ideology was radical Shintō purism combined with visceral hostility to every aspect of Western influence. Keene's account (from [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]) captures their ethos:

> "They were not satisfied merely with arresting the spread of Western influence; they were determined to eradicate every trace of it, whether the wearing of Western clothes or the use of the Western calendar. Some members displayed their hatred of electricity, for example, by holding white fans over their heads when they had to pass under telegraph wires, in order to protect themselves from baleful foreign influences. Many carried salt to sprinkle (by way of purification) whenever they saw a Buddhist priest, a Japanese in Western clothes, or a funeral. And one man, convinced that paper money was imitated from the West, refused to touch it lest he be polluted and accepted money only with chopsticks."

Fatally for their military effectiveness, the Shinpūren also refused to use modern weapons. They fought with swords and spears against soldiers equipped with rifles and cannon.

## The Attack

After Ōtaguro consulted Shintō divination and declared he had received divine authorization, fewer than 200 Shinpūren gathered secretly on the night of October 24. They divided into squads targeting the Kumamoto garrison, the telegraph office, and the residences of the prefectural governor and senior military officers. Their initial attack achieved near-complete surprise: they killed the garrison commandant Major General Taneda Masaaki, mortally wounded the prefectural governor Yasuoka Ryōsuke, and burned his house. More than 300 garrison soldiers were killed or wounded in the initial assault.

The massacre was indiscriminate: "the attack on the barracks, completely without warning, caught the unarmed soldiers in their nightclothes, but this did not induce the Shinpūren to capture rather than kill. They showed no mercy even toward men who were too badly wounded to defend themselves." Keene notes the class dimension: "It seems to have given the Shinpūren samurai special pleasure to kill lowly peasants who had dared to usurp their place as military men."

Once the army officers recovered from the shock, modern weapons overwhelmed the sword-armed rebels. Ōtaguro, badly wounded, commanded his followers to decapitate him, and they did. Most survivors committed *seppuku*. By dawn the revolt was over.

## Significance

The Shinpūren revolt had no lasting political effect — it achieved nothing, in Keene's blunt assessment, "except the deaths of some 500 men who might otherwise have been of service to their country." But it demonstrated "that it was possible for a small body of men, if their attack was unforeseen and they themselves were ready to risk their lives, to defeat much larger forces or at least to reduce them to a state of terror. This lesson of terrorism was communicated to samurai dissidents throughout Japan, some of whom soon demonstrated that they also were prepared to start a rebellion with a mere handful of men" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

The revolt occurred at the same moment that the Kumamoto garrison had been weakened by the dispatch of troops to suppress the [[saga-rebellion-1874|Saga Rebellion]] — an operational detail not lost on dissidents elsewhere. Within days, the Akizuki samurai (fewer than 200 men, rallying under the banner *hōkoku*, "serve the nation") and Maebara Issei's Hagi revolt (about 100 men) launched near-simultaneous uprisings in sympathy.

All three were quickly suppressed; their leaders were executed on December 3, 1876. But the pattern — small groups, suicide-readiness, targeting of government officials, coordination across domains — pointed directly toward Kagoshima, where Saigō Takamori's private military schools were already training thousands.

## Historiography

The Shinpūren has attracted attention disproportionate to its military significance because of the extremity of its ideology. The faction represents the logical terminus of *sonnō jōi* purism — not anti-Western in the way a modernizing nationalist might be, but anti-modern in a genuinely radical sense, seeking to eradicate not just foreign domination but the material traces of foreign contact. Their refusal to use modern weapons was ideologically consistent and militarily suicidal. Keene's account warns against the romanticism that surrounds the Sakurayama Shrine graves: "visitors who stand before these tombs today may be so impressed by the dedication displayed on behalf of a doomed cause as to forget that the attack was brutal in the extreme and that the ideals for which the young men died were insensate."
