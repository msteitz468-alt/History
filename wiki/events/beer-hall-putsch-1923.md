# Beer Hall Putsch (1923)

## Narrative

The Beer Hall Putsch (also known as the Hitler Putsch or Munich Putsch) was a failed armed coup attempt by the NSDAP and allied paramilitary groups (the Kampfbund) on 8–9 November 1923 in Munich. It marked the climax of the 1923 crisis in Bavaria and the turning point that redirected the Nazi movement from putschism toward the 'legal' path to power.

In the explosive atmosphere of hyperinflation, French occupation of the Ruhr, and Bavarian hostility to the Berlin government, Hitler had positioned himself as the 'drummer' of the nationalist Right. 'I am nothing but a drummer and rallier,' he told Arthur Moeller van den Bruck in 1922. He repeatedly told audiences he was paving the way for a coming dictator: 'Our task is to give the dictator, when he comes, a people ready for him!' (speech, 4 May 1923). The SA had grown rapidly to several thousand; the Kampfbund united the SA with Bund Oberland, Reichsflagge and others under military head Hermann Kriebel, with Scheubner-Richter as business manager. Röhm supplied weapons from secret Reichswehr stocks.

The Bavarian triumvirate (Minister President Gustav Ritter von Kahr as General State Commissar, Reichswehr commander General Otto von Lossow, and police chief Colonel Hans von Seißer) had themselves flirted with a 'march on Berlin' but repeatedly pulled back. Kahr warned the patriotic associations against independent action. On 7 November 1923 Hitler decided to force their hand. He chose the evening of 8 November, when Kahr was addressing a large meeting in the Bürgerbräukeller on the fifth anniversary of the November Revolution.

Around 8:30 p.m., Hitler's stormtroopers surrounded the hall. Hitler entered with armed bodyguards, fired a pistol shot into the ceiling, and announced: 'The national revolution has broken out... The Bavarian government is deposed.' He forced Kahr, Lossow and Seißer into an adjoining room, declared a new Reich government with himself at its head, Ludendorff in charge of the army, and the triumvirate in key posts. 'If things went wrong, he had four bullets in his pistol – three for his collaborators, the last for himself.'

After about an hour, the triumvirate were brought back to the podium. Kahr announced (under duress) he would serve as regent for the monarchy. Ludendorff arrived in uniform. The mood in the hall swung to euphoria. But once released (Ludendorff trusted their officer's word), the triumvirate renounced the agreement and mobilized against the putsch.

On 9 November the putschists marched through Munich toward the War Ministry, hoping popular acclaim and Ludendorff's presence would win over the army and police. At the Odeonsplatz a police cordon opened fire. Fourteen putschists and four policemen were killed. Scheubner-Richter, linking arms with Hitler, was shot dead; Hitler dislocated his shoulder (or was pulled down). Göring was wounded in the leg. Hitler fled, was arrested days later at Uffing, and sent to Landsberg prison.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by`: The structural crisis of 1923 — hyperinflation destroying the middle class, French Ruhr occupation inflaming nationalism, and the Weimar government's weakness — created the conditions. Bavaria's self-proclaimed role as a 'cell of order' and the toleration (and active support) of paramilitary training by Reichswehr elements under Lossow made armed action thinkable.

`contributed_to`: Hitler's personal style: 'The tendency to "go for broke" was not simply a character-trait of Hitler; it was built into the nature of his leadership, his political aims, and the party he led.' He could not keep his followers at fever pitch indefinitely without action. The example of Mussolini and pressure from SA and Kampfbund activists pushed him.

`concurrent_with`: The triumvirate's own ambiguous plotting against Berlin; without their prior collusion in arming and training the formations, 'an armed putsch would scarcely have been possible.'

`preceded_by`: The May Day 1923 fiasco, when Lossow refused to hand over weapons and the planned assault on the Left collapsed, taught Hitler he could not act without Reichswehr backing yet also that the authorities were weak.

The half-baked planning and improvisation bore Hitler's imprint: 'the dogmatic anti-Berlin stance of the ruling groups in Bavaria... contrived to save him when his "career" ought to have been over.'

## Consequence Analysis

`produced`: Immediate military and political failure. The putsch fizzled; the Kampfbund was dissolved; paramilitary activities curtailed. The völkisch vote collapsed in the December 1924 elections (NSFB down to 3%).

`produced`: Hitler's trial (26 February – 1 April 1924) became a propaganda triumph. He accepted full responsibility, glorified the attempt, and threatened to expose the triumvirate's complicity in treason. The court, heavily biased, gave him only five years (less time served) and rejected deportation. 'Hitler is a German-Austrian. He considers himself to be a German.'

`produced`: Landsberg imprisonment (November 1923 – 20 December 1924). Here Hitler dictated the first volume of *Mein Kampf*, received adulation that reinforced his messianic self-image, and made the decisive internal transition: 'Not from modesty did I want at that time to be the drummer. That is the highest there is. The rest is unimportant.' By release he had concluded he himself was the predestined Leader.

`enabled`: The 'legal' path. The failure convinced Hitler that armed seizure was premature; the movement must first win mass support and then power through constitutional means while building toward a future putsch or legal takeover. This lesson shaped the 1925 refoundation.

`enabled`: The Führer cult and mastery over the movement. The 13 months without Hitler demonstrated his indispensability; rivals (Rosenberg, Ludendorff, Graefe, Strasser factions) fragmented. On release he imposed sole leadership and the Führerprinzip.

`produced`: The myth of the 'martyrs of 9 November' and the annual commemoration that later sacralized the movement.

## Actors

- **Primary**: Adolf Hitler (political leader of the enterprise, self-appointed head of provisional Reich government); Erich Ludendorff (figurehead and military symbol); Ernst Röhm (paramilitary organizer and weapons supplier); Dr Max Erwin von Scheubner-Richter (key planner, killed on the march); Hermann Göring (SA leader).
- **Triumvirate** (initially complicit, then opponents): Kahr, Lossow, Seißer.
- **Supporting formations**: SA (under Göring), Bund Oberland (Weber), Reichsflagge.

## Historiography

Kershaw emphasises that the putsch was never merely Hitler's putsch. 'The "Hitler-Putsch" was, as we have seen, by no means merely Hitler's putsch.' Bavarian elites, Reichswehr collusion, and the broader nationalist paramilitary culture supplied the stage. The trial leniency and failure to deport 'explain the determination of the Bavarian judiciary to insist upon Hitler's early release, despite the well-reasoned opposition of the Munich police and the state prosecutor's office.'

The event sealed Hitler's personal transition from agitator/drummer to self-proclaimed Führer. It also illustrated the structural dependence of his early career on Bavarian particularism and elite miscalculation. Later Nazi historiography turned the fiasco into a heroic foundation myth; serious scholarship (Kershaw) stresses contingency, dilettantism, and the enabling role of conservative nationalists who thought they could use or contain him.

**Collection Coverage Note**: Kershaw provides the definitive modern narrative synthesis based on Goebbels diaries, speech editions, and Bavarian archival material. The putsch is treated as the necessary precondition for the later 'legal' strategy and the consolidation of charismatic leadership.

## Paxton & Hessler (2011) Corroboration

Paxton and Hessler situate the putsch within the full 1923 crisis —
Ruhr occupation, hyperinflation, and the KPD's attempted risings in Hamburg
and Thuringia — and confirm the core mechanics: Kahr, Lossow, and Seißer's
pledge of support, extorted in the Bürgerbräukeller on 8 November, was
repudiated once they were released; Röhm held the War Ministry building
during the putsch; the 9 November march to the Odeonsplatz was met with
police gunfire that left, per Paxton and Hessler, sixteen Nazis and four
police dead — a minor **[CONTRADICTION]** with the fourteen-marcher count
given elsewhere on this page from Evans (2003); the underlying event and
its consequences are not in dispute, only the precise Nazi death toll (14
vs. 16). At his
subsequent trial, Hitler turned the courtroom into a propaganda platform,
declaring "I wanted to become the destroyer of Marxism" — a line Paxton and
Hessler read as the moment Hitler recast personal failure as the founding
myth of his movement. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## Evans (2003) Additions

- **Setting**: the [[ruhr-occupation-crisis-1923|Ruhr crisis]] and
  [[hyperinflation-1923|hyperinflation]] autumn; Kahr, Lossow, and Seisser
  readying their own anti-Berlin plans while Seeckt refused cooperation;
  Röhm's "Working Community of Patriotic Fighting Leagues" made Hitler
  nominal political leader of a paramilitary bloc larger than the SA — Evans
  describes him as "being swept along by events," with
  [[ludendorff|Ludendorff]] as figurehead.
- **Mechanics**: the 9 November marchers — ~2,000 armed men — were each paid
  2 billion inflation-marks (just over $3) from 14,000 billion "confiscated"
  from two Jewish banknote printers. Who fired first at the police cordon is
  disputed in the sources (Evans flags it); 14 marchers and 4 policemen died;
  Göring, wounded in the leg, fled abroad and acquired his morphine addiction.
- **The trial as judicial scandal**: held before a specially constituted
  Munich "People's Court" under Georg Neithardt (chosen for nationalist
  sympathies by Justice Minister Franz Gürtner — the same Gürtner who
  reappears as Reich Justice Minister in 1933); Hitler allowed to orate for
  hours; Ludendorff's role suppressed and he was acquitted; deportation
  refused on grounds of war service; five years for high treason with the
  court citing the defendants' "pure patriotic spirit and the most noble
  will." At Landsberg ("fortress incarceration"; his cell previously held
  Eisner's assassin Arco-Valley) Hitler received over 500 visitors and
  dictated [[mein-kampf|Mein Kampf]].
- **The lesson** (Evans, attributed): paramilitary violence alone, without
  army backing, could not seize power in Germany — unlike Mussolini's Rome.
  Evans explicitly denies Hitler adopted a strict "legality" strategy after
  1923; what he adopted was the neutralization and co-optation of the army
  and institutions, applied in 1933 (see [[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]]).
