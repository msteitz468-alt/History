## Narrative

The Austro-Prussian War of 1866 — known in German as the *Deutschen Krieg* (German War) or *Bruderkrieg* (Brothers' War), and in English as the Seven Weeks' War — was the decisive military conflict that settled the question of German unification in favor of Prussia and against Austria. It lasted only seven weeks, culminating in the decisive Prussian victory at the Battle of Königgrätz (Sadowa) on 3 July 1866.

The immediate trigger was a dispute over administration of the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein, which Prussia and Austria had jointly seized from Denmark in 1864. Bismarck engineered a crisis over Holstein's governance that gave Prussia a pretext to invade. The war mobilized states across the [[german-confederation]]: most of the medium German states (Hanover, Saxony, Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, Hesse) sided with Austria; Italy allied with Prussia.

Prussian military superiority — the needle-gun (*Zündnadelgewehr*), superior railway mobilization, and the strategic vision of Chief of Staff Helmuth von Moltke the Elder — produced a rapid, crushing victory. Austria was decisively defeated at Königgrätz within seven weeks. The Peace of Prague (23 August 1866) dissolved the [[german-confederation]] and Austria was excluded from German affairs permanently.

## Causal Analysis

Smith's chapter on the 1850–1878 period emphasizes that the war should not be read retrospectively as an inevitable step toward German [[german-unification|unification]]. "These wars — against Denmark over Schleswig and Holstein in 1864, then against Austria over federal reform in 1866, and finally against France in 1870 — were national wars of unification only from the perspective of 1871. From the viewpoint of 1850, they could just as well have been called 'national wars of exclusion,' because they ultimately sealed the exclusion of Austria from Germany" (Smith, ch. 13).

The proximate cause was the Prussian constitutional conflict (1860–1866), during which Bismarck had governed without an approved budget in defiance of the liberal Prussian Diet. Military victories retroactively legitimized this breach: "Prussia's military success over Denmark at the battle of Dybbøl on 18 April 1864, and, even more so, the victory over the Austrian troops at Königgrätz on 3 July 1866, seemed to legitimize Bismarck's policy of 1862 retrospectively." The shift in liberal opinion was rapid — Karl Twesten, a founder of the Progressive Party and former critic of Bismarck, declared that internal conflicts must give way "when it comes to the integrity of the German fatherland."

Deeper structural causes included: (1) Austria's failure to constitutionalize in 1848 while Prussia did, making the two states structurally incompatible as co-leaders of Germany; (2) Bismarck's recognition, from his Frankfurt posting, that [[nationalism]] could be made useful for Prussian great-power expansion; (3) the economic dynamism of Prussia relative to Austria.

## Consequence Analysis

The most consequential result was not military but political. The [[habsburg-empire]] was expelled from German affairs, and the "world of the German states changed completely." The Indemnity Bill (26 September 1866) resolved the Prussian constitutional crisis by parliamentary self-abnegation — the liberal Diet majority retrospectively absolved the government of its unconstitutional conduct. This created what Smith calls the "liberal era" of institution-building (1867–1878).

The annexations — Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt — were politically transformative. Opposition parties from these newly Prussian territories entered the North German Confederation's Reichstag and "accepted the Reich by taking part in the Reichstag elections," demonstrating democracy's integrative function. Saxon patriots participated in elections only eight months after their defeat.

The war framed German unification as a Protestant victory: "the victory over Catholic Austria, and to an even greater extent the victory against France, was staged as a victory of Protestant Germany over Catholicism itself." This framing directly fueled the [[kulturkampf]] after 1871.

## Historiography

The historiographical debate centers on whether 1866 represented a missed liberal opportunity or the pragmatic resolution of an irresolvable constitutional conflict. The older "Sonderweg" interpretation (Wehler, Rosenberg) emphasized the defeat of liberalism in 1866 as a turning point that prepared the way for 1933. More recent scholarship (Blackbourn, Eley, Anderson) has questioned this teleology, stressing that the institutions built in the liberal era 1867–1878 — democratic suffrage, federalism, the Reichstag — had genuine democratic content and were not simply facades for Junker power.

Smith's own reading in this handbook chapter emphasizes the war's ambiguity: "To see only authoritarian solutions and their consequences for the twentieth century does not take into consideration that there were also other institutions of conflict resolution between 1867 and 1871 — ones that did not lead to 1933."
