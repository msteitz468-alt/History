# Long Island Expressway Construction (1955 onward)

Major Robert Moses expressway project on Long Island, New York. Construction began in 1955 and continued through the 1960s with repeated expansions. Designed capacity was 80,000 vehicles per day. By 1963 it was carrying 132,000 vehicles daily and became known as the "world's longest parking lot" during rush hours.

## Narrative

The Long Island Expressway was the flagship of Moses' post-war highway program under the Joint Program for arterial facilities. Despite planners' repeated demands for mass transit or at least bus lanes, Moses refused. Independent studies (Day & Zimmerman) showed that even a limited rapid transit line on the center mall would be low-cost and divert significant traffic, including thousands of car drivers.

Moses began construction with Triborough funds while the study was underway. By the time planners reported, actual work rendered transit integration "impracticable" without massive waste. The road was built and immediately over capacity.

Subsequent "solutions" were more highway construction: massive interchanges ($75M single interchange), additional lanes, cantilevered service roads.

## Causal Analysis

- Joint Program (Moses) committed Triborough and Port Authority revenues almost exclusively to automobile facilities.
- Deliberate exclusion of transit options (no reservation of space, low bridge clearances on related parkways).
- Preemptive construction to foreclose alternatives.
- Traffic generation effect: new capacity induced new trips rather than relieving parallel routes.
- Political insulation via authorities and mayoral dependence.

## Consequence Analysis

The LIE locked Long Island into automobile dependence for decades. Development remained low-density and car-oriented. Congestion became chronic and self-reinforcing. The absence of rapid transit meant that every subsequent surge (Throgs Neck, Verrazano) compounded the failure. Planners' warnings were vindicated; the road failed as designed before it was completed.
