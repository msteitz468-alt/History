# The Warlord Era (1916–1928)

The **Warlord Era** was the period after the death of **Yuan Shikai** in which China fragmented
among rival **regional militarists ("warlords")**, the central [[republic-of-china-1912|Republican]]
government in Beijing became a hollow shell, and real power lay with whoever commanded troops. From
the [[cambridge-history-of-china|Cambridge History of China]] (Republican China volumes).

## Narrative

With Yuan Shikai's failed monarchy and death (1916), no figure or institution could hold the
[[republic-of-china-1912|Republic]] together. Power devolved to **military commanders** controlling
provinces or regions — organized into shifting **cliques** (the Zhili, Fengtian, Anhui factions and
many lesser figures) — who fought constantly for territory, revenue, and control of the
prestige-bearing but powerless **Beijing government**. The country was effectively partitioned;
foreign powers backed rival warlords; taxation, conscription, and banditry devastated the
peasantry. Against this disorder the cultural **May Fourth** nationalism of 1919 and new political
movements (the [[kuomintang|Nationalists]] and the new Communist Party) gathered force.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **death of Yuan Shikai (1916)**, which removed the only national strongman and
  left a vacuum.
- `caused_by:` the **failure of republican institutions** after 1911 to take root, and the prior
  growth of **regional armies** loyal to commanders rather than the state.
- `contributed_to:` **foreign interference**, as powers armed and financed competing cliques.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` over a decade of **civil war, fragmentation, and suffering**, and the collapse of the
  Beijing government's legitimacy.
- `produced:` the conditions for **Nationalist reunification**: the [[kuomintang|Kuomintang]], allied
  with the Communists, launched the **Northern Expedition (1926–28)** under **Chiang Kai-shek**,
  defeating the major warlords and nominally reunifying China under the Nanjing government — though
  many warlords survived by switching allegiance, leaving Chiang's control incomplete.
- `enabled:` the rise of the **Communists** in the rural disorder the era left behind.

## Actors

The **regional warlords and their cliques**, the impotent **Beijing government**, and the
Canton-based **[[kuomintang]]** that ultimately ended the era. See [[republic-of-china-1912]].

## Historiography

The CHC treats the warlords not merely as bandit-chiefs but within the structural breakdown of the
Chinese state after 1911 — some were reformers or modernizers in their domains, and "warlordism" was
a symptom of the failure to build national institutions. The era frames the central problem of
Republican China: the **search for a power able to reunify and modernize the country**, ultimately
contested between Nationalists and Communists.
