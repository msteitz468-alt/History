# The Meiji Restoration (1868)

The overthrow of the [[shogunate-bakufu|Tokugawa shogunate]] and the "restoration" of
direct imperial rule under the young **Emperor Meiji** — the hinge between
[[edo-period|early-modern]] and modern [[japan|Japan]], and "a major event in modern world
history." From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]]
(ch. 15).

## Narrative

The crisis began with the **forced opening** of the country: **Commodore Perry's** "black
ships" (1853) and the **unequal treaties** (1858) that ended [[sakoku|seclusion]],
exposing the bakufu as unable to "expel the barbarian." The cry ***sonnō jōi*** ("revere
the emperor, expel the barbarian") rallied disaffected young samurai, especially in the
reformist western *tozama* domains of **Satsuma, Chōshū, and Tosa**. Allied with
anti-bakufu court nobles (**Iwakura Tomomi**), their leaders (**Ōkubo Toshimichi**, **Saigō
Takamori**, **Kido Takayoshi**) maneuvered the last shogun, **Tokugawa Yoshinobu**, into
resigning (Nov. 1867); on **3 January 1868** they seized the palace and proclaimed the
**Restoration**, abolishing the bakufu. The **Boshin War** against Tokugawa loyalists
lasted until June 1869; the emperor was moved to Edo, renamed **Tokyo** ("eastern
capital").

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Western intrusion** (Perry; the unequal treaties) — Mason and Caiger
  argue this was "the most proximate cause," the failure of the preindustrial seclusion
  policy in the face of the industrial West.
- `contributed_to:` the **reformist western han**, whose Tempō-era military and fiscal
  reforms put them in a position to act; and the **sonnō jōi** nationalist xenophobia among
  young samurai and rural landlord-entrepreneurs.
- Crucially, the Restoration worked **through baku-han pluralism** — change from *within*
  the existing structure: "neither a Norman Conquest nor a French Revolution," led by
  middle- and lower-rank samurai, not a mass rising (90% of the population had little to do
  with it).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **abolition of the shogunate and feudalism** — the *han* replaced by
  prefectures (1871), samurai stipends commuted and sword-wearing banned, conscription
  introduced (1872), provoking the failed **Satsuma Rebellion (1877)**.
- `produced:` the [[meiji-period|Meiji project]] of deliberate **modernization** —
  industrialization, a constitution and Diet (1889), and the drive for **treaty revision**
  (achieved 1894).
- `enabled:` Japan's emergence as the first non-Western **great power**.

## Jansen's reading: a transition, not a coup (Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5)

The [[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History Vol. 5]] (Marius
Jansen) insists the Restoration be read **as a whole century-long transition**, not the events
of 1867–68 alone: "limited to the events of 1867 and 1868, it constituted little more than a
coup that shifted rule from one sector of the ruling class to another," but seen as a process
from the late-Tokugawa [[opening-of-japan-1853-1858|opening]] to the constitutional state of
1889, it was **revolutionary**. Jansen sets it among Japan's great structural transitions
between civil and warrior rule (Yamato→ritsuryō; Heian→Kamakura; now warrior→civil), each
**preceded by a long preparation** (the bureaucratization of the samurai, the Tempō reforms'
new administrator-officials) and each clothed in **archaicizing, revivalist rhetoric** — here
the "return to antiquity" of imperial restoration (the Heian and Meiji shrines, the reign-name
chosen from the *Book of Changes*, the rewriting of history to decry "military usurpation").

## Historiography

Jansen traces how interpretation tracked the political climate. The **prewar orthodoxy** (not
yet dead) made the Restoration the triumph of selfless imperial-loyalist *shishi* awakening a
"long-slumbering imperial consciousness" — laudatory, nationalist, and equitably allocating
credit among the southwestern *han*. Against it, an early-twentieth-century revisionism
(**Tokutomi Sohō**) separated the leaders from their achievement and credited **"inexorable
trends"** — the fragility of feudal society and the rise of "stouthearted rural leaders" —
rather than great men, often to advance liberal politics. Postwar **Marxist** scholarship
debated whether the Restoration was a bourgeois revolution or an "absolutist" reordering. The
Cambridge consensus: a **legitimacy transfer led from within the ruling class** that
nonetheless set in motion genuinely revolutionary change.

## Significance

The Restoration is read not as a sudden revolution but as a **legitimacy transfer** that
let a determined elite remake Japan from above — "as much creative as imitative." Its
genius was to **channel an old institution (the sacred throne) toward a wholly new end
(a modern nation-state)**, making it one of history's most successful self-directed
transformations.
