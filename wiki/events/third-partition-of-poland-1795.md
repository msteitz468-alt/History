# Third Partition of Poland

## Narrative
The **Third Partition of Poland** took place in October 1795, resulting in the final dismemberment and complete political dissolution of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth by Russia, Prussia, and Austria (L3456-3458).

Following the Second Partition of 1793, the Polish patriot Thaddeus Kosciuszko launched a national liberation uprising in March 1794 (L3456). Despite initial successes and courageous resistance, the Polish forces were crushed by the combined armies of Prussia and Russia, the latter commanded by General Alexander Suvorov (L3456). 

With Poland completely occupied, the three neighboring monarchies carried out the final partition of the remaining territory in late 1795 (L3456).

By the provisions of the third partition:
* **Russia:** Obtained the remainder of Lithuania and Ukraine (containing 1,200,000 inhabitants) and the Duchy of Courland (L3456-3458).
* **Prussia:** Annexed Mazovia, including the capital city of Warsaw, containing 1,000,000 inhabitants (L3458).
* **Austria:** Appropriated the remaining parts of Little Poland, including Cracow, with 1,500,000 inhabitants (L3458). 

The last king of Poland, Stanislaw Poniatowski, was forced to abdicate, and Poland ceased to exist as an independent state (L3440, L3458).

## Causal Analysis
The partition was caused by:
* **Kosciuszko Uprising:** The 1794 national revolt threatened the security of the annexed territories in Russia, Prussia, and Austria (L3456). The monarchies resolved that only the complete destruction of the Polish state could prevent future revolutions.
* **Geopolitical Collusion:** The three eastern monarchies colluded to remove a persistent source of diplomatic conflict in Eastern Europe, replacing a weak, unstable state with clear, shared borders (L3458).

## Consequence Analysis
The Third Partition produced:
* **Eradication of Poland:** The Polish state was removed from the map of Europe for over a century, until its restoration after World War I in 1918 (L3458).
* **Eastern Alliance:** The shared dismemberment bonded Russia, Prussia, and Austria together in a long-term conservative alliance on the international stage (L3458).
* **Polish Resentment and Rebellions:** The partition left Europe with a persistent "Polish Question." The Poles never accepted the dismemberment, leading to Polish support for Napoleon in 1812 and massive, bloody rebellions against Russian rule in 1831 and 1863 (L3458-3462).
* **Class and Social Continuity:** Although the Russian government took control, the social structure of the annexed territories remained unchanged; Polish and Polonized landlords continued to dominate and exploit the Ukrainian and White Russian peasantry (L3468).

## Historiography
This entry again rests on a single secondary synthesis; primary materials on the Kosciuszko Uprising (his own proclamations, Suvorov's campaign reports, and diplomatic correspondence among the three partitioning courts) are not represented in the collection. Historians debate whether the Uprising was a genuinely broad-based national movement — as later Polish nationalist historiography emphasizes — or a narrower gentry-led rising with limited peasant participation despite Kosciuszko's Proclamation of Połaniec; the massacre at Praga during Suvorov's assault on Warsaw is itself a contested episode in Polish national memory. The partition's long shadow as the origin point of the modern "Polish Question" has generated a substantial nationalist historiographical tradition this collection does not directly hold, and the entry's account of the surviving social order reflects a single source's framing rather than a cross-checked consensus.
