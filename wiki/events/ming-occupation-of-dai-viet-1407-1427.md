## Narrative

In 1406–1407 the Ming Yongle emperor Zhu Di sent armies of roughly 135,000 men from Guangxi and 80,000 from Yunnan into Dai Viet, ostensibly to restore the deposed Tran dynasty against the usurper [[ho-quy-ly|Ho Quy Ly]]. Ho Quy Ly's riverine defense strategy, anchored on the Da Bang fortress, collapsed when more than half his army deserted amid Ming propaganda promising a Tran restoration; prominent Red River plain families (led by Mac Thuy, ancestor of the later Mac dynasty) defected to the Ming. Ho Quy Ly and his sons were captured in southern Ha Tinh in 1407 and taken to Nanjing, where he disappears from the record.

By mid-1407 the Ming, declaring the Tran line extinct, organized the conquered territory as the imperial province of Giao Chi under senior civil administrator Huang Fu, who spent the next decade building an integrated Ming provincial government, and military commander Zhang Fu, who spent two decades suppressing residual Tran-loyalist resistance (led successively by Tran Ngoi and Tran Quy Khoang, both eventually captured by 1413). Ming rule extended Confucian education (shrines to Confucius, a full civil-examination system, new editions of the classics sent from Nanjing in 1419), an efficient road/post/communications network, and — for its first years — deliberately light taxation to win over the population and finance postwar reconstruction. It also brought a systematic policy of collecting "heterodox" Vietnamese-language and historical writings (many destroyed on the spot or shipped north and subsequently lost), sumptuary rules (e.g., forbidding short skirts, forbidding children to cut their hair) aimed at "civilizing" local custom along Ming lines, and the ordinary abuses of a colonial administration staffed partly by demoted or failed northern officials.

Resistance was strongest not in the Red River plain, where many Kinh people accepted or even supported Ming government, but among the Trai upland populations of the southern provinces (Thanh Hoa, Nghe An), where a succession of local uprisings occurred through the 1410s. After the Yongle emperor's death in 1424, his successors Zhu Gaozhi and Zhu Zhanji progressively deprioritized Giao Chi, ending commodity requisitions, considering restoration of the Tran, and withdrawing administrative commitment even as tens of thousands of Ming soldiers and officials remained on the ground. This policy vacuum was exploited by [[le-loi|Le Loi]], whose decade-long insurgency (the [[lam-son-uprising-1418-1428|Lam Son uprising]]) escalated dramatically after 1424 and forced a full Ming withdrawal by 1427.

## Causal Analysis

The occupation was enabled by the internal collapse of Tran authority and Ho Quy Ly's failure to secure the loyalty of the Red River plain before facing a much stronger external threat (`contributed_to`). It was directly caused by Ming imperial ambition under Zhu Di, "not by a law or theme of history but rather by the ambition and limitation of a particular man" (`caused_by`). Its end was `caused_by` a combination of a change in Ming imperial priorities after 1424 (Zhu Gaozhi's and Zhu Zhanji's retrenchment) and the escalating military success of Le Loi's insurgency, which the Ming court was unwilling to fund a full-scale response to.

## Consequence Analysis

Taylor frames the occupation as a genuine "historic watershed," not merely an interruption: it completed the destruction of the old Tran aristocracy that Ho Quy Ly had begun, permanently installed Zhu Xi Confucianism as the dominant framework of governance (displacing Buddhism), and — through the systematic destruction of vernacular and historical texts — created the documentary gap that makes pre-fifteenth-century Vietnamese history unusually hard to reconstruct from indigenous sources. It also hardened a regional/cultural fault line (Kinh lowland vs. Trai/Muong upland, Red River plain vs. southern provinces) that "became a prominent feature of Vietnamese politics for the next four centuries," feeding directly into the later [[seventy-years-war-vietnam-1533-1592|Seventy Years War]] and the broader era of inter-regional warfare.

## Historiography

Later Vietnamese court historiography (the *Complete Book of the History of Great Viet*, compiled under the Le dynasty, chiefly by Phan Phu Tien in 1455 and Ngo Si Lien in 1479) portrays Ming Giao Chi uniformly as a cruel, exploitative regime destined to fall to righteous Vietnamese resistance. Taylor explicitly challenges this as legitimizing propaganda for the succeeding Le dynasty, arguing that, "like nearly every regime that has tried to govern anywhere," Ming administration under Huang Fu was a genuine mixture of positive and negative features, and that resistance was neither universal nor inevitable — it was concentrated among specific populations (the Trai of the southern uplands) and was ultimately decided by a change of policy at the Ming court, not solely by Vietnamese agency. This anti-teleological reading is central to Taylor's broader argument that no single narrative of continuous "Vietnamese resistance" adequately describes the fifteenth century.
