# Deification of Ieyasu as Tosho Daigongen (1617)

In the third year of Genna (1617), one year after the death of [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]],
the Emperor conferred the divine title **Tosho Daigongen** ("Illuminating the East, Great
Incarnation") on the founder of the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]]. Ieyasu's remains
were transferred from their temporary burial at Kunozan on the Suruga coast to a newly constructed
shrine at **Nikko** in Shimozuke Province, where he would be venerated as the tutelary deity of the
ruling house for the duration of the [[edo-period|Edo period]].

## Narrative

Immediately after Ieyasu's death in the fourth month of 1616, a dispute arose between the two
senior ecclesiastics in bakufu service over the form his posthumous cult should take. **Konchiin
Suden** (a Zen monk who had managed Ieyasu's foreign correspondence and temple foundations) aligned
with **Bonshun** (son of the Shinto ritualist Urabe Kanetomo) to advocate making Ieyasu a pure
Shinto deity of the Yui-itsu school, with the title *Daimyojin* — the same title given the deceased
Toyotomi Hideyoshi as Toyokuni Daimyojin. **Tenkai**, the Tendai monk who had been Ieyasu's closest
religious adviser in his last years, opposed this: the title *Daimyojin* was of ill omen because of
its Toyotomi associations, and he advocated instead the title *Gongen* ("Incarnation"), reflecting
the more comprehensive Sanno Ichijitsu Shinto of Hieizan — a form integrating Buddhist and Shinto
elements. Gongen was also, he argued, more august.

Shogun Hidetada initially found the prospect of his father becoming "both god and Buddha too" most
satisfactory, and the Confucianist Hayashi was consulted. Tenkai prevailed. In the new year of 1617
the imperial edict conferred the title **Tosho Daigongen** — "Illuminating the East Great
Incarnation." The title Tosho (East-Illuminating) was chosen because Tenkai identified Ieyasu with
the Buddha Yakushi Nyorai (the Healing Buddha of the Eastern Quarter), whose light "brightens the
hundred and eighteen thousand fields of the Eastern Region."

In the third month of 1617, Ieyasu's remains were disinterred at Kunozan — Tenkai himself wielded
the shovel — and borne by stages to Nikko, arriving on the fourth day of the fourth month. The
shrine had been prepared under the supervision of Todo Takatora and Honda Masazumi since the tenth
month of 1616. The ceremony of handing over the Imperial Edict of Deity was performed by the court
envoy Minister Ano Saneaki, who read the edict aloud and handed it to Archbishop Tenkai, who placed
it in the shrine. Shinto services followed, with sacred cars carried in honor of the Three Gongen of
Nikko — the other two being San-o Gongen and Madara Gongen.

## Why Nikko

Tenkai cited Ieyasu's own deathbed instructions as the authority for Nikko: "After my death I am to
be buried at Kunozan in Tsuruga, and after a year has elapsed a Divine title is to be sought from
the Emperor and I am to be removed to Nikko." Nikko had ancient Shinto associations (the *Engishiki*
listed its deities) and was identified with Minamoto Yoritomo's patronage — a lineage Ieyasu claimed
through the Tokugawa/Matsudaira genealogy. Its position to the north-east of Edo gave it cosmological
significance as a guardian of the capital against inauspicious directions.

Sadler notes that the shrine was also strategically positioned: "the center of this influence being
situated east of Edo was intended to counteract any insidious and softening and disintegrating
tendencies that might... emanate from Kyoto." Tenkai and Todo Takatora were, in popular rumor,
identified with the two other Gongen flanking Ieyasu's shrine.

## Causal Analysis

The deification was caused_by Ieyasu's deathbed instructions and enabled_by the existing precedent
of Toyotomi Hideyoshi's deification as Toyokuni Daimyojin. It was produced by the rivalry of
Tenkai and Suden/Bonshun for influence with the new Shogun Hidetada. The choice of the Gongen title
(over Daimyojin) was specifically designed to distinguish Ieyasu's cult from Hideyoshi's — whose
shrine Iemitsu later suppressed — and to embed Tokugawa legitimacy in a more comprehensive
Tendai-Shinto synthesis that was politically controllable from Edo.

## Consequence Analysis

For two hundred and sixty years Tosho Daigongen remained the tutelary deity of the Tokugawa ruling
powers and, through them, of all Japan. The shrine at Nikko became a major pilgrimage site and the
most elaborate architectural ensemble of the Edo period (substantially rebuilt under the third
Shogun Iemitsu in the 1630s). Veneration of Ieyasu gave subsequent Tokugawa shoguns a divine
warrant for their authority that did not depend on either the Emperor's political will or the
military power that had faded in peacetime. When the Meiji Restoration came in 1868, the shrine's
significance was deliberately displaced by the Meiji Jingu and the imperial cult: "after the
interval of a few decades his place was taken by the deity of the Meiji Jingu."

## Historiography

Sadler reconstructs the dispute between Tenkai and Suden from the *Reminiscences of Jigen Daishi*
(Tenkai's posthumous title) and official chronicles. The source — [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]]
— is sympathetic to the Tokugawa narrative and takes Tenkai's account of Ieyasu's deathbed
instructions largely at face value. Sadler himself notes that "Tenkai maintained that he was in
communication by means of inspired dreams with the Divine Ieyasu," which casts some retrospective
authority on the entire deification program. Scholarly debate on the Nikko cult has since examined
the degree to which the "instructions" attributed to Ieyasu were post-hoc legitimation by Tenkai
and the bakufu; the [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]] does not fully resolve this question.
