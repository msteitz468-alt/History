# Destruction of the Serapeum of Alexandria (391 CE)

In **391 CE** the great [[alexandria|Alexandrian]] **Serapeum** — the temple of
[[sarapis|Sarapis]], one of the most important monuments of the ancient city — became the
flashpoint of pagan–Christian violence and was destroyed. It is the symbolic end of organised
public paganism in Egypt.

## Narrative

According to Bagnall and Rathbone, in 391 "the Serapeum became the focus for intercommunal
violence and street battles between pagans and Christians. Pagans barricaded themselves in the
temple." The emperor [[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]] — whose reign saw a series of laws against
pagan cult — "ordered the cult to be suppressed and the pagans fled. A Christian soldier hacked
the famous statue into pieces, which were then symbolically buried in different parts of the
city." The chryselephantine cult statue by Bryaxis, the focus of the Mediterranean-wide cult,
was annihilated; the temple complex, which had included a library (the "daughter" of the Great
[[alexandria|Library]]), lecture rooms, and shrines, ceased to function. The destruction was
led in tradition by the patriarch **Theophilus of Alexandria**.

The episode belongs to a sequence of conflicts that mark Alexandria's turn to Christianity:
Bagnall and Rathbone group it with "the lynching of the philosopher Hypatia in AD 415, and
Justinian's supposed sixth-century closing of the Temple of Isis on Philae," behind
which "lurks the true story of the conversion of Egypt to Christianity."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire]]` and Theodosius I's anti-pagan
  legislation, which removed legal protection from temple cult and emboldened Christian crowds.
- `contributed_to:` the long-standing communal antagonism in Alexandria — among pagans, Jews,
  and Christians — that had repeatedly erupted in street violence since the first century.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the effective end of public pagan worship in the greatest cult-centre of Egypt,
  and the destruction of one of antiquity's famous libraries.
- `contributed_to: [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire]]` — a landmark in the
  Christianisation of Egypt, after which (by the fifth century) "Egypt had become a Christian
  land."

## Historiography

The Serapeum's destruction is among the best-attested acts of late-antique temple destruction,
described by Christian authors (Rufinus, Socrates, Sozomen) who celebrated it and by the pagan
Eunapius who mourned it — a contested event whose sources are openly partisan on both sides.
Modern scholarship (Bagnall, *Egypt in Late Antiquity*, 1993) cautions that such dramatic
literary set-pieces can overstate the suddenness of conversion: the archaeological and
documentary record shows Christianisation as a gradual fourth-to-fifth-century process of which
the Serapeum is the visible peak, not the whole. The associated killing of **Hypatia** (415) is
likewise heavily mythologised in both ancient and modern retellings.
