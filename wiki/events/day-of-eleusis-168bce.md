# The Day of Eleusis (168 BCE)

The famous moment when a single Roman envoy, with a stick and a circle drawn in the
sand, halted a victorious Hellenistic king — the symbol of Rome's new power to
dictate to the East. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 10 (Habicht).

## Narrative

In the **Sixth Syrian War**, **[[antiochus-iv-epiphanes|Antiochus IV]]** had defeated
Egypt and, in 168, was besieging **Alexandria**, having overrun almost all of
Ptolemaic territory. Days after the Roman victory over Perseus at
**[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|Pydna]]** (22 June 168), the Roman envoy
**C. Popillius Laenas** met him in the suburb of **Eleusis** and handed him a Senate
decree ordering him to **evacuate Egypt and Cyprus**. When Antiochus asked for time
to consult, Popillius drew a circle in the sand around the king's feet and demanded
his answer **before he stepped out of it**. Antiochus complied and withdrew.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the war with Egypt that put Antiochus on the verge of swallowing the
Ptolemaic kingdom — and, decisively, the **Roman victory at Pydna**, which freed the
Senate to act in the East.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **Seleucid withdrawal** from Egypt and Cyprus, and the survival of a
weakened **[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic Egypt]]** as effectively a Roman protectorate.
enabled: a vivid demonstration that Rome could now **dictate to the Hellenistic East
without fighting** — Habicht notes the demand effectively "added a new clause" to the
treaty of Apamea (Antiochus was under no obligation to spare Egypt), and that the
brusque treatment matched the contemporary humiliation of Rome's own allies Eumenes
and Rhodes after Pydna: "demands on one side and obedience on the other."

## Historiography

[[polybius]] is the source; the circle-in-the-sand detail became the archetype of
Roman *imperium* expressed as bare command. Habicht uses Antiochus' restrained
compliance to refute the "madman" (*Epimanes*) tradition. See
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism]].
