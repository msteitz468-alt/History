## Narrative

After 1989, the disappearance of the Cold War division of Europe made a genuinely
united continent imaginable for the first time since World War II, and the European
Community (EC) pursued two potentially incompatible strategies simultaneously:
**deepening** integration among existing members and **widening** membership eastward.
EC Commission president Jacques Delors (1985–1995), the most activist commission
president since the 1960s, pushed deepening first. The 1986 Single European Act had
already proposed the unimpeded transfer of labor, capital, and services (achieved
January 1, 1993, when the EC renamed itself the European Union), a single currency, and
a common foreign and military policy.

French president François Mitterrand and German chancellor Helmut Kohl pressed Delors to
carry this momentum forward. British opposition, led by Margaret Thatcher until her
November 1990 resignation, was overcome by her more accommodating successor John Major's
acceptance of limited federalism. After a year of negotiation, a convention at
Maastricht in the Netherlands produced the **Maastricht Treaty** (Treaty on European
Union) in December 1991 — the most radical revision of the European project since its
founding: a timetable for a single currency by 1998, proposals for common foreign and
defense policy, expanded majority voting, and greater powers for the European
Parliament.

Ratification proved surprisingly difficult across the twelve member states, exposing deep
popular resistance to an unresponsive Brussels bureaucracy and to EU standards seen as
threatening national traditions (hunting, brewing, cheese-making). Denmark had to vote
twice; France ratified by a narrow 51 percent margin. The treaty finally took effect in
November 1993.

Despite this friction, the single-currency provision was substantially realized: eleven
EU members fused their currencies into the euro on January 1, 1999 (initially a
"virtual," accounting-only currency), with physical euro notes and coins entering
circulation January 1, 2002, retiring national currencies including the franc, lira, and
mark. Currency union required member states to bring inflation, deficits, and national
debt down to German levels (tolerating higher unemployment along the way) — Britain and
Italy had already been forced out of the earlier European Monetary System by a 1992
recession. The euro subsequently weakened sharply against the dollar during the
dot-com boom (falling from $1.17 to $0.87 by August 2000) before recovering to $1.50 by
2008 as the U.S. deficit ballooned.

Deepening reached its limits outside monetary and trade policy: the European Council of
national leaders, not the supranational Commission, increasingly drove EU policy,
especially under the less visionary Commission presidents who followed Delors. A 2003
"Constitution for Europe," which would have created a standing EU Council presidency and
an EU foreign-affairs post, was rejected by French and Dutch voters in referenda in 2005;
a scaled-back Lisbon Treaty (December 2007) achieved similar ends without constitutional
pretensions, entering into force December 1, 2009 after Ireland voted twice and Poland
and the Czech Republic negotiated opt-outs.

Widening proceeded in parallel: Austria, Finland, and Sweden joined January 1, 1995
(EU membership to fifteen). Ten former communist and Mediterranean states — the Baltic
states, Poland, Hungary, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Slovenia, Malta, and Cyprus —
signed a Treaty of Accession in April 2003 and joined May 1, 2004, before the EU's
governing machinery was fully adapted to accommodate them; Romania and Bulgaria joined
January 1, 2007, bringing membership to twenty-seven. Existing members restricted new
members' worker immigration for up to seven years and phased in farm aid only gradually
— "second-class membership," in the assessment of critics, that widened the union at
the expense of deepening it.

## Causal Analysis

`enabled` by the end of the Cold War division of Europe (see [[revolutions-of-1989]]),
which removed the geopolitical barrier to eastward EU widening and created the political
momentum among Western leaders (Mitterrand, Kohl) to lock in deeper integration before
that momentum dissipated. `contributed_to`: German reunification (see
[[german-reunification-1990]]) increased both the incentive for France to bind a larger
Germany into EU structures and the practical urgency of institutional reform.

## Consequence Analysis

Produced: the renaming of the European Community as the European Union (1993); the euro
currency union (1999 virtual, 2002 physical); a persistent "multiple-speed" Europe in
which Britain, Sweden, Denmark, and (initially) other states remained outside the euro
zone. Enabled: the 2004 and 2007 eastward enlargements bringing former Warsaw Pact
states into the EU. Contributed to: recurring later strains over unequal integration,
including the 2009–2010 sovereign debt pressures on Greece and other heavily indebted
members.

## Actors

Jacques Delors (EC/EU Commission president); François Mitterrand (France); Helmut Kohl
(Germany); Margaret Thatcher and John Major (Britain).

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler frame Maastricht within a "deepening versus widening" tension that
persisted through the 1990s–2000s and shaped both the euro's rocky birth and the EU's
awkward eastward enlargement — a useful interpretive lens distinct from purely
institutional-legal accounts of European integration.
