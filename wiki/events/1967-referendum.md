## Narrative

The referendum held on 27 May 1967 amended two provisions of the Australian Constitution. It removed the exclusion of Aboriginal people from the national census and removed the restriction preventing the Commonwealth from making laws for "people of any race" that had originally been inserted to enable racial exclusion — thus giving the federal government power to legislate for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, overriding discriminatory State regimes.

The result was 90.7 per cent in favour — one of the largest affirmative votes in Australian referendum history. It came after sustained campaigning by FCAATSI (Federal Council for the Advancement of Aborigines and Torres Strait Islanders), which had been building momentum since 1958, and years of civil rights activism including the 1965 Freedom Ride led by Charles Perkins and the 1966 Gurindji Walk-off.

Source: [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], chapter by Anna Haebich and Steve Kinnane: "A referendum in 1967 secured support from 90.7 per cent of Australian voters. This success persuaded many white Australians that the 'Aboriginal problem' had been solved, but younger, urban Indigenous people held that 'equality with whites was no longer an aim, but a stepping stone.'"

## Consequence Analysis

The referendum gave the Commonwealth power to legislate for Indigenous people; it did not itself confer citizenship rights or land rights. The Coalition government of Harold Holt "did not use its new power to override discriminatory State practices." A new generation of Indigenous leaders, influenced by African American Black Power tactics, concluded the referendum was a stepping stone, not a destination. The 1971 Blackburn judgment (upholding terra nullius), the 1972 Aboriginal Tent Embassy, and the subsequent land rights campaigns were the direct political sequels.

## Historiography

The referendum is among the most symbolically significant events in Australian political history. Historical debate has focused on the gap between the overwhelming public vote and the lack of immediate policy change, and on the competing framings — civil rights assimilation versus Indigenous sovereignty. The chapter notes that at the time of writing (c.2013), the 1967 referendum and the 1996 Wik decision "stand as high points in a century punctuated by struggles rich in complexity and driven by the desire for social justice and a reconciled nation."
