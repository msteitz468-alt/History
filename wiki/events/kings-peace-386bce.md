# The King's Peace (Peace of Antalcidas), 386 BCE

## Narrative

The **King's Peace** — also called the **Peace of Antalcidas** after its Spartan
architect — ended the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]] and is the defining
diplomatic act of the fourth century: a settlement **dictated by the Persian King**
[[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]] and imposed on the Greek states. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH
Vol. VI]] (Ch. 4, Seager) describes how, in autumn 386, the satrap **Tiribazus** summoned
Greek envoys to **Sardis** and read out a **royal rescript** under the King's seals; the
oaths were then sworn at **Sparta** in spring 386, with a representative of the King
present.

## Terms

Artaxerxes "proclaimed the terms which he deemed just":

> *The cities of Asia, with [the islands of] Clazomenae and Cyprus, are to belong to the
> King. The other Greek cities, both small and great, are to be left autonomous, except
> Lemnos, Imbros and Scyros, which are to belong to Athens as of old. Whichever side does
> not accept this peace, on them I will make war, together with those who share my
> wishes, by land and by sea, with ships and with money.*

Two features stand out. First, the **Greeks of Asia Minor were formally surrendered to
Persia** — the price Sparta had long been willing to pay. Second, the **autonomy clause**
("the other cities to be autonomous") became the operative principle of the so-called
**Common Peace** (*koine eirene*): a general settlement, in theory applicable to all,
backed by the King's threat of war on any who refused.

## Sparta as enforcer

Seager stresses that the peace's "autonomy" was a **weapon, not a guarantee**: "autonomy
was to mean what Sparta wanted it to mean in any given case." Though Sparta "had done no
more than hold her own" in the war, **[[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]]** used the clause to
dismantle every rival power bloc — forcing **Thebes** to dissolve its hold on Boeotia,
compelling **Argos** to give up its union with Corinth, and restoring Corinth to the
Spartan alliance. No clause formally made Sparta *prostates* ("champion") of the peace;
it was simply that "the favour of Persia and her own military strength" let her interpret
and enforce the terms. The result was the apogee of the [[spartan-hegemony|Spartan
hegemony]] — and the overreach (the seizure of the Theban Cadmea in 382, see
[[liberation-of-thebes-379bce]]) that the [[second-athenian-confederacy|Second Athenian
Confederacy]] arose to resist.

## Significance: Persia as arbiter

The King's Peace inaugurated the characteristic instrument of fourth-century diplomacy —
the **Common Peace renewed under Persian sponsorship** (375, 371, and after). It made
explicit what the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]] had demonstrated: that **the
Persian King had become the arbiter of the Greek world**, able to settle Greek quarrels
"with ships and with money." By the renewal of 371, however, "**the King's Peace had
become no more than a name**" — the first renewal in which Persia played no part — and
the initiative had passed to a resurgent Thebes.

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: the stalemate of the [[corinthian-war-395bce|Corinthian War]] and Antalcidas'
strangling of the Athenian corn supply. produced: the cession of Asian Greeks to
[[achaemenid-empire|Persia]]; the **Common Peace** principle of autonomy; Spartan
supremacy under [[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]]; and the precedent of **Persian arbitration**
that shaped Greek politics until Philip of Macedon.

## Historiography

Unusually for classical Greek history, **Xenophon** (*Hellenica* v.1.31) preserves what
purports to be a **near-verbatim quotation of the actual royal rescript** — a rare case
of documentary text embedded in a narrative source, which gives the treaty's terms
unusual evidentiary solidity compared to most fourth-century events known only through
narrative summary. Whether Xenophon reproduces the literal wording of the Persian
chancellery's document or a Greek paraphrase circulated at the time is debated, but the
substance is corroborated by **Diodorus Siculus** (via Ephorus) and by the treaty's
consistent later citation as a reference point (the "Common Peace" template renewed in
375 and 371). Modern historians debate how much genuine initiative lay with Persia
versus Sparta — Xenophon's narrative and the later Isocratean tradition both have reasons
to inflate or downplay Persian agency — and how cynically to read Sparta's selective
enforcement of the "autonomy" clause, a question that shapes assessments of Agesilaus's
statesmanship versus opportunism.
