# First Fitna (656–661)

The **First Fitna** (656–661 CE) was the first civil war within the early Islamic empire. It began following the assassination of the third caliph, Uthman ibn Affan, in 656, and pitted the fourth caliph, **Ali ibn Abi Talib** (the Prophet Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law), against several aristocratic factions, primarily led by **Mu'awiya I** (the Umayyad governor of Syria). The five-year war resulted in the end of the [[rashidun-caliphate|Rashidun Caliphate]], the establishment of the hereditary [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]], and the permanent sectarian division of Islam into Sunni and Shi'a branches.

## Narrative

### 1. Outbreak and the Battle of the Camel (656 CE)
Following Uthman's assassination by mutinous soldiers in Medina, Ali ibn Abi Talib was proclaimed caliph. His elevation was immediately challenged by a faction in Mecca led by Talha ibn Ubayd Allah, al-Zubayr ibn al-Awwam, and Muhammad's widow A'isha. They accused Ali of failing to bring Uthman's killers to justice. Ali marched to Iraq, where he defeated this coalition at the **Battle of the Camel** (December 656) near Basra, during which Talha and al-Zubayr were killed, and A'isha was captured and sent back to Medina. Ali then established Kufa in Iraq as his new capital.

### 2. Battle of Siffin and Arbitration (657–658 CE)
Ali's rule was next challenged by Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, the governor of Syria and a close relative of Uthman, who also demanded justice for Uthman. The two armies clashed at the **Battle of Siffin** (July 657) along the Euphrates River. Facing defeat, Mu'awiya's general, Amr ibn al-A'as, ordered his soldiers to fix leaves of the Qur'an to their spears, calling for arbitration based on holy scripture. 

Ali was forced by his own soldiers to accept. The subsequent arbitration at Adhruh (658) proved disastrous for Ali, as the arbiters declared Uthman's killing unjust and stripped both Ali and Mu'awiya of their claims, leaving the caliphate in political limbo.

### 3. The Kharijite Secession and End of the War (658–661 CE)
The decision to arbitrate alienated a large faction of Ali's army, who declared, *"Judgment belongs to God alone"* (*la hukma illa lillah*), arguing that Ali had sinned by delegating divine judgment to humans. This faction, known as the **Kharijites** (Seceders), revolted. Ali crushed the Kharijites at the Battle of Nahrawan (658), but the slaughter alienated many of his remaining supporters in Iraq.

Mu'awiya exploited Ali's weakness, seizing control of Egypt and launching raids into Arabia. In January 661, Ali was assassinated while praying in the Great Mosque of Kufa by a Kharijite seeking revenge for Nahrawan. Ali's eldest son, Hasan, briefly succeeded him but abdicated in favor of Mu'awiya to prevent further bloodshed, allowing Mu'awiya to establish the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Caliphate]].

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad Supremacy]]`: The war centralizes power in Damascus, shifting the capital away from Arabia and consolidating control under the Marwanid branch of Quraysh.
- `produced: [[schism-in-islam|Sunni-Shi'a Division]]`: The conflict created the fundamental division in Islam. Ali's supporters became the *Shi'at Ali* ("Party of Ali" or Shi'ites), who held that leadership of the Muslim community was a divine right limited to Muhammad's household (Ahl al-Bayt). Their opponents, who eventually coalesced around the majority tradition, became the Sunnis.
- `produced: [[kharijites|Kharijite Movement]]`: The war produced the first major sectarian splinter group, the Kharijites, who advocated an egalitarian model of leadership based strictly on religious piety, declaring any sinning ruler an apostate.

## Historiography

The First Fitna is known only through the later Islamic historiographical tradition —
above all the great compilers **al-Tabari** and **al-Baladhuri** (9th–10th centuries),
who assembled earlier oral *akhbar* (reports) transmitted for generations before being
committed to writing, with named chains of transmission (*isnads*) of very uneven
reliability. No contemporary 7th-century Arabic narrative source survives independently
of these later compilations, and the material is deeply shaped by **sectarian
retrospection**: Sunni and Shi'a traditions diverge sharply on Ali's conduct, Mu'awiya's
legitimacy, and the culpability for Uthman's murder, since each community's later
self-understanding was built substantially on how this civil war was remembered. Modern
scholars treat specific numbers, speeches, and dramatic details (such as the leaves of
the Qur'an raised at Siffin) with caution as likely later elaboration, while accepting
the broad outline of events — the Battle of the Camel, Siffin, the arbitration, and the
Kharijite secession — as reasonably secure. The episode remains a live subject of
historiographical dispute precisely because how it is narrated bears directly on
competing claims to religious authority within Islam.
