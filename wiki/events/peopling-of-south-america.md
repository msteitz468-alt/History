## Narrative

South America was the last major landmass settled by our species. The evidence from archaeology, genetics, and linguistics converges on a picture of rapid colonization by a small founding group derived from the Paleoindian florescence in North America.

Thomas F. Lynch (Ch. 3 of [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-1]]) describes the founding population as an extension of "Eurasian Upper Paleolithic lifeways": "it is clear that the human career in America is an extension of Eurasian Upper Paleolithic lifeways." These people had already adapted to the Beringian mammoth steppe and entered North America by ~12,000–11,500 BP, with their descendants reaching the southern tip of South America within approximately 1,000 years.

The favoured route into South America ran through the Isthmus of Panama and Darién. Palaeoenvironmental evidence — pollen cores from La Yeguada (Panama), lake records from Venezuela and Colombia, and glaciological data — indicates that late-glacial cooling created a strip of savannah and thorn-scrub along the Pacific side of lower Central America, allowing megafauna and subsequently human hunters to move south. Lynch writes that Collinvaux portrays the late-glacial Venezuelan landscape as "principally savanna land but with patches of woodland or even forest within a day or two's march… an easy land for people to penetrate if their culture was one already adapted to more open regions in the northern continent."

**Fishtail/Paleoindian projectile points** are the primary material signature of the initial colonisation. Stemmed fishtail points (sometimes fluted) are found from Ecuador and Colombia southward through Argentina and Chile, with the best-dated examples from Fell's Cave, Cerro La China, and Cueva del Medio in the Southern Cone dating to 11,000–10,600 BP (radiocarbon). Lynch notes a stylistic connection to North American Clovis: "The fluted, eared, and waisted points of North America and the fluted fishtail points of South America are both too complex and stylized to have been independently invented a few hundred years apart." A single Clovis-type point, unfuted on neither side, was recovered from the earliest layer at Fell's Cave.

Kill and butchering sites — horse and mastodon remains in association with Paleoindian artefacts — are documented at Tagua-Tagua (11,380 ± 320 BP) in central Chile and at Quereo I (11,600 ± 190 BP). Together, these bracket the Paleoindian horizon in South America "roughly between 12,000 and 10,000 radiocarbon years ago."

Anna C. Roosevelt (Ch. 4) notes that the Central Andean record diverges from the North American big-game-hunting model: "Archaeology provides little support for the hypothesis that the Central Andean highlands were a corridor for the penetration of North American big-game hunters into South America. Rather, it reveals a distinct coastal fishing and plant-collecting culture that differs from the Clovis or Folsom cultures of North America in ecological adaptation and material culture." The Paijan culture (coastal Peru, ~11,000–8,000 BP), with its large stemmed fish-spearing points and diverse marine/terrestrial diet, exemplifies this regional divergence.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Paleoindian radiation from North America through Panama — the direct cultural and demographic source
- `contributed_to:` Late-glacial climatic corridor (savannah strip along Pacific Central America) — an enabling environmental condition
- `contributed_to:` Focal subsistence strategy on migratory megafauna — the social and ecological engine that drove rapid long-distance movement
- `preceded_by:` [[unknown]] — the initial peopling of North America (~12,000–11,500 BP) set the absolute chronological ceiling

## Consequence Analysis

The colonisation was effectively instantaneous in geological terms. Lynch and Turner agree: "the early Palaeo-Indian hunters of mammoth and other large animals were in North America by 12,000 B.P. Their descendants reached the southern tip of Chile 1,000 years later."

The mtDNA studies of D.C. Wallace and colleagues identified four primary maternal lineages shared across all Amerindian peoples, "suggesting that the original Paleoindian group, as it separated from the Asian gene pool, was fairly small." Christy Turner's dental morphology studies (Sinodont pattern) point to northeast Asia as the ancestral homeland for all South American peoples, with very little divergence between the earliest Paleoindian specimens from Tierra del Fuego and later Native Americans — consistent with a rapid expansion from a genetically homogeneous founder group.

The Paleoindian horizon gives way — gradually and regionally variable — to the [[south-american-archaic|Archaic stage]] after ~10,000 BP, driven by megafaunal extinctions, postglacial climate change, and demographic growth.

## Actors

No named individual actors are recoverable for the Paleoindian entry. The founding group is inferred to have been small (on the order of four female lineages at the Beringian bottleneck, per Wallace's mtDNA evidence). The first unambiguously documented Paleoindian presence in South America comes from highland Colombia (~11,700 BP at Tibito) and from Tierra del Fuego (~11,000 BP at Fell's Cave).

## Historiography

The central historiographical axis in this debate is the **"short chronology" vs. "long chronology"** dispute:

**Mainstream (short chronology):** Lynch, Christy Turner, C. Vance Haynes, Richard Morlan, and the majority of North and South American archaeologists place the founding entry through Beringia at ~12,000–11,500 BP, consistent with the Clovis horizon. Linguistic diversification, mtDNA evidence, and dental morphology all point to a single, comparatively late entry. Lynch notes that "the patterns of dental morphology, immunoglobulin allotypes, and linguistic diversification point to a colonization through eastern Siberia only 12,000 to 15,000 radiocarbon years ago."

**Pre-Clovis (long chronology):** A minority — including Alan Bryan, Ruth Gruhn, and a number of European and Brazilian scholars — argue for human occupation before 12,000 BP, pointing to sites such as Monte Verde (Chile), Taima-Taima (Venezuela), Pedra Furada (Brazil), and Alice Boer (Brazil). Lynch systematically evaluates and largely rejects these claims: "The bone evidence, then, for a pre-Paleoindian occupation of South America, is essentially nil."

A second controversy concerns the **route of entry**: the inland corridor through Beringia is favoured by most, but coastal/maritime routes along the North Pacific have scholarly advocates (Fladmark, Gruhn).

See [[monte-verde]] for site-specific treatment of the pre-Clovis dating disputes.

**Fagan & Durrani (2020)** add a genetic dimension to the entry-timing and route debate. The **Beringian Standstill Hypothesis** holds that deep-sea-core and drilling research shows the Bering Land Bridge itself was relatively warmer and moister — not simply barren steppe-tundra — owing to North Pacific oceanic influence. Genetic data indicate Native Americans descend from a population genetically isolated on the Land Bridge through the Last Glacial Maximum, estimated at no more than 10,000 people, who only began expanding southward after warming set in around 16,000–17,000 BP. This is offered as an explanation for the gap between an early genetic divergence date and the archaeological record south of the ice sheets. While consensus still favors first settlement south of the ice at roughly 15,000 BP (immediately post-LGM), Fagan & Durrani flag that "current DNA evidence is suggesting a first settlement as early as 23,000 years ago" — a significant, contested revision consistent with pre-Clovis positions.

On route: Fagan & Durrani note the Cordilleran ice sheet had retreated from the Pacific coast by 17,000 BP, opening a possible coastal route, while the Cordilleran and Laurentide sheets did not partially separate to open an interior ice-free corridor until roughly 14,000 BP — favoring, on timing grounds, an earlier coastal/maritime passage by skin boats over the interior corridor. They are candid that "we simply do not know" which route was used, in part because candidate coastal sites are now submerged. The earliest evidence for the coastal route so far is at Haida Gwaii (Queen Charlotte Islands, British Columbia), with sites dated to roughly 13,000 BP — the highest coastal-route evidence currently known.

On Monte Verde specifically, Fagan & Durrani give Tom Dillehay's site a radiocarbon range of roughly 12,220–11,390 BC (~11,800–12,000 BC in their body text), with wooden house remains (a rectangular, skin-covered structure on log/plank foundations), clay hearths, a wide range of plant foods including wild potatoes, chewed seaweed carried some 55 km from the Pacific coast, and wooden tools including hafted scrapers — treated as a major, well-established pre-Clovis site.
