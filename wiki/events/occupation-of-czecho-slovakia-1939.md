# Occupation of the Remainder of Czechoslovakia (15 March 1939)

**Narrative**: After the Munich Agreement (30 September 1938) left the dismembered Czechoslovak state exposed and at Germany's mercy, Hitler regarded the 'small plan' (Sudetenland) as only temporary. The 'big plan' — total destruction of Czechoslovakia — had been his aim since spring 1938 and was merely deferred. A new Wehrmacht directive of 21 October 1938 explicitly ordered preparations for 'liquidation of remainder of the Czech state' (plus Memelland occupation). 'It must be possible to smash at any time the remainder of the Czech State should it pursue an anti-German policy.'

The Czechs, recognizing their peril, bent over backwards to accommodate German interests and avoid extinction as a state. This made the occupation politically unnecessary in a narrow sense, but Hitler was driven by personal 'sheer bloody mindedness' ('That fellow Chamberlain has spoiled my entry into Prague'), the desire for full military triumph denied him, and powerful economic/strategic calculations. Bohemia and Moravia held the vast bulk of Czech industry (four-fifths of engineering, machine-tool, electrical); Skoda produced superior arms (machine-guns, field-guns, anti-aircraft, heavy artillery) and locomotives; large gold and foreign-currency reserves existed; raw materials (tungsten, uranium) beckoned. Enough Czech arms were seized to equip another twenty German divisions. Strategically, the mountain-rimmed territories offered an ideal platform for further eastward expansion, opened the road to the Balkans, strengthened Germany's position against Poland, and consolidated eastern defences in case of western conflict.

On 15 March 1939 German troops occupied the remainder. President Hácha was summoned to Berlin and coerced. The Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia was proclaimed; Slovakia became a German satellite under Jozef Tiso; Ruthenia (Carpatho-Ukraine) went to Hungary. Hitler entered Prague. Goebbels noted the Czechs 'must always have something to lose' (persecution milder than later meted to Poles). Hitler himself showed remarkably little interest in the Czechs once the occupation was complete. Memel followed days later (22–23 March) as the last bloodless annexation.

**Causal Analysis**:
- `caused_by:` Hitler's post-Munich determination to complete the 1938 project; 21 Oct 1938 directive; economic and strategic imperatives that could not be met while Czech autonomy persisted.
- `contributed_to:` Munich settlement's exposure of Czech weakness; Hitler's personal psychology (Austrian dislike of Czechs + resentment at Western 'cheating'); prior economic boost from 1938 gains making further exploitation logical.
- `preceded_by:` Munich Agreement (temporary interruption, not halt, of full destruction plans); summer 1938 two-stage thinking.
- `part_of:` Sequence of unopposed expansions testing Western resolve.

**Consequence Analysis**:
- `produced:` Protectorate and satellite structure; massive resource seizure (arms for 20 divisions, industry, gold); strategic platform for Poland and beyond.
- `enabled:` Polish crisis (guarantee followed directly); accelerated German war preparations; confirmation to Hitler of Western inaction.
- `contributed_to:` [[events/invasion-of-poland-1939]] (improved logistics and confidence); end of any lingering Munich illusions.
- `contrasts_with:` Earlier limited Sudeten concession at Munich; demonstrated that appeasement only invited the next demand.
- Long-term: tore up post-Versailles order; made general European war far more likely.

**Actors**:
- Primary: Hitler (decision, directive, occupation order); Hácha (coerced signatory).
- Secondary: Ribbentrop (diplomatic pressure); Göring (economic exploitation interest); Keitel (military execution); Goebbels (propaganda framing and internal commentary).

**Historiography**:
Kershaw (*Nemesis*, Range 02) emphasizes the mixture of personal psychology ('cheated' of triumph; 'bloody mindedness'), rational economic/strategic calculation, and pre-Munich planning already pointing to a second stage. The occupation was not forced by immediate political necessity but by Hitler's insistence on the 'big plan.' Economic data (Skoda value, division-equivalents) and strategic geography are stressed. The event marked the definitive end of any British/French hope that Hitler was a man of limited, negotiable ambitions. 'Munich' now meant betrayal to Czechs and weakness to Hitler. Source base: contemporary directives, Goebbels diary, economic assessments, diplomatic records. Strong on Hitler's motives and immediate consequences; cross-references needed for Czech internal politics and Western reactions. No major factual dispute on mechanics; interpretive weight on whether economic or personal/ideological drivers predominated (Kershaw integrates both under Hitler's personalized rule).

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer's account of the 21 October 1938 directive (top-secret, issued eleven days after Munich, before Sudeten occupation was even complete) matches the wiki's existing account and adds verbatim text: it ordered Wehrmacht readiness for "the liquidation of the remainder of Czechoslovakia" and "occupation of the Memel district," aiming for "the swift occupation of Bohemia and Moravia and the cutting off of Slovakia" via surprise assault depriving Czechoslovakia "of all possibility of organized resistance." A 17 December 1938 Keitel supplement specified the action be carried out by peacetime forces only, to "clearly appear... a peaceful action and not a warlike undertaking" (Shirer 1960).

Shirer gives fuller mechanics for Slovak "independence" (14 March 1939) as a Berlin-engineered operation: Seyss-Inquart and Gauleiter Josef Bürckel, with five German generals, interrupted a Slovak cabinet meeting in Bratislava on the night of 11 March demanding immediate proclamation of independence. Monsignor Tiso was flown (against his will — he had intended to take the train) to Berlin on 13 March and met Hitler, flanked by Ribbentrop, Brauchitsch, and Keitel. Hitler told him: "It was a question not of days but of hours. If Slovakia wished to become independent he would support and even guarantee it... If she hesitated... he would leave the fate of Slovakia to events for which he was no longer responsible." Germans then drafted both Tiso's "request" telegram and Slovakia's declaration of independence, which Tiso read to the Slovak parliament on 14 March under threat that German troops would occupy the country otherwise (Shirer 1960).

Documentary detail on Hungary's parallel seizure of Ruthenia: Regent Miklós Horthy's handwritten letter to Hitler (13 March 1939) read, "YOUR EXCELLENCY: Heartfelt thanks!... On Thursday, the 16th, a frontier incident will take place, to be followed Saturday by the big thrust." No incident was in fact needed; Hungarian troops simply entered Ruthenia at 6am on 15 March in step with the German invasion, and the "Republic of Carpatho-Ukraine" (proclaimed 14 March) lasted 24 hours before annexation (Shirer 1960).

Shirer's account of the "Ordeal of Dr. Hácha" adds significant verbatim detail to this page's existing (Evans-sourced) coverage: President Emil Hácha and Foreign Minister Chvalkovsky were received by Hitler at 1:15 a.m. on 15 March, flanked by Ribbentrop, Weizsäcker, Göring (recalled from San Remo), Keitel, and Hitler's physician Dr. Theodor Morell (present in anticipation). Hácha groveled, asking himself whether it was even a good thing for Czechoslovakia to have been an independent state; Hitler informed him German troops would enter at 6 a.m. regardless, gave the ultimatum choice between "peaceful" entry with nominal autonomy or resistance "broken by brute force," and cited the German proverb "The Moor has done his duty; the Moor can go." Per French ambassador Coulondre's contemporaneous dispatch, quoted by Shirer, Göring and Ribbentrop "literally hunted Dr. Hácha and M. Chvalkovsky round the table," pushing pens into their hands and threatening that "half of Prague would lie in ruins from bombing within two hours" if they did not sign; Hácha fainted and was revived twice by injections from Dr. Morell before signing at 3:55 a.m., ceding Czech fate to Hitler's "protection." Hitler reportedly told his secretaries afterward: "Children! This is the greatest day of my life! I shall go down in history as the greatest German!" (Shirer 1960).

German troops entered Bohemia and Moravia at 6 a.m., 15 March 1939, meeting no resistance; Hitler proclaimed "Czechoslovakia has ceased to exist!" and slept that night in Hradschin Castle. The next day he proclaimed the "Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia" under a Reich Protector — Konstantin von Neurath, brought out of "cold storage" to placate British and French opinion — with Sudeten leaders Konrad Henlein and Karl Hermann Frank installed as Head of Civil Administration and Secretary of State respectively; Himmler and Frank soon controlled the protectorate's police apparatus (Shirer 1960).

Chamberlain used Slovakia's German-engineered "secession" as legal cover to disclaim the Munich guarantee of Czech frontiers: "The effect of this declaration put an end by internal disruption to the State whose frontier we had proposed to guarantee. His Majesty's Government cannot accordingly hold themselves any longer bound by this obligation" (15 March 1939) — while explicitly declining to accuse Hitler of bad faith: "I do not wish to associate myself today with any charges of that character." French ambassador Coulondre lodged a sharper protest than British ambassador Henderson; State Secretary Weizsäcker literally thrust the French note back at Coulondre, refusing to accept it, on 18 March (Shirer 1960).

[SHIRER-THESIS] Fates of key figures, noted by Shirer as a coda: Karl Hermann Frank was hanged near Prague 22 May 1946 by a postwar Czech court; Konrad Henlein committed suicide after arrest by Czech resistance in 1945; Chvalkovsky was killed in an Allied bombing of Berlin in 1944; Hácha was arrested by the Czechs 14 May 1945 and died before trial; Monsignor Tiso was hanged 18 April 1947.

## Historiography

The occupation is among the best-documented Nazi aggressions: the October–December 1938 premeditation directives, the engineered Slovak secession, and the Hácha coercion protocol all survived in the German Foreign Office files (DGFP) and were aired at Nuremberg — Shirer's account rests directly on them. Debate concerns Chamberlain's response (the Birmingham reversal) and whether March 1939 or Kristallnacht was the true terminus of appeasement; Taylor's minimizing reading contrasts with the documentary-intentionalist one. Collection coverage: strong (Evans, Kershaw, Taylor, Shirer).
