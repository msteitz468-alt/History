# Berlin Olympics (1936)

The XI Olympiad, staged by the Nazi regime as a global propaganda showcase of
a peaceful, orderly, prosperous "new Germany." Antisemitic signage was removed,
persecution publicly paused (on Hitler's direct instruction — a demonstration
that the regime could throttle antisemitism when convenient), and the Games
choreographed as spectacle (Riefenstahl's *Olympia*). Boycott campaigns abroad
failed; the propaganda success was substantial.

## Narrative (Evans, 2005)

The IOC award predated the regime; Hitler, initially sceptical of the Games'
internationalism, was converted by the American boycott campaign into seeing
an unmissable propaganda opportunity. The German team contained no Jews
(training facilities had been denied them since 1933); three half-Jews,
including fencer Helene Mayer, were recruited to defuse the boycott.
Antisemitic signs came down nationwide (Shirer reported it from Garmisch),
the *Stormer* was pulled from Berlin display, Hitler publicly distanced
himself from Streicher in June 1936, and his 1936 speeches barely mentioned
Jews — while Bormann reminded party officials in February 1936 that the aim
of shutting Jewry "out of every sphere of life... remains irremoveably
fixed." The famous Jesse Owens "snub" is legend — the IOC had barred Hitler
from selective congratulations — though Speer recorded Hitler privately
wanting non-whites barred from future Games. Riefenstahl's *Olympia*, the
first experimental television broadcast, and Strauss conducting his Olympic
Hymn completed the spectacle; Shirer's verdict as the Games closed: "the
Nazis have succeeded with their propaganda." Klemperer's diary saw through
the staging in real time. Hitler ordered Speer to design a far larger
stadium — after Tokyo 1940 the Games were to sit permanently in Berlin.

## Shirer (1960) Addition

[EYEWITNESS] Shirer describes the Games as a deliberate propaganda opportunity from the ground: antisemitic "Juden unerwuenscht" ("Jews Not Admitted") signs quietly removed for the duration; lavish parties by Göring, Ribbentrop, and Goebbels (Goebbels's "Italian Night" on the Pfaueninsel drew over 1,000 guests). Foreign visitors, especially British and American, came away impressed by an apparently "happy, healthy, friendly people," differing sharply from press dispatches — Shirer cites Lloyd George's 1936 Obersalzberg visit and public praise of Hitler as "a great man" who had solved unemployment, contrasted with Britain's own unresolved joblessness, as an example of how foreign statesmen were "taken in." Shirer himself was "violently attacked in the German press and on the radio, and threatened with expulsion" after reporting the temporary removal of antisemitic signage in a dispatch — a first-person data point on the regime's press-control apparatus, cross-linked from [[nuremberg-laws-1935]].
