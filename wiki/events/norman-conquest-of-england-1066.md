# The Norman Conquest of England (1066)

## Narrative

The **Norman Conquest** united the duchy of **Normandy** with the kingdom of
**England** under **[[william-the-conqueror|William the Conqueror]]** in 1066, creating
a cross-Channel **Anglo-Norman realm**. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Marjorie
Chibnall, Ch. 7, "England and Normandy, 1042–1137") frames the century after Edward the
Confessor's accession (1042) as "the century of the Norman Conquest."

When the childless **Edward the Confessor** — himself half-Norman and raised in exile in
Normandy — died in January 1066, the English magnates chose **Harold Godwinson**, the
most powerful earl, as king. William, duke of Normandy, claimed the throne by an alleged
promise from Edward and an oath sworn by Harold, and secured **papal backing** for his
expedition. Harold, having just destroyed a Norwegian invasion under Harald Hardrada at
**Stamford Bridge** (25 September 1066), marched south and was defeated and killed by
William at the **Battle of Hastings** (14 October 1066). William was crowned at
Westminster on Christmas Day 1066, and over the next five years crushed English revolts
(the "Harrying of the North," 1069–70), replacing almost the entire Anglo-Saxon landed
aristocracy with a Norman and French one.

Chibnall stresses that William inherited an **unusually strong, wealthy, and
administratively sophisticated kingdom** — the late Anglo-Saxon state of shires,
hundreds, the geld (territorial taxation), and a unified coinage — which the Normans
took over and exploited rather than dismantled. The great inquest of **Domesday Book
(1086)** epitomises the fusion of Norman lordship with Anglo-Saxon administrative
machinery.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the **disputed succession** to the childless Edward the Confessor and the
rival claims of Harold, William, and Harald Hardrada.
`caused_by:` the long-standing **dynastic ties** between England and Normandy (Edward's
Norman mother and upbringing) that gave William a plausible claim.
`contributed_to:` the **military and ducal strength** William had built in Normandy, and
the **papal sanction** that lent his invasion legitimacy in the reform era.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **Anglo-Norman realm** and the wholesale **replacement of the
Anglo-Saxon aristocracy** by a Norman/French elite, with profound effects on language,
law, landholding (the introduction of continental tenurial relations), and the Church.
`produced:` **Domesday Book (1086)**, the most detailed administrative survey in
pre-modern Europe.
`enabled:` the lasting **entanglement of England in continental French politics** — the
cross-Channel realm that would grow into the [[angevin-empire|Angevin empire]] under
[[henry-ii-of-england|Henry II]] and generate centuries of Anglo-French conflict.

## Historiography

NCMH IV's Chibnall represents the mature view that stresses **continuity** as much as
rupture: the conquest grafted Norman lordship onto a precociously strong English state,
and the Anglo-Norman realm was a "short-lived" but consequential political union. The
older debate over how revolutionary 1066 was — total catastrophe for the English vs.
substantial institutional continuity — is resolved here toward a synthesis of violent
elite replacement atop administrative continuity.
