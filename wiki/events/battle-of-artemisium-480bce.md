# Battle of Artemisium (480 BCE)

## Narrative

At about the same time as the engagements at Thermopylae, the two fleets were fighting for three days running at Artemisium. In later popular accounts Artemisium is treated as a victory. According to Herodotus, the battle was about even, but the Greeks, because of the damage to so many of their ships, had decided to start a withdrawal and had no sooner begun it than the news of the catastrophe of Leonidas arrived.

It seems basically that this account has to be accepted as the admission of a defeat. The withdrawal from the northern point of Euboea meant sacrificing Thermopylae, and that sacrifice meant the evacuation of all of middle Greece and Attica.

It is very remarkable that the Persians allowed them to withdraw unpursued. The Persian admirals knew that their land army was fighting before a narrow pass; they knew what great merit they would earn if they drove off the Greek ships and thereby facilitated the envelopment of Thermopylae by water. Nevertheless, they reportedly did not sail out again to combat on the fourth day; only after receiving the report that the Greeks had sailed away did they move out from their anchorage.

It appears, therefore, that the Greeks gave a good account of themselves in the three-day battle. It is perhaps a false account that the withdrawal was already decided before the arrival of the report of Thermopylae. At any rate, the circumstances would appear to be much more understandable if one assumes that it was only this news that swung the balance and brought the final decision to withdraw.

However that may be, it appears certain that the Greek fleet showed itself the equal of the Persian fleet on the open sea and that it could not be defeated in a three-day fight.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Greek strategic plan that the real decision must be at sea; the need to assemble contingents; Persian caution until the land army arrived.

**contributed_to:** Damage to many Greek ships over three days; the fall of Thermopylae making further northern position untenable.

**preceded_by:** Tempe abandonment and the shift to fleet primacy.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced:** Decision to fight the decisive sea battle farther south (Salamis area).

**enabled:** Repair of damaged ships in home ports (much easier for Greeks than Persians); return for reinforcements.

**concurrent_with:** The heroic auxiliary action at Thermopylae.

From this we may conclude that the two fleets were quite equal in strength.

## Actors

Greek crews: partly excellent sailors but largely citizens with little sea-going experience (Attic farmers, charcoal burners, artisans with only emergency training from the two preceding years). Persian crews: professional sailors (Phoenicians and Ionians).

## Historiography

The fable that the Persians sent 200 ships around Euboea and all foundered in a storm is eliminated once fleet strengths are established as roughly equal. Herodotus himself points out technical superiority of the opponents and has Themistocles say expressly that the ships of the Greeks were less maneuverable. Later naval history (Athenian superiority in the Peloponnesian War) teaches how much difference professional training makes.

The report that the Greeks had already moved back once before the battle from Artemisium to the Euripus deserves no credence, since in that event Leonidas would also have had to evacuate Thermopylae.
