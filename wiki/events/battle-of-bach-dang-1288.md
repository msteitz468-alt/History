## Narrative

In the winter of 1287–88, the third Mongol/Yuan invasion under Togan (land forces) and Omar (naval forces) advanced to Thang Long and then, after a month occupying an empty and provisionless capital, withdrew to Van Kiep. Their supply fleet had already been destroyed near Van Don by Tran Khanh Du days after the main invasion fleet passed. Tran forces gathered and besieged Togan and Omar at Van Kiep; after another month, with food exhausted, the Mongols broke out to return to China. Omar's route took his fleet back down and out through the Bach Dang estuary, where [[tran-hung-dao|Tran Quoc Tuan]] had prepared an ambush after "fighting and foraging his way back up the Bach Dang River" in the preceding weeks (lines 3851–52). The text does not describe the tactical mechanics of the ambush in detail within this slice — no mention of the traditional iron-tipped stakes driven into the riverbed (a device associated with the earlier 938 Bach Dang battle) appears in the extracted passage — but records the outcome plainly: "Omar went downriver where he was captured and his entire fleet was lost when Tran Quoc Tuan ambushed him at the Bach Dang estuary" (lines 3856–58). Meanwhile Togan escaped overland through Lang Son, and the Yunnan Mongol column withdrew the way it had come.

Kublai never reconciled himself to the defeat and continued issuing invasion plans until his death in 1294, but ongoing Mongol campaigns in Burma and Java, and growing war-weariness among his followers, meant no further expedition against Dai Viet was launched. In 1289 the Tran released most Mongol prisoners of war, but Omar — whose return Kublai specifically demanded — was deliberately drowned when the boat carrying him north was contrived to sink.

## Causal Analysis

The victory was **enabled** by the prior destruction of the Mongol supply fleet (a separate action, not part of the battle itself), which left Omar's force weakened and poorly provisioned before the retreat began. It was **caused_by** the deliberate Tran strategy of forcing the Mongols into a starvation retreat at Van Kiep and then intercepting the only viable river route to the sea. The choice of the Bach Dang estuary specifically is **analogous_to [[battle-of-bach-dang-938]]**, the earlier destruction of a Southern Han fleet at the same location, and the estuary had already been fortified with an "underwater obstacle" noted during Ly-era defensive preparations against Song in the 1070s (line 1470), indicating long-standing recognition of the site's tactical value for naval ambush.

## Consequence Analysis

The battle **produced** the definitive end of the third Mongol invasion and, in practice, of Yuan military ambitions against Dai Viet altogether for the rest of Kublai's reign. It **contributed_to** the Tran dynasty's peak prestige and the extensive post-war reward/punishment reckoning (purges of wartime defectors, honors for veterans) that followed in 1288–89.

## Historiography

This slice of Taylor's text gives a comparatively brief, matter-of-fact account of the 1288 Bach Dang battle relative to its later fame in Vietnamese national memory — it does not include the stake-trap tactical description or troop/casualty figures found in some other treatments of this battle; this may reflect the specific section of Taylor's narrative extracted here rather than an omission in the full source, and should be verified against other sections of Taylor's book or other sources before assuming the stakes tactic is unattested in this monograph. Taylor frames the battle's significance primarily through its strategic consequence (ending Yuan invasion plans) rather than through tactical narrative, consistent with his broader emphasis in this slice on political and dynastic causation over battlefield mechanics. The battle's symbolic resonance with the 938 Bach Dang victory over the Southern Han (part_of the "southern imperial mandate" ideological tradition articulated by Le Van Huu in 1272 and the Ly Thuong Kiet poem tradition) made it a natural culminating episode in Tran-era claims to a distinct southern political destiny.
