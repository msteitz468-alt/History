## Narrative

The Australian Labor Party split in 1955 with the formation of the Anti-Communist Labor Party, which subsequently became the Democratic Labor Party (DLP). The split was the culmination of years of internal conflict over communism and its influence in the trade union movement, catalysed by the Petrov defection and H.V. Evatt's mishandling of its aftermath.

The pre-history lay in two parallel anti-communist movements within the labour movement. The ALP had established Industrial Groups in some unions to combat communist influence, as revealed at the 1945 ACTU congress. Simultaneously, a young Catholic social activist, B.A. Santamaria, was using parishes to organise Catholic anti-communists into the Catholic Social Studies Movement (generally called "the Movement"). The two organisations overlapped, especially in Melbourne where the "powerful and charismatic Catholic Archbishop of Melbourne, Daniel Mannix, provided the Movement with official sanction and support."

The immediate trigger was the Petrov affair of 1954. Labor lost the 1954 election narrowly and Evatt was convinced Menzies had "cynically used Petrov's defection to fan public fears about communism and rob him of victory." When members of his staff were named in the royal commission, Evatt appeared in their defence, "making such intemperate claims about conspiracies that he was banned from further appearance." His "paranoia about disloyalty in the party grew."

In 1955 the party split. Taking advantage of Labor's turmoil, Menzies called an election in December 1955; Labor's vote dropped by 5.4 per cent and further in 1958, to a primary vote of 42.8 per cent — its lowest. In Victoria, DLP defections brought down the Labor government of John Cain senior.

## Consequence Analysis

The DLP had "a core vote of around 10 per cent" but "little chance of winning lower house seats." Its electoral strategy was to deny Labor office by directing preferences to the Liberal Party and to win Senate seats. "The DLP ensured Coalition victories in the close federal elections of 1961 and 1969." This structurally extended the Menzies era: without the DLP preference flow, Labor might have won in 1961 (when the Coalition held on by only one seat).

In 1963 Catholic DLP supporters received a reward when Menzies reversed longstanding policy and announced Commonwealth funds for church schools' science facilities.

The split condemned Labor to opposition until 1972, forcing internal modernisation under Whitlam that was itself shaped by the need to appeal beyond the working-class base that the DLP had partly captured.

## Historiography

Key works: Robert Murray, *The Split: Australian Labor in the Fifties* (1970/1984); Bruce Duncan, *Crusade or Conspiracy: Catholics and the Anti-Communist Struggle in Australia* (UNSW Press, 2001); Brian Costar, Peter Love and Paul Strangio (eds), *The Great Labor Schism: A Retrospective* (Scribe, 2005). The split has been interpreted as: (1) a principled anti-communist defence of democratic unionism; (2) Santamaria's quasi-conspiratorial infiltration of the labour movement; and (3) a product of Evatt's personal psychological instability. Most recent work emphasises all three factors.
