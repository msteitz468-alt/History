# The Persian Conquest of Egypt (525 BCE)

In **525 BCE** **[[cambyses|Cambyses II]]**, son of [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]],
conquered Egypt, ending the native **[[saite-egypt|Twenty-Sixth (Saite) Dynasty]]** and
making the Nile valley a satrapy of the **[[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid empire]]** — the
[[achaemenid-egypt|First Persian Period]]. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 3g,
Ray) treats it as "one of the critical dates" in Egyptian history.

## Narrative

The Persian army marched south to **Gaza** and crossed the Sinai with the help of the
**nomadic Arabs**, who supplied water — a service Herodotus thought indispensable. Past
the desolate Lake Serbonis they reached **Pelusium**, where the **decisive battle** was
fought (Greek and Carian mercenaries serving on both sides; Herodotus claims to have seen
the bones on the field 75 years later). The Persians then followed the river to
**Memphis**, which surrendered after some resistance; **Psammetichus III** fell into their
hands and was later eliminated as a possible focus of revolt. A visit to the temple of
**Neith at Sais** set the seal on a conquest completed by late spring 525.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the momentum of [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid]] expansion (Cyrus had taken
Babylon and the Levant; Egypt was the obvious next prize) and the **strategic logic** Ray
puts in Cambyses' mouth — *as long as Egypt is free, Persia can never securely hold
[[eber-nari|Syria-Palestine]]*, since a pharaoh can always subvert that coast and shelter
traitors.
contributed_to: the moment was opportune — **Amasis had just died**, leaving the young and
"inexperienced" Psammetichus III, and Egyptian officials (notably the admiral
[[udjahorresne]]) were ready to **collaborate**.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **end of native independence** and the **[[achaemenid-egypt|27th Dynasty]]** —
a century of Persian rule; Persian command of the **eastern Mediterranean approaches** and
the [[phoenicians|Phoenician]]–Egyptian naval resources.
enabled: a recurring **strategic wound** for Persia. Ray's verdict: "in 525, unwittingly,
[Persia] dealt herself a wound through which she began to bleed to death" — Egypt was a
country Persia "could hardly govern, yet could not afford to lose," and **Athens** soon
realized that "one of the quickest routes to Persepolis lay through Memphis."
contributed_to: the hostile **"mad Cambyses" tradition**, and conversely the Egyptian
strategy of **assimilating the conqueror** as a legitimate pharaoh ([[udjahorresne]]'s
titulary).

## Historiography

The conquest is known mainly from **Herodotus** (III.1–16), whose lurid Cambyses Ray
substantially **rehabilitates** using the hieroglyphic autobiography of
**[[udjahorresne]]**, the Apis burial records (which contradict the bull-stabbing story),
and a temple-finance decree. The personal motives Herodotus ascribes to Cambyses are, Ray
suggests, no more reliable than the strategic calculation a modern historian would impute.
