# Hoßbach Memorandum Meeting (5 November 1937)

## Narrative

On the afternoon of 5 November 1937, in the Reich Chancellery, Hitler addressed the Commanders-in-Chief of the three armed services, the War Minister, and the Foreign Minister in what became known as the Hoßbach Memorandum (after Colonel Friedrich Hoßbach, Hitler's army adjutant, who took notes). The meeting had been called ostensibly to resolve a steel allocation dispute for the services, but Hitler used it to deliver a lengthy monologue on Germany's need for 'living space' and the timing of expansion by force.

Hoßbach had not been forewarned of the content and began taking notes because he sensed the importance. No formal minutes were kept; Hoßbach later reconstructed the record from his diary notes.

Hitler opened by stressing the need to expand German 'living space'. Without it, 'sterility' would set in, leading to social disorder. He dismissed limited autarky and reliance on the world economy as inadequate. 'Living space' meant European territory for agricultural production, not colonies. Britain and France stood in the way but were weakened. Germany's problem could only be solved by force, accompanied by risks.

He outlined three scenarios:
1. By 1943–5 at the latest (time not on Germany's side; relative armaments strength would decline; economic difficulties could create a 'weakening-point of the regime').
2–3. Earlier if France became internally paralysed or embroiled in war with another power (e.g. with Italy over Spain/Mediterranean). In such cases, attack Austria and Czechoslovakia simultaneously to secure the eastern flank.

Britain and France, he conjectured, had already written off Czechoslovakia. Empire problems (India) and reluctance for long European war would deter Britain. France would not act without Britain. Italy would not object to the elimination of Czechoslovakia; its attitude to Austria depended on Mussolini's survival. Poland would be preoccupied with Russia; Russia with Japan.

The first objective would be to overthrow Czechoslovakia and Austria 'lightning fast' ('blitzartig schnell'). Annexation would free twelve divisions and provide food for 5–6 million after expelling 3 million.

The military leaders were not convinced. Neurath thought a Mediterranean conflict as Hitler described highly unlikely. The generals pointed to deficiencies in the analysis and insisted Germany must not find itself at war with Britain and France. Göring (initially quiet) still favoured trying to reach agreement with Britain. Only Raeder seemed unperturbed.

Hitler was aware of the negative reaction. He later refused more than once to read Hoßbach's memorandum.

## Analysis (Kershaw)

The meeting was Hitler's attempt to impress his thinking on the military leadership at a time when his adventurism was already causing alarm. The arguments were consistent with what he had been telling Goebbels and Party leaders. The 'Hoßbach meeting' made explicit to the armed forces chiefs the likely timing and circumstances for action against Austria and Czechoslovakia.

It did not create new plans; it revealed Hitler's mind. The reaction of the listeners (especially Blomberg, Fritsch, Neurath, and Beck when he saw the record) showed the growing gulf between Hitler's risk-taking and the caution of the professionals. The meeting is a key documentary landmark for the shift toward war.

## Verbatim / Direct from Kershaw (this range)

- 'It is not the aim of this memorandum to prophesy the time when the untenable situation in Europe will become an open crisis... I only want... to set down my conviction that this crisis cannot and will not fail to arrive.'
- On timing: 'unalterable determination to solve the German problem of space by 1943–5 at the latest'.
- Attack on Czechs to be 'lightning fast' ('blitzartig schnell').
- Living space as 'the task of the political leadership one day to solve this problem'.

## Consequences and Links

The meeting contributed directly to the context of the Blomberg–Fritsch crisis (Hitler already losing faith in the military leadership). It fed into revised Case Green planning (Jodl's amendment including 'offensive war' against Czechoslovakia). It confirmed for Hitler the need to remove obstacles to rapid action.

## Historiography Note (Kershaw)

Kershaw presents the meeting as a critical window into Hitler's thinking: ideological imperatives (Lebensraum, anti-Bolshevism) framed in strategic terms that accommodated but went beyond the military and economic interests of his listeners. The lack of detailed plans reflected Hitler's preference for broad directives and opportunism rather than rigid timetables. The negative military reaction foreshadowed the 1938 crisis of confidence.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer supplies the immediate institutional backdrop: Blomberg's 24 June 1937 "Top Secret" directive (only 4 copies) to the three service chiefs stated "the general political situation justifies the supposition that Germany need not consider an attack from any side," yet demanded "constant preparedness for war... to make possible the military exploitation of politically favorable opportunities." It defined two contingencies — Case "Rot" (war in the West, a French surprise attack) and Case "Grün" (war in the Southeast, a surprise German operation against Czechoslovakia "to parry the imminent attack of a superior enemy coalition," conditions "to be created beforehand") — plus three special cases: "Otto" (armed intervention in Austria against a Habsburg restoration), "Richard" (Spain), and a contingency covering England/Poland/Lithuania joining against Germany, warning "England will employ all her available economic and military resources against us" if drawn in (Shirer 1960).

Shirer's account of the meeting itself matches the wiki's Kershaw-sourced narrative closely and adds verbatim texture: the conference ran 4:15–8:30 p.m., 5 November 1937; Hitler framed his remarks as his "last will and testament" in case of death, and stated Germany's future was "wholly conditional upon the solving of the need for space [Lebensraum]," achievable only "in immediate proximity to the Reich," by force, since "there had never been spaces without a master." On timing, Hitler again distinguished three cases, specifying that under Case III (France embroiled in war with another state, likely Italy) he was "resolved to take advantage of it, whenever it happened, even as early as 1938." Benefits cited included better strategic frontiers, roughly 12 million "Germans" annexed, food for 5–6 million more Reich Germans, and manpower for 12 new Army divisions (Shirer 1960).

[SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer stresses that Blomberg, Fritsch, and Neurath objected on practical/military grounds — Germany not yet ready for a wider war — not on moral grounds: "Not... because they were struck down by the immorality of their Leader's proposals but for more practical reasons." Within three months all three were removed from office ([[blomberg-fritsch-affair-1938]]). Shirer treats this distinction as evidence of the generals' moral complicity rather than principled resistance — a position that should be read as one interpretive stance among others on officer-corps culpability, not adopted as wiki-voice.

The May 1939 Obersalzberg conference (23 May, 14 officers present) is explicitly framed by Shirer as a larger repeat performance of the Hossbach meeting, with the crucial difference that by 1939 the generals offered no objection at all — see [[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939]] and [[invasion-of-poland-1939]] for the fuller documentary record of that later conference.
