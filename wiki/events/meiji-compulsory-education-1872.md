## Narrative

On September 4, 1872, the emperor sent a message stressing the importance of education and announcing plans for a national educational system at every level. The plan called for eight universities and 53,760 elementary schools. All children from the age of six would be required to enter elementary schools; the system was designed to meet the different needs of boys and girls, urban and rural children. The French educational system was adopted as the model. These plans implemented the promise made by the emperor in the Five Article Charter Oath to "seek learning throughout the world."

The announcement came shortly after the emperor's return from a tour of western Japan (July–August 1872) during which he consistently visited schools, observed classes, and inspected local products. The emperor watched pupils be questioned on "punctuation, arithmetic, and foreign languages," visited schools where both boys and girls of the aristocracy were taught foreign languages (English, German, and French) and manual arts — and where children of the commoner class were now also admitted. Wherever he went, the emperor gave audiences to foreign teachers (yatoi) employed as instructors in Western science and languages.

Keene identifies education as one of the three pillars of Meiji's vision for Japan as a modern nation: "He seems to have decided that Japan's future as a modern nation would depend on these three factors: industry, education, and the military." The September 4 message made the educational component of this vision formal policy.

Popular reaction was not uniformly enthusiastic. Among the grievances cited in the peasant uprisings of 1873 was resentment at "paying taxes for schools." The education ordinance, like the conscription law, represented government impositions on rural communities that disrupted existing arrangements without immediate visible benefit to those who bore the costs.

### Emperor's Personal Interest in Education

Keene documents the emperor's consistent personal engagement with schools throughout his journeys. In Nagasaki, the local newspaper praised his visit for "having shaken its inhabitants from obstinate ignorance and eradicated their narrow-mindedness and for having weeded out the thorns that lay in the road to civilization and progress." At Kumamoto he visited Leroy L. Janes's Yōgakkō (School of Foreign Learning), where Mrs. Janes scattered flower petals over him from a second-floor balcony — a greeting he "never experienced before or afterward."

The emperor himself continued formal studies at the palace throughout this period, with monthly lectures on Japanese history, Western practical guides (notably Samuel Smiles's *Self-Help*, translated as *Saikoku risshihen*), poetry composition, and German (which he eventually abandoned). His tutor, Motoda Nagazane, delivered twelve lectures per month on Confucian texts, insisting that Eastern moral learning must complement Western scientific knowledge — the "Eastern ethics, Western science" synthesis that would characterize the Rescript on Education of 1890.

## Historiography

Keene frames the education ordinance in the context of the emperor's domestic journeys, emphasizing the personal dimension of Meiji's engagement with schools over the bureaucratic history of the *gakusei* itself. This is characteristic of the biography's focus on the emperor as agent. For the administrative and pedagogical history, the Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5 provides fuller coverage. The tension between the Confucian-moral content championed by Motoda and the Western-scientific curriculum advocated by reformers like Mori Arinori would define Japanese educational politics for decades.
