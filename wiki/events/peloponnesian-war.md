# The Peloponnesian War, 431–404 BCE

## Overview

The **Peloponnesian War** was the great conflict between **[[places/athens|Athens]]** (with its
[[delian-league|maritime empire]]) and **[[sparta]]** (with the
[[peloponnesian-league|Peloponnesian League]]) that consumed the Greek world for a
generation and ended Athenian power. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Chs. 9–11; Lewis,
Andrewes) treats it as the climax of the volume — and few events in history are "so closely
associated with their chronicler," **[[thucydides]]**, whose analysis still determines how
the war is understood. It falls into three phases:

1. **The [[archidamian-war|Archidamian War]]** (431–421) — the war of Periclean attrition,
   ended (inconclusively) by the **Peace of Nicias**.
2. **The Peace of Nicias and the [[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian Expedition]]** (421–413) —
   the unstable interlude and Athens' catastrophic gamble against [[syracuse|Syracuse]].
3. **The Ionian (Decelean) War** (413–404) — Spartan war from the fort at Decelea, Persian
   money (new treaty Feb 411 conceding Asian Greeks), the oligarchic revolution of 411 at [[places/athens|Athens]] (Four Hundred coup, Five Thousand interlude), Hellespont naval recovery (Cynossema, Cyzicus), and the **[[fall-of-athens-404bce|fall of Athens]]**. (See Kagan range on aftermath of Sicily, Rhodes/Euboea crises, Alcibiades' role, and Thrasybulus' Hellespont command.)

## The causes — aitiai and the "truest cause"

[[thucydides]] distinguishes the **immediate grievances** (*aitiai*) from the **truest
cause** (*alethestate prophasis*). The grievances were the quarrels over **Corcyra and
Epidamnus** (433), the siege of **Potidaea**, and above all the **Megarian Decree** barring
Megara from the empire's harbours and the Athenian market — with **Corinth** the most active
agitator. But the truest cause, "most inconspicuous in talk," was that "**the growth of
Athenian power provoked fear in the Spartans and forced them to make war**." Lewis stresses
Thucydides' theme of ***ananke*** (necessity): the Spartans "acted out of necessity; they had
no choice," and he doubts Thucydides thought the Athenians had much choice either — the war
was, on this reading, structurally **inevitable**, the breakdown of the bipolar order fixed
by the [[thirty-years-peace-446bce|Thirty Years' Peace]].

## The course in brief

The **[[archidamian-war|Archidamian War]]** tested [[pericles|Pericles']] defensive strategy
(evacuate Attica, hold the walls and the fleet, raid the coast, keep the allies and the
revenue) against annual Spartan invasions — derailed by the **[[plague-of-athens-430bce|plague]]**
that killed perhaps a quarter of Athens and Pericles himself (429). It produced the bitter
*stasis* Thucydides anatomized (Corcyra), the **Mytilenean** revolt and debate, the Athenian
triumph at **Pylos** (425, with 120 Spartiate prisoners), and **[[brasidas|Brasidas']]**
counter-stroke in Thrace (the loss of **Amphipolis**, 424). The simultaneous deaths of
**[[cleon]]** and **Brasidas** at Amphipolis (422) opened the way to the inconclusive **Peace
of Nicias** (421).

**Kagan (2003, Range 2)**: Theban surprise attack on Plataea (March 431, 300+ Thebans; first atrocity executions of 180 captives) triggered full war; Archidamus' political delay at Oenoe before ravaging Attica. Periclean "minimal deterrence" at Sybota (433) saved Corcyra but failed strategically. Plague killed 4,400 hoplites + 300 cavalry (~1/3 pop.); crowding policy blamed; religious oracles invoked against Pericles. Athenians sued for peace against his advice; war party (Cleon) drove terror executions. Pericles re-elected but died Sept 429; "no one of the Athenians now alive has put on mourning because of me." New politicians "more equal to one another" (Thuc. via Kagan); Cleon from trade/manufacturing class, "first to shout" in assembly. Phormio's Gulf victories showed Athenian naval edge; Spartan sea/Persia turn; reserve funds halved by 429/8. "The original strategy for victory had failed, and the Athenians had as yet formulated no substitute." (Ch. 5-9; Theban attack, plague, Pericles' last days, new politicians)

The peace collapsed; Athens, swayed by **Alcibiades**, launched the **[[sicilian-expedition|
Sicilian Expedition]]** (415–413), which ended in total disaster at [[syracuse|Syracuse]].
In the final **Ionian War**, Sparta — funded by **[[achaemenid-empire|Persia]]** and
fortified at **Decelea** in Attica — broke the empire at sea; the **oligarchic revolution of
411** convulsed Athens; and **Lysander's** victory at Aegospotami (405) led to the
**[[fall-of-athens-404bce|surrender of Athens]]** in 404.

## Consequences

produced: the **dissolution of the Athenian empire** and the (temporary) fall of the
democracy to the Thirty Tyrants; a brief, resented **Spartan hegemony**; the **exhaustion**
of the leading city-states and the demonstration (Davies' theme) that the [[city-state|polis]]
could not sustain a stable hegemony; and the opening that [[hellenistic-world|Macedon]] would
exploit within two generations. It also gave the West, through Thucydides, its **paradigm of
power politics** — empire as "tyranny," the Melian Dialogue, the analysis of *stasis* and the
gap between justice and necessity.

## Osborne (2000) — the fifth-century narrative

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed., 2000)]] (Ch. 7, Kallet) gives a
compact narrative confirming and extending the CAH/Kagan account. Osborne separates the
immediate "grievances and disputes" — the Corinth-Corcyra dispute over Epidamnus and the
Corinthian-aided revolt of Potidaea — from the real cause, "the one least articulated
openly": the growth of Athenian power and the fear it provoked in the Spartans, which
"compelled war to happen" (Thuc. 1.23.6). Sparta issued three escalating ultimatums in
432/1, the last simply demanding "let the Greeks be free and autonomous"; Athens's
counter-offer of arbitration was undercut by the fact that its *archê* itself was
non-negotiable. Osborne notes the irony that the Spartan king **Archidamus** argued against
premature war (hence the "Archidamian War" misnomer for the conflict's first phase); the
Spartan vote for war was carried instead by the ephor **Sthenelaidas** and by a Sparta
eager for battle after some fifty years without a major engagement.

**Pericles's strategy** (431) was to abandon the Attic countryside and retreat within the
**Long Walls** linking Athens to Piraeus (built since mid-century), avoiding land battle
while using naval control to provision the city and raid the Peloponnese. The **plague**
that broke out in Athens in 430 — said by tradition to have originated in Ethiopia — lasted
two years with a brief recurrence in 427, killed thousands including, according to
Plutarch, Pericles himself; Thucydides caught the disease and described its symptoms in
meticulous detail. Sparta destroyed **Plataea** in 427; **Lesbos** revolted in 428, banking
on Athenian weakness from the plague, but was suppressed before Spartan help could arrive.

In a brilliant sequence of manoeuvres, the Athenian general **Demosthenes** (the general —
not the later orator and opponent of Philip II, a distinct figure of the following century)
engineered the capture of the fort at **Pylos** and forced the surrender of **292 Spartan
hoplites** blockaded on the island of **Sphacteria** (425); Thucydides commented that
nothing in the war surprised the Greeks more, since Spartans regarded surrender as
anathema. **Cleon** persuaded Athens to reject peace proposals after Sphacteria;
**Brasidas**'s northern campaign (424) took **Amphipolis** — which Thucydides, the Athenian
general in the area, arrived too late to prevent (he was exiled in consequence) — while the
Athenian defeat at **Delium** killed nearly 1,000. The **Peace of Nicias** (421) followed
the deaths of both Cleon and Brasidas; it aimed at fifty years but lasted six, and its
unpeaceful character — continued Corinthian agitation, the [[battle-of-mantinea-418bce|
battle of Mantinea]] (418) — led Thucydides to treat the entire period 431–404 as a single
war.

In 416 Athens conquered the neutral, Dorian island of **Melos**, killing the men and
enslaving the women and children; Thucydides uses the episode for his **Melian
Dialogue**, illuminating the relationship between human nature and power. The
[[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian Expedition]] (415–413) was nominally launched to aid the ally
Egesta against Selinus and Syracuse, but Thucydides states plainly that the "truest
explanation" was a desire to conquer the whole island. Preceding its departure, the
mutilation of the Herms and reports of mock celebrations of the Eleusinian Mysteries (415)
scandalized Athens; **Alcibiades** was accused but his complicity unproven, and when his
enemies recalled him to stand trial he fled to Sparta instead, where — on his advice — the
Spartans fortified **Decelea** (413) in Attica. In the final stage (413–404) Spartan-Persian
cooperation, chiefly **Lysander**'s friendship with the Persian prince **Cyrus**, financed
the Spartan navy far more regularly than before, while Alcibiades shifted allegiance
repeatedly — Sparta, then Persia (Tissaphernes), then Athens again, then exile. Sparta
finally took the Athenian navy by surprise at **Aegospotami** (405), blockaded the Piraeus,
and forced Athens's total surrender (404); Xenophon records that the Spartans spared Athens
in recognition of its role in the Persian Wars, though Osborne judges it more likely that
Sparta saw strategic value in preserving Athens as a buffer state.

## Historiography

Thucydides is the indispensable and inescapable source for 431–411 (now ingested as
[[thucydides-peloponnesian-war|*The Peloponnesian War*]]); **Xenophon** and **Diodorus** (from
Ephorus) carry the story to 404. The war's interpretation is therefore largely a debate with
Thucydides — over the reality of his "truest cause," the wisdom of Periclean strategy, the artistry
of his selection (the Melian Dialogue, the Sicilian books), and whether his pro-Athenian, class
viewpoint distorts the picture of allied loyalty (see [[classical-greek-historiography]]).

### CHGRW contribution

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] reframes scale and
method. Hall (I.4): few Athenian conflicts before the late sixth century; even across the
twenty-seven-year war only **five major infantry battles** (contra older “two of every three
years” estimates). Across 27 years Thucydides+Xenophon report **47 land battles** vs **101
poliorcetic incidents** plus numerous ravaging-only invasions — pitched battles relatively rare.
Opening spring 431 hinged on contested sacred oaths over Theban siege of Plataea. Thucydidean
shift from honour+fear+interest (1.75.3) as imperial motives to Melian Dialogue (416) where
honour yields to self-interest. Wheeler (I.6): the war’s “military revolution” was
**incremental**, more strategic than tactical; seeds of Philip II’s all-arms army sown then;
first detailed contemporary Greek-vs-Greek battle account is Delium (424); first Thucydidean
pre-battle etiquette sequence is Syracuse 415. Siege hothouse (Plataea, Potidaea, Melos,
Mytilene, Syracuse, Athens 405–404); *epiteichismos* at Pylos and Decelea; more massacres of
conquered adult males than earlier or pre-Macedonian fourth century (Plataea, Scione, Melos,
Mycalessus 413, Mytilene, Potidaea). Gabrielsen (I.8): fiscal crisis hinge — Potidaea 2,000
talents, sacred borrowing ~6,000 talents 433–423, Mytilene forcing non-regular revenues.

**Kagan (2003)** supplies the leading modern strategic narrative: stresses individual decisions, Pericles' political mastery and the vacuum after 429, precise calculations of resources/war aims (Sparta "freedom for the Greeks" vs. reality of fear), contingency of plague + leadership change opening path to Cleon-style "new politicians," and why Periclean attrition was not abandoned immediately. Complements Thucydides (primary) and Hanson (operational). (Range 2 claims focus on Archidamian phases 431-428/7.)

**Osborne (2000)** (Ch. 7, Kallet) underlines Thucydides's own historiographical
self-presentation: his history was to be "a possession for all time," useful to those
examining "the clear truth of past events and of similar events that will occur in the
future — human nature being what it is" (1.22.4) — a fundamental pessimism about human
nature that recurs in his judgement on *stasis* (civil strife): "many terrible things
befell the cities in stasis, which happen and will always happen, as long as human nature
is what it is" (3.82.2). Osborne contrasts this with Herodotus's explanatory framework:
where Herodotus reads the rise and fall of power through divine envy provoked by hybris,
Thucydides explains it in purely human terms — power grows through the successful
combination of wealth, leadership, intelligence and resources, and falls through the
failure to combine them. Xenophon's *Hellenica* continues Thucydides's narrative from 411
to 362; a rival, independently surviving account, the fragmentary *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*,
sometimes contradicts Xenophon on the same events.

### OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Hornblower (Ch.6)

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]] (Simon Hornblower, Ch.6) compresses the path into and through the Great War:

**Path from 446.** After the First Peloponnesian settlement, Athens expanded north (Amphipolis 437), east (Samos crushed 440/39), and west (timber alliances); renewed Megarian aggression reawakened Corinthian suspicions, producing the Second/Great Peloponnesian War of 431–404, which Athens lost.

**Grand strategies.** Sparta’s early plan was annual Attica invasions to force submission; [[pericles|Pericles]] abandoned Attica, concentrated population within Athens–Piraeus Long Walls, and relied on imperial supplies/finance so that to “survive” was to “win” (Thucydides uses the same Greek word for both). Sparta lacked reserves/tribute and needed Persian money for a fleet, but liberating Asia Minor Greeks from Persia conflicted with League ideology until Athens’s support for Amorges gave Persia a motive.

**Archidamian War to Peace of Nicias.** Sparta founded Heraclea-in-Trachis (426); [[brasidas|Brasidas]] took Amphipolis (424); [[cleon|Cleon]]’s capture of over a hundred Spartiates at Pylos cancelled Brasidas’s gains politically; the Peace of [[nicias|Nicias]] (421–415) left Athens with her empire — she had “won” the Archidamian War.

**Sicily and final defeat.** Athenian support for rebel satraps Pissouthnes and Amorges gave the King motive to help Sparta; the Sicilian expedition (415) annihilated at Syracuse (413) shook the empire; despite 411 oligarchic revolution and naval recovery (Cyzicus 410), Persian money via Cyrus after 407 enabled Lysander’s victory at Aegospotami (405). Hornblower stresses that the Hellespont blockade, not the battle alone, was decisive — empire dissolved.

**Military change.** The war increased professionalism (politician and general as separate callings; mercenaries by late fifth century); peltasts appear alongside hoplites; siege catapults develop c.400 (non-torsion) and c.350 (torsion).

## Freeman (2014) — the final two years

[[sources/freeman-egypt-greece-rome-2014|Freeman (2014)]] adds two events to the closing
sequence between Aegospotami and surrender: the **Arginusae generals' trial (406)**, in
which the victorious generals were tried and executed en masse for failing to recover the
shipwrecked dead after a storm; and, following the surrender of 404, the physical
demolition of the **Long Walls** and the imposition of the **Government of the Thirty**.
