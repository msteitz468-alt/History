## Narrative

The Bougainville civil war had its immediate origins in the Panguna copper mine, operated by Bougainville Copper Ltd (a subsidiary of Rio Tinto). "The government of Papua New Guinea did not fully resolve the question of Bougainville's autonomy, nor the distribution of costs and benefits of large-scale mining. Tensions therefore accumulated until full-blown civil war erupted in 1989, when rebels closed the mine."

The escalation sequence described in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] ran as follows. Landowners blew up electricity pylons and blocked the road linking the port to the mine as a last attempt to raise grievances. The government sent "a police riot squad, who, instead of controlling the conflict, behaved appallingly, burning houses, raping women, and shooting civilians and animals at will." Young men then armed themselves to defend their families and land; the government reinforced the riot squad with soldiers. By this point the objective had shifted to Bougainville independence. The fight "was not new: long before 1962 (when copper was discovered in Panguna) the Bougainville people had demanded their independence" — indeed Bougainvilleans had declared separate independence two weeks before Papua New Guinea achieved independence from Australia.

A year after civil war erupted (1990) the national government withdrew the Papua New Guinea Defence Force (PNGDF) and "imposed a blockade that prevented food and medical supplies from reaching Bougainville." The rebels declared a "Republic of Bougainville" in May 1990 and formed the Bougainville Interim Government, but real power lay with the Bougainville Revolutionary Army (BRA) and its self-styled "Supreme Commander" Francis Ona.

PNG forces retook Buka Island north of Bougainville in September 1990 and armed a militia, the Buka Liberation Front. More than 12,000 residents were relocated into refugee camps. By early 1992 the PNGDF had re-established control over northern Bougainville; in the 1992 national elections only 240 of 30,000 possible voters in South Bougainville cast ballots.

Abortive peace talks at Arawa (October 1994) led to the Mirigini Accord and creation of the Bougainville Transitional Government under Theodore Miriung — formerly a prominent lawyer who had sided with rebels in 1990 but broke from the BRA when they refused the Arawa talks. A first round of exploratory peace talks was held in Cairns, Australia, in August 1995.

## Causal Analysis

**Direct causes:**
- caused_by: Landowner grievances over "inadequate royalties, destruction of their environment, and related social problems" from the Panguna mine
- caused_by: Disproportionate and abusive police response to initial protests

**Contributing factors:**
- contributed_to: Absence of effective civilian control over the PNGDF
- contributed_to: Pre-existing independence sentiment "long before 1962"
- contributed_to: "Acute internal divisions — generational and ethnic — among Bougainvilleans"

**Structural context:**
- preceded_by: Panguna mine conflicts and unresolved autonomy questions at independence
- contributed_to: The source notes the civil war "has been called an example of ethnic 'nationalism', but clearly this term inadequately describes the roots and dimensions of the conflict. The rebellion has divided Bougainville, setting language groups, regions, and generations against one another."

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: Closure of the Panguna mine (one of the world's largest copper mines)
- produced: Humanitarian catastrophe — blockade, displacement, atrocities by both sides
- produced: Republic of Bougainville declaration (May 1990)
- produced: Factionalisation of the rebel side — BRA vs. civilian Bougainville Interim Government, BRA vs. non-BRA communities
- enabled: Long-running peace process; Bougainville Peace Agreement eventually signed 2001 (beyond this source's coverage)
- produced: Questions about civilian control of PNG military — Australia criticised for "allowing the PNGDF to outfit helicopters as 'gunships' (in technical terms, firing platforms), in violation of an agreement between the two governments"

## Actors

- Francis Ona — self-styled Supreme Commander and President of BRA, "Father of the Nation"
- Bougainville Revolutionary Army (BRA) — "a guerrilla force composed largely of young men, minimally controlled by the provisional government or by its own command structures"
- PNG Defence Force (PNGDF) — deployed by Port Moresby; accused of human rights abuses
- Bougainville Interim Government — civilian rebel political body allied with BRA
- Theodore Miriung — lawyer; led Bougainville Transitional Government from 1994
- Buka Liberation Front — militia armed by PNG against the BRA
- Agnes Titus — president of North Solomons Women's Council, called for peace at Arawa 1994
- Sister Elizabeth Ropoke — one of five nuns held prisoner by BRA, testimony on civilian suffering cited verbatim in the source

## Women and the Civil War

The source devotes extended attention to women's experience and agency. "Women have been the backbone: maintaining some degree of peace and order, sustaining the cohesiveness of families, gathering food, and providing security for their children. Yet they have become victims, together with children and the elderly, of a guerrilla war which was not of their making."

A culturally important dimension: in Bougainville's matrilineal systems, "a woman mothers the land." In the Teop language: "a moon to sinana ni na bono kasuana" ('a woman is the mother of the land'). In Nagovisi "the authority figure of a lineage is a senior female, called 'tumeli'" who arbitrates land rights. The source argues that women as landowners had a legitimate claim to inclusion in decisions about the mine — "Francis Ona is not going to make a lone decision on reopening the mine, nor are the male relatives. Women as landowners will have to be consulted."

## Historiography

Updates on Bougainville were published by the Australian National University from 1990 onwards. The source's treatment (by contributors with direct knowledge including an author who visited Bougainville in 1992) is sympathetic to Bougainvillean autonomy claims and critical of both the PNGDF's conduct and Australia's complicity. The label "ethnic nationalism" for the conflict is explicitly questioned — the rebellion is treated as a multi-causal insurgency combining resource grievances, pre-existing independence sentiment, and generational conflict, rather than a straightforward ethnic nationalist movement.
