# The Fall of Acre (1291)

The **fall of Acre** to the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]]** sultan **al-Ashraf Khalil** in
1291 ended the Latin Christian presence on the Syrian mainland, almost two centuries after the
First Crusade. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Peter Edbury, Ch. 16c) treats it as the
culmination of the Mamluks' methodical reduction of the crusader states.

## Narrative

After the [[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|defeat of the Mongols]] (1260), the Mamluks turned the
**jihad** on the Christians. **Baybars** took Caesarea, Arsuf, Safed, **Antioch (1268)** and
Crac des Chevaliers (1271); **Qalawun** took **Tripoli (1289)** and began preparing "an even
grander siege operation against Acre," dying in November 1290. His son **al-Ashraf Khalil**
"reaped the benefits of his father's preparations," and **Acre fell in June 1291**. In its
wake **Tyre, Beirut and Sidon** were occupied without resistance.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **methodical Mamluk conquest** of the Latin east under Baybars, Qalawun and
  Khalil, exploiting truces as "pretexts for resuming the war."
- `contributed_to:` the **structural weakness** of the crusader states — chronic shortage of
  manpower, lay lordships unable to fund defence (Arsuf and Sidon ceded to the military
  Orders), and the absence of sustained western help despite the rhetoric of crusade.

## Consequence Analysis

The fall of Acre ended the **kingdom of Jerusalem** as a territorial state. The **military
Orders** relocated — the Templars and Hospitallers to **Cyprus** (the Hospitallers later to
Rhodes), the **Teutonic Knights** turning to the Baltic. Western commerce shifted to **Cyprus**
(Famagusta) and **Cilician Armenia**. The loss reverberated through Europe: it discredited the
existing crusading model, fed plans for new crusades that never sailed, and provided the
pretext for [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV's]] destruction of the **Templars** (1307), an order
"no longer defending the Holy Land." Crusading energy was increasingly redirected — to the
Mediterranean, Iberia, and the Baltic.

## Historiography

Edbury sets the loss against the surprising **longevity** of the truncated states: after the
Third Crusade the Muslims made no systematic attempt at reconquest until the 1260s, largely
because of **Ayyubid disunity**, and truces covered Acre for roughly eighty of the hundred
years 1192–1291. It was the rise of the centralised **Mamluk** state — not Christian weakness
alone — that finally made the conquest possible.
