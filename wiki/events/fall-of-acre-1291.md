# The Fall of Acre (1291)

The **fall of Acre** to the **[[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]]** sultan **al-Ashraf Khalil** in
1291 ended the Latin Christian presence on the Syrian mainland, almost two centuries after the
First Crusade. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Peter Edbury, Ch. 16c) treats it as the
culmination of the Mamluks' methodical reduction of the crusader states.

## Narrative

After the [[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|defeat of the Mongols]] (1260), the Mamluks turned the
**jihad** on the Christians. **Baybars** took Caesarea, Arsuf, Safed, **Antioch (1268)** and
Crac des Chevaliers (1271); **Qalawun** took **Tripoli (1289)** and began preparing "an even
grander siege operation against Acre," dying on the road from Cairo. His son **al-Ashraf Khalil**
renewed the campaign, and **Acre fell on 18 May 1291** (NCMH V’s “June 1291” is a coarser
dating). In its wake **Tyre, Beirut, Sidon, and Haifa** surrendered with little or no
resistance.

### Qalawun and al-Ashraf Khalil ([[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb 2014]])

**Qalawun** (Qalavun; “al-Alfi”/Thousand-Coin) — old Bahri colleague of Baybars, a Qipchaq Turk —
supplanted Baybars’s sons after 1279 and continued Baybars’s plan to drive Franks from Syria. By
his day only **Tripoli** remained of the original Frankish capitals (Jerusalem’s capital had
moved to Acre in Saladin’s day). After Tripoli fell (**26 April 1289** — men in the streets
killed, others sold; city demolished), only Acre was left of the four original Frankish states.
Qalawun arranged a temporary truce while building an army. In **August 1290** a riot in which
Franks killed Muslim townsmen (possibly merchants) gave the pretext that the truce was broken; he
called *jihad* and died of sickness a few miles from Cairo on the march.

In **March 1291** his son and heir **al-Ashraf Khalil** renewed the campaign; Qalawun’s funeral
served as a *jihad* pulpit; volunteers were said to outnumber regulars; siege gear was hauled
through rain and snow. The army reached Acre **6 April 1291**. Garrison supplemented by
Hospitallers and Templars; ships with mangonels; daily knight sorties; a Cyprus contingent under
King Hugh did not linger. Tower collapse opened inner defenses. Mid-May notables’ truce rejected;
a safe-conduct-for-abandon offer aborted when a mangonel nearly killed the sultan. At dawn
**18 May**, al-Ashraf ordered **~300 drums** to signal the final offensive; banners were on the
walls by midday; volunteers pillaged; the Templar tower, twice broken, surrendered in the
bloodiest last hours and was then sapped. News reached Damascus the same day by pigeon; a Gothic
church façade was later taken to Cairo for the madrasa/tomb of al-Nasir Muhammad. Casualties
estimated in the **tens of thousands** (hedge). The chronicler al-Yunini (d. 1326) framed Acre as
requital for **Richard’s betrayal of Muslims at Acre under Saladin**.

After Acre: Sidon, Beirut, Tyre, and Haifa soon surrendered (Tyre said to hold only a few dozen
old men and women); last Templar forts Tortosa/Antartus and ʿAtlith easily taken. Al-Ashraf
triumphed in Damascus (280 Frankish prisoners; inverted banner; hair of the slain) then in Cairo
at Qalawun’s tomb. A poet: “the nation of the Cross has fallen; through the Turks the religion of
the chosen Arab has triumphed!”

### Cypriot dimension ([[edbury-kingdom-cyprus-crusades-1991|Edbury 1991]])

**[[henry-ii-of-cyprus|Henry II]]** had recovered Acre from the Angevins in 1286 and brought
Cypriot reinforcements (~200 knights as an upper spareable limit) for the final defence;
personal conduct during the siege was creditable, but Cypriot numbers could not tilt the
balance. Aftermath: Syrian refugees flooded Cyprus; the military Orders relocated
headquarters; Cyprus became the **sole major western outpost** in the eastern Mediterranean
(with [[cilician-armenian-kingdom|Cilician Armenia]] the only other Christian state nearby).
Henry's post-1291 policy — embargo patrols, abortive Mongol coordination (Ghazan 1299–1301),
Ruad (1302) — defined the first generation of that solitary role. See [[kingdom-of-cyprus]].

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **methodical Mamluk conquest** of the Latin east under Baybars, Qalawun and
  Khalil, exploiting truces as "pretexts for resuming the war."
- `contributed_to:` the **structural weakness** of the crusader states — chronic shortage of
  manpower, lay lordships unable to fund defence (Arsuf and Sidon ceded to the military
  Orders), and the absence of sustained western help despite the rhetoric of crusade.

## Consequence Analysis

The fall of Acre ended the **kingdom of Jerusalem** as a territorial state. The **military
Orders** relocated — the Templars and Hospitallers to **Cyprus** (the Hospitallers later to
Rhodes), the **Teutonic Knights** turning to the Baltic. Western commerce shifted to **Cyprus**
(Famagusta) and **Cilician Armenia**. The loss reverberated through Europe: it discredited the
existing crusading model, fed plans for new crusades that never sailed, and provided the
pretext for [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV's]] destruction of the **Templars** (1307), an order
"no longer defending the Holy Land." Crusading energy was increasingly redirected — to the
Mediterranean, Iberia, and the Baltic.

## Historiography

Edbury sets the loss against the surprising **longevity** of the truncated states: after the
Third Crusade the Muslims made no systematic attempt at reconquest until the 1260s, largely
because of **Ayyubid disunity**, and truces covered Acre for roughly eighty of the hundred
years 1192–1291. It was the rise of the centralised **Mamluk** state — not Christian weakness
alone — that finally made the conquest possible.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] supplies the Qalawun → al-Ashraf Khalil endgame above and an
important periodization thesis: **1291 traditionally ends the main Crusades story for Western
observers**, but Muslim chroniclers do **not** treat 1291 as an ending. Frankish threat
continued (Jubayl near Tripoli; Templars on **Arwad** off Tartus until **1302**; Cyprus
Lusignans); the Near East was only one of many fronts (al-Andalus, eastern Europe). For the
Mamluks the Frankish threat was greatly weakened but relocated **offshore**. Later European
memory linked the fall of Acre to other Muslim losses (e.g. Charles II’s liquidation of Lucera
in 1300, framed as Christian triumph while Acre was still fresh).
