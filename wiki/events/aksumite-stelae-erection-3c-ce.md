# Aksumite Great Stelae Erection Phase

## Narrative

The great stelae erection phase at [[aksum-city]] represents the most ambitious monumental construction project in the ancient history of sub-Saharan Africa, and by some measures — in terms of single-piece stone mass — of any ancient culture worldwide. Over approximately one century, from the latter part of the third century CE to the early fourth century, the ruling elite of the [[aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]] quarried, transported, carved, and erected a series of monolithic granite obelisks of unprecedented scale, each marking an elaborate underground royal tomb.

The immediate setting was the Stelae Park at Aksum — a location that had served as an elite funerary area from at least the first century CE, where burial platforms with associated stelae (grave-markers) had gradually accumulated. The transformation that occurred in the late third century was not a new practice but a dramatic escalation of an existing one. Whereas earlier stelae were modest by comparison, the new monuments were carved to represent the facades of multi-storey buildings in the distinctive Aksumite architectural vocabulary (monkey-heads, recessed windows, false doors), and reached heights and weights without parallel in the ancient world.

**Stela 1** — the largest, now fallen and broken where it lies — was carved to represent thirteen storeys and is estimated to have weighed over 520 tonnes. **Stela 2** (now re-erected after its return from Rome in 2008) stood 33 m tall and weighed approximately 160 tonnes; it is carved with nine storeys. **Stela 3** (still standing, 24 m) has seven storeys. Three further carved "storeyed" stelae are known; six in total form the core of the great monument group.

The sequence of erection of these six can be approximately reconstructed from their relative positions, associated tomb types, and the coins found in the sealed fill of the tombs beneath them. Attribution of specific stelae to named kings relies entirely on numismatic inference (no inscription names the buried individual). On these grounds, Stela 2 is attributed to Ousanas/Ella Amida or to wzb, placing it in the early fourth century. Stela 1, the largest, is tentatively placed in the early fourth century as well — its fall approximately coinciding with Ezana's conversion to Christianity.

The tombs beneath the great stelae were themselves remarkable engineering works: multi-chambered stone-lined underground complexes reached by shafts or stairways, using fired-brick vaulting and iron clamps (of Graeco-Roman type) to join stone blocks. The Mausoleum (below Stela 2) and the Tomb of the Brick Arches (below Stela 3) are the best documented. All had been robbed in antiquity; the Tomb of the Brick Arches nonetheless yielded ivory and metalwork, indicating the richness of the original deposits.

The erection of the giant stelae ceased with Ezana's adoption of Christianity, probably in the 330s–340s CE. The fall of Stela 1 is archaeologically associated with this period. The pre-Christian funerary ideology that the stelae embodied — displaying royal power through vast mobilised labour and associating the king with multi-storey palatial architecture — was incompatible with Christian burial practice. Smaller stelae continued to serve as grave-markers for the population at large, but no monument of comparable scale was ever erected again.

## Causal Analysis

**Direct causes:**
- caused_by: [[aksum]] — the political authority capable of mobilising the labour and resources required
- contributed_to: Increasing social stratification in the Aksumite kingdom during the third century CE, reflected in burial complexity
- contributed_to: Economic prosperity through [[indian-ocean-trade]] and Red Sea commerce, which funded the logistical capacity
- preceded_by: Earlier stelae-and-platform burial tradition at the Stelae Park site (first century CE onward)

**Enabling conditions:**
- enabled_by: Local availability of granite suitable for monolithic quarrying c. 4 km from the Stelae Park
- enabled_by: Existing Aksumite architectural vocabulary that the stelae facades could represent

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced**: The six great carved stelae, the most massive ancient monoliths known
- **produced**: Elaborated underground royal tomb tradition (Mausoleum, Tomb of the Brick Arches, Tomb of the False Door)
- **enabled**: [[aksumite-stelae]] as a concept and monument-type that shaped subsequent understanding of Aksumite royal ideology
- **followed_by**: Cessation of the tradition under Ezana and the shift to Christian burial practice
- **followed_by**: The stelae as enduring physical presences — the standing and fallen obelisks remain the most visible monuments of Aksumite civilisation

## Actors

- **[[aksum|Kingdom of Aksum]]** (primary): The state as collective agent; the specific kings who ordered the monuments cannot be identified by name from the stelae themselves
- **[[ezana-of-aksum]]** (secondary): His conversion to Christianity marked the end of the great stelae tradition; the fall of Stela 1 is approximately contemporary with his reign

## Historiography

The stelae have attracted scholarly attention since the earliest European descriptions of Aksum. The foundational modern documentation is the **Deutsche Aksum-Expedition** (1906; published Littmann et al. 1913), which produced the first systematic record of the stelae's dimensions and carved detail. Phillipson's own detailed survey (D. Phillipson 1997) and the assessment in *Foundations of an African Civilisation* (2012) are the current standard. Archaeological excavation of the associated tombs (D. Phillipson 2000b) established the chronological and material context. Key unresolved questions include the precise attribution of individual stelae to named kings, the exact sequence of erection, and the mechanics of the transport and raising of the largest monoliths. Phillipson (2012) provides a reconstruction of probable erection technique involving timber sledges and levers; the logistical details remain incompletely understood.
