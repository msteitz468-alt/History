# Arab Conquest of Egypt (639–642 CE)

Between **639 and 642 CE** an Arab army under **Amr ibn al-As** conquered Egypt for the
[[rashidun-caliphate|Rashidun Caliphate]], ending nearly a thousand years of Greco-Roman rule
(from Alexander in 332 BCE) and beginning the long transformation of Egypt into an Arabic-speaking,
predominantly Muslim land. It is the terminal event of the "Egypt from Alexander to the Copts"
period.

## Narrative

In **AD 639, seven years after the death of Muhammad**, Egypt was invaded by the Arabs under
**Amr ibn al-As**, fresh from the conquest of Roman Syria (see [[battle-of-yarmuk-636|Yarmuk]]).
The decisive struggle centred on the great Roman fortress of **Babylon** (at the apex of the Delta,
in what is now Old Cairo), which fell after a siege. "Two years later, the country was in Arab
hands, and in AD 642 the Byzantines abandoned [[alexandria|Alexandria]] to the victorious Arabs."

The conquest came on the heels of acute Byzantine weakness: Egypt had been **occupied by the
Sasanian Persians under Chosroes II's general Shahrbaraz from 619 until their negotiated withdrawal
in 629**, and the empire as a whole was exhausted by the long Roman–Persian war that
[[heraclius|Heraclius]] had only just won. The new Arab government established its headquarters
just north of the fortress of Babylon at a place that came to be called **Fustat** (the nucleus of
later Cairo).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the explosive [[rashidun-caliphate|early Islamic expansion]] out of Arabia that had
  already taken Syria.
- `contributed_to:` **Byzantine exhaustion** after the Sasanian war and the disruptive Persian
  occupation of 619–629, which left Egypt's defences and administration weakened.
- `contributed_to:` (cautiously) Egypt's **religious estrangement** — the Miaphysite
  [[coptic-church|Coptic majority]] alienated from the Chalcedonian imperial church after
  [[council-of-chalcedon-451|Chalcedon]] — though scholars warn against reducing the conquest to
  Coptic disaffection.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the end of Greco-Roman Egypt and the start of **Arab rule**; the founding of
  **Fustat** as the new capital.
- `produced:` a **gradual Arabisation and Islamisation** — Greek remained an administrative language
  for roughly a century; Arabic was made official for state affairs in **705**; the 720s saw Coptic
  **tax revolts**; conversion proceeded "as evolutions rather than revolutions" over centuries and
  was "never complete."
- `enabled:` the survival of the [[coptic-church|Coptic Church]] as the institution of a permanent
  Christian minority.

## Historiography

The narrative sources for the conquest — Arabic (e.g. al-Baladhuri) and Coptic (the *Chronicle of
John of Nikiou*) — are later and tendentious, and the documentary papyri illuminate the *aftermath*
(the correspondence of the governor Qurra ibn Sharik with the official Flavius Basilius at
Aphrodito) far better than the campaign itself. Bagnall and Rathbone treat early Islamic Egypt as a
"neglected period" lying just beyond the book's scope, noting that the documentary record
counterbalances the hostile literary portrayal of Arab governors. The role of Coptic disaffection
in the rapid Byzantine collapse remains debated (Kaegi; Sijpesteijn).
