# Invasion of Iraq (2003)

The United States, with a "coalition of the willing" led principally by Britain, invaded
Iraq on 20 March 2003, citing intelligence claims (later shown to be mistaken) that
Saddam Hussein's government possessed weapons of mass destruction, alleged links to
al-Qaeda, and the broader goal of regime change and democratization. President
[[bush-george-w|George W. Bush]], along with Vice President [[cheney-richard-b|Dick
Cheney]] and other senior officials, pressed the case for war despite the absence of UN
Security Council authorization and considerable international opposition.

## Course and Consequences

Conventional Iraqi resistance collapsed within weeks; Bush declared "Mission Accomplished"
from the USS Abraham Lincoln on 1 May 2003, and simultaneously shifted the war's stated
goal toward bringing democracy to Iraq. The subsequent decision by the US-led Coalition
Provisional Authority to disband the Iraqi army and pursue sweeping de-Baathification
removed the state's security apparatus and administrative capacity, producing a security
vacuum, insurgency, and sectarian civil conflict that persisted for years. No weapons of
mass destruction were found, discrediting the war's original justification.

## Historiography

Retrospective assessments — including by many former officials and prominent historians —
have often ranked the decision among the most consequential and heavily criticized US
foreign-policy decisions of the postwar era, citing the flawed intelligence, absence of
adequate postwar planning, and the war's long-term costs in lives, money, and regional
stability. Defenders argue Saddam Hussein's removal ended a brutal dictatorship and that
subsequent failures owed more to occupation and reconstruction choices than to the
decision to invade itself; the balance of professional historical opinion has trended
critical of the war's justification and execution.
