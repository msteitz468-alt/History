# Seikanron — The Korea Debate and Government Split of 1873

The *seikanron* (征韓論, "debate on the subjugation of Korea") was the defining political crisis of the early Meiji period. In 1873 the Grand Council of State (Daijō-kan) fractured over whether Japan should dispatch an armed mission to Korea to compel the Chosŏn dynasty to accept diplomatic relations on Meiji terms — or whether the young state should first consolidate itself internally before seeking overseas expansion.

## Narrative

Korea had refused to recognize diplomatic correspondence from the new Meiji government because the letters used titles implying that the Japanese emperor was the equal of — or superior to — the Chinese emperor, which violated the Sinocentric diplomatic hierarchy to which Chosŏn adhered. This refusal was experienced by Japanese officials as a national humiliation. **[[saigo-takamori|Saigō Takamori]]** proposed a dramatic solution: he would go to Korea himself as an envoy, deliberately provoke the Koreans into killing him, and thus provide a *casus belli* for a military expedition. The proposal had wide support among officials who were also concerned that the dissolution of the han (domains) and the commutation of samurai stipends were dangerously destabilizing the warrior class, and that a foreign war would redirect that energy outward.

The opposing faction, led by [[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]], **Kido Takayoshi**, and **[[iwakura-tomomi|Iwakura Tomomi]]** — men who had just returned from the extended Iwakura Mission to Europe and America (1871–73) — argued that Japan was too weak militarily and economically to risk war with Korea, which might draw in China or Western powers. They had seen firsthand the industrial and military gap between Japan and the advanced nations and believed the priority must be internal development. Iwakura, as minister of the right, secured the emperor's approval for the anti-invasion position.

The cabinet decision went against Saigō. He and his allies — **[[itagaki-taisuke|Itagaki Taisuke]]**, Etō Shimpei, Gotō Shōjirō, and Soejima Taneomi — resigned en masse from the government in what became known as the *Meiji-roku-nen seihen* (政変 — the political upheaval of 1873). Keene's text refers repeatedly to "the crisis of 1873" and "the political upheaval of 1873" as the originating shock that sent reverberations through the rest of the decade: "ever since the crisis of 1873, most of the troubles plaguing the country had originated in Satsuma" (Kido Takayoshi's December 1876 memorandum, as cited by [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

## Causal Analysis

The seikanron was not simply a debate about Korea. It was a conflict over the direction of the Meiji state, fought between:

- **The externalist-expansionist faction** (Saigō, Itagaki, Etō): believed samurai martial values and overseas expansion were essential, distrusted rapid Westernization, and saw Korea as the immediate outlet
- **The internalist-developmentalist faction** (Ōkubo, Kido, Iwakura): prioritized industry, constitutional reform, and avoiding foreign entanglements until Japan was strong enough to resist Western pressure

The Iwakura Mission's exposure to Western industrial power was decisive in forming the latter faction's position. Ōkubo in particular returned convinced that "ever since he returned from America and Europe in 1873, Ōkubo's aim had been to strengthen the country politically and economically in order to compete on terms of equality with the advanced countries of the West" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

Both sides invoked the emperor's authority. The conflict was thus also about who controlled access to the throne — a structural question the seikanron did not resolve but merely deferred.

## Consequence Analysis

The consequences of the seikanron ramified through the rest of the 1870s:

1. **Saigō in Kagoshima**: Returning to his home prefecture, Saigō founded private military schools (*shigakkō*) in June 1874. These schools, which emphasized samurai values and the Chinese military classics over Western learning, became the institutional base for the [[satsuma-rebellion-1877]].

2. **Etō Shimpei and the [[saga-rebellion-1874]]**: Etō channeled the frustration of the invasion faction into immediate armed revolt, leading the Saga uprising less than a year after the seikanron.

3. **Itagaki and the popular-rights movement**: Rather than armed revolt, Itagaki turned toward political agitation. He helped found the *Aikokusha* (Patriotic Society) and later the Jiyūtō (Liberal Party), becoming the central figure of the *jiyū minken undō* (Freedom and Popular Rights Movement), which persistently challenged the oligarchy through the late 1870s and 1880s.

4. **Ōkubo's dominance**: With Saigō and Itagaki gone, Ōkubo Toshimichi became the single most powerful figure in the Meiji government. His tenure as interior minister lasted until his assassination in 1878, during which he shaped economic and administrative policy with near-autocratic authority.

5. **The Korea question deferred, not resolved**: Japan did eventually establish relations with Korea through the coercive **[[kanghwa-treaty-1876|Treaty of Kanghwa (1876)]]**, achieved not through an envoy's martyrdom but through gunboat diplomacy following the Unyō incident of 1875. The seikanron advocates had been wrong about the method but not about the direction of Japanese policy.

## Actors

- **[[saigo-takamori|Saigō Takamori]]** (1827–1877): Proposed the envoy mission; resigned; founded private schools; led the Satsuma Rebellion; died at Shiroyama.
- **[[itagaki-taisuke|Itagaki Taisuke]]** (1837–1919): Resigned; became leader of the popular-rights movement.
- **Etō Shimpei** (1834–1874): Resigned; led the Saga Rebellion; executed.
- **[[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]]** (1830–1878): Led the anti-invasion bloc; consolidated power; assassinated by samurai dissidents in 1878.
- **Iwakura Tomomi** (1825–1883): Minister of the right; secured the emperor's support against invasion; key stabilizing figure of the oligarchy.
- **[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]**: Formally ratified Ōkubo and Iwakura's position; his authority was invoked by both sides.

## Historiography

The seikanron has been interpreted variously as:
- A conflict between **realists and romantics** — pragmatists who understood Japan's weakness versus men who underestimated the risks of war
- A conflict between **Westernizing modernizers and samurai traditionalists**, with the seikanron as the moment the modernizers won
- A **power struggle** disguised as a policy debate, in which Ōkubo and Iwakura used the Korea question to marginalize political rivals
- A **foundational moment of Japanese imperialism**: the annexationist impulse was not defeated but only redirected; Japan's eventual conquest of Korea (1910) vindicated the expansionists' diagnosis if not their timing

Keene's account of the mid-1870s emphasizes the second and third interpretations, showing Ōkubo using his post-seikanron dominance to pursue industrial development while managing continuous samurai discontent — discontent that the seikanron itself had helped to radicalize. The debate echoes through Kido's late memorials, Saigō's private schools, and the series of armed revolts (Shinpūren, Akizuki, Hagi, Saga, and ultimately Satsuma) that marked the decade.

**Collection coverage**: [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] treats the seikanron as background — the text's explicit focus is on 1875–79 — but the crisis is referenced numerous times as the originating cause of later unrest. Deep treatment of the seikanron itself is found in works on the [[meiji-oligarchs]] and on Saigō's life.
