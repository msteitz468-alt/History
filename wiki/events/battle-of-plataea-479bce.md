# Plataea and Mycale (479 BCE)

In **479 BCE** the Greeks destroyed the Persian land army at **Plataea** in Boeotia and, on
the same reckoning of days, burned the Persian fleet and army at **Mycale** in Ionia —
the twin victories that ended [[xerxes-i|Xerxes']] invasion and began the **liberation of the
eastern Greeks**. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 11, J. P. Barron) rates Plataea
as, "more than Marathon or even Salamis," the battle that "determined the course of European
history."

## The campaign of Plataea

After Salamis, Xerxes withdrew, leaving **Mardonius** with a picked army (perhaps ~120,000
under arms, plus medizing Greeks). Through the Macedonian king **Alexander I**, Mardonius
offered Athens generous terms to defect — refused, the councillor **Lycidas** lynched for
even proposing debate. The Athenian threat to medize finally forced Sparta to march: the
regent **Pausanias** (nephew of Leonidas) led ~5,000 Spartiates with 35,000 Helots, joined by
**Aristides'** Athenians, into Boeotia. Across the Asopus the two armies, each on terrain
hostile to the other's strength (Persian **cavalry** vs. Greek **phalanx**), held off for
days of skirmish, omen-reading, and harassment — the cavalry fouling the Greeks' spring at
**Gargaphia** and cutting their supply trains.

## The battle of Plataea

A difficult **night retreat** to a better-watered position scattered the Greek line by dawn.
Mardonius, believing it a rout, threw his whole force into a pursuit — the **fatal mistake**.
The Spartans and Tegeans, after Pausanias waited (his seer Tisamenus) until the omens turned,
**charged and overran the Persian wicker-shield barricade**; in hand-to-hand fighting the
bronze-clad hoplites destroyed Mardonius' infantry. When **Mardonius was killed** (by the
Spartan Aeimnestus), the army broke and fled to its stockade, which the Athenians stormed.
Nine-tenths were said to have died; the prudent **Artabazus** alone withdrew his corps intact
toward Asia. Pausanias' **restraint in victory** (refusing to mutilate Mardonius' corpse) and
the vast booty became famous; he then took **Thebes** and executed the chief medizers.

## Mycale and the liberation of Ionia

Simultaneously, the Greek fleet under the Spartan king **Leotychidas**, joined by Athens'
squadron and urged on by Samian patriots, crossed to **Mt Mycale**, where the Persians had
beached their fleet and joined Tigranes' land force behind a stockade. A Greek amphibious
assault — aided by a **rumour of the Plataea victory** sweeping the ranks — stormed the
palisade; the **Ionians changed sides**, the Persians were routed, and the **fleet was
burned**. Mycale "sparked a new Ionian revolt": Leotychidas accepted **Samos, Chios, Lesbos**
into the Greek alliance, and **Xanthippus'** Athenians stayed to besiege and take **Sestus**
on the Hellespont — the first act of the coming **Athenian empire**.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the army of occupation [[xerxes-i|Xerxes]] left under Mardonius after
[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]; the Greek resolve (forced by the Athenian fleet-threat)
to liberate central Greece; the Ionian appeal at Mycale.
contributed_to: Mardonius' over-confidence in pursuit; the **structural mismatch** of cavalry
vs. phalanx that each side tried to exploit.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **annihilation of the Persian field army** and the end of the invasion of the
Greek mainland; the **burning of the Persian fleet** and the **liberation of the islands and
Ionia**.
enabled: the **Delian League** and Athenian hegemony — at the post-Mycale council on Samos
"the erosion of Sparta's hegemony began"; the commemorations (the **Serpent Column** at
Delphi, the *Eleutheria* festival, the Oath of Plataea) that fixed the wars in Greek memory.

## Historiography

**[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]** (Book IX), based on veteran testimony and personal
inspection, is the spine, with Plutarch's *Aristides*, the disputed **Oath of Plataea**, and
the **Serpent Column** inscription. Barron notes that Herodotus, writing for Athenians when
**Pausanias was disgraced**, makes Plataea "a soldiers' battle" of luck and courage —
possibly obscuring Pausanias' tactical generalship (the lured pursuit and counter-attack).
