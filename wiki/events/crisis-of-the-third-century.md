# The Crisis of the Third Century

## Narrative

A fifty-year period of military, political, and fiscal instability in the
[[roman-empire|Roman Empire]], running from the assassination of **Severus Alexander (19
March 235 CE)** — killed by his own troops, who resented his paying tribute to Germanic
chiefs — to the accession of **Diocletian (284 CE)**. The Senate recognized **26 emperors
in 49 years**. [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]] divides it into three
phases: (1) 235–c.253, traditions broadly maintained; (2) c.253–268, peak crisis under
Valerian and Gallienus; (3) 268–284, recovery under the "barracks emperors." The year
**238 CE** is the "Year of Six Emperors."

At its nadir, after Valerian's death (260 CE), the empire fractured into **three political
entities**: the **Gallic Empire** (Britain, Gaul, Hispania), the **[[palmyra|Palmyrene
Empire]]** (Syria, Palestine, Egypt), and a rump Rome centered on Italy. Recovery began
with the defeat of a Gothic army at **Naissus (268/269)**; the barracks emperors Claudius
II Gothicus and **Aurelian** won further victories, and Aurelian reunited the empire by
the end of **274 CE** — a restoration that lasted roughly a century.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` chronic military indiscipline (rival legions proclaiming "barracks emperors")
and simultaneous frontier pressure from Goths, Vandals, and the new
[[sasanian-empire|Sasanian]] power in the east. `contributed_to:` the
[[plague-of-cyprian-250|Plague of Cyprian]] (250–270), which depleted the army and labour
force.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` temporary tripartite fragmentation; severe disruption of the long-distance
trade that linked Rome to the [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]] and Central Asia (a key
component of the wider [[collapse-of-first-silk-roads-era-250|collapse of the First Silk
Roads Era]]); and, traditionally, a western shift toward large self-sufficient estates
(proto-manorialism) and semi-free *coloni*. The eastern provinces remained strong and
evolved into the [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]]. `followed_by:` Diocletian's
reforms and the Dominate.

## Historiography

[CONTROVERSY] Benjamin endorses a **modern revisionist reading**: few scholars today accept
that the period was an existential threat to the Roman state. Imperial turnover was a
normal response to unrest; several barracks emperors defended the empire effectively; many
regions (Egypt, the African provinces, Hispania) actually prospered; and the supposed
demographic catastrophe is disputed. The dark traditional portrait reflects the
**senatorial-class bias** of the ancient sources (Eutropius, the *Historia Augusta*) and
the western experience, where invasion was worst. See `contrasts_with:` the older
catastrophist account in [[fall-of-rome-causes]].
