# The Opium Wars and the Treaty System (1839–1860)

The wars in which Britain (and later France) forced [[qing-dynasty|Qing China]] open to foreign
trade and imposed the **"unequal treaties"** — the start of China's nineteenth-century
subjugation, remembered as the opening of the **"century of humiliation."** From Jonathan
Spence's [[spence-search-for-modern-china|*The Search for Modern China*]].

## The First Opium War (1839–42)

By the 1830s British merchants (notably Jardine) were balancing their tea trade by selling vast
quantities of Indian **opium** into China, draining its silver and spreading addiction. In 1839
the **Daoguang emperor** appointed the upright Confucian official **Lin Zexu** as imperial
commissioner to suppress the trade; Lin confiscated and destroyed some 20,000 chests at
Guangzhou and expelled the merchants (who decamped to **Hong Kong**). Spence stresses Lin's
**fatal misjudgment**: he assumed the young Queen Victoria would repudiate the "wicked"
merchants, and dismissed reports of British military preparations as "empty bluster." Instead,
pressured by the opium lobby and chambers of commerce, **Palmerston** sent a fleet. The war
exposed the Qing military's "woeful inadequacies" against Western industrial power — above all
the iron steamer ***Nemesis***, which roamed the shallows firing shells and ferrying troops.
The British seized Zhoushan, Shanghai, and the Yangzi, cut the Grand Canal, and threatened
Nanjing, forcing the Qing to sue for peace.

## The "unequal treaties"

The **Treaty of Nanjing (1842)** and its successors established the **treaty-port system**:
the opening of **five ports** (Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, Shanghai), the cession of
**Hong Kong**, a large indemnity, a fixed low tariff (China losing tariff autonomy), and the
abolition of the Cohong monopoly. Follow-on American (1844) and French (1844) treaties added
two corrosive principles: **extraterritoriality** (foreigners tried under their own national
law, not Qing law — "a lightning rod for Chinese nationalism") and toleration for Christian
missionaries. Britain's 1843 supplement added the **most-favored-nation clause** — any
concession to any power automatically extended to Britain — which "prevented the Qing from
playing off one nation against another." Of the five ports, only **Shanghai** boomed.

## The Second Opium War (1856–60)

Frustrated that the treaties had not opened China as hoped, Britain and France launched a
**second war (1856–60)**, culminating in the occupation of Beijing and the **burning of the
Summer Palace (1860)**. The resulting treaties opened more ports, legalized opium, permitted
foreign legations in Beijing and missionaries in the interior, and deepened China's
semi-colonial subjection.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the British **opium trade** and the Qing attempt to suppress it; more broadly the
  clash between the Qing world-order (the Canton system, tribute) and Western demands for free
  trade and legal autonomy.
- `caused_by:` Britain's decisive **naval-industrial superiority** (the steam gunboat).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **treaty-port system, extraterritoriality, and the loss of tariff and legal
  sovereignty** — the framework of foreign penetration for a century.
- `produced:` the cession of **Hong Kong** (British until 1997).
- `contributed_to:` the internal collapse — the war's costs and dislocations fed the
  **[[taiping-rebellion|Taiping]]** and other mid-century rebellions.
- `enabled:` the "**century of humiliation**" narrative central to modern Chinese nationalism
  and the **[[chinese-communist-revolution|Communist revolution's]]** legitimacy.

## Significance

The Opium Wars are the hinge of modern Chinese history: the moment the [[qing-dynasty|Qing]]
empire's claim to centrality collapsed before Western power, beginning the long crisis — through
the [[taiping-rebellion|Taiping]], [[self-strengthening|self-strengthening]], and the
[[fall-of-the-qing-1911|1911 Revolution]] — that Spence frames as China's "search for modern
China."

## Historiography

Spence's account draws on both Chinese official correspondence (Lin Zexu's memorials and reports to the Daoguang emperor) and British parliamentary and mercantile records, giving a genuinely two-sided source base for the First Opium War, though Chinese-language material is comparatively thinner for the more diplomatically complex Second Opium War. Scholarly assessment of the wars has shifted substantially: an older Western historiography treated them within a "trade friction" frame that downplayed the opium trade's centrality, while post-1949 Chinese historiography and most current Western scholarship treat the wars unambiguously as fought to protect a narcotics trade — foundational to the modern Chinese "century of humiliation" narrative, itself now studied as a political-ideological construct in its own right. Debate continues over how much weight to give the Qing's own institutional failures (military technology, administrative rigidity) versus the sheer disproportion of industrial-era British naval power in explaining the swift Qing defeats.
