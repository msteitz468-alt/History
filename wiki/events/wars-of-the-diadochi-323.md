## Narrative

The Wars of the Diadochi ("Successors") are the roughly four decades of conflict
among [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] marshals over the inheritance of his
empire. CAH VII.1 (Édouard Will, chs. 2 & 4) treats the principal phase, 323–301,
ending at the [[battle-of-ipsus-301|battle of Ipsus]]; the coda runs to
[[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]] (281), where the last two surviving Successors,
Seleucus and Lysimachus, fought it out. The wars dissolved the largest empire the world
had yet seen into the system of competing Hellenistic kingdoms.

**The Babylon settlement (323).** Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 leaving no
viable heir — only a half-witted half-brother, Arrhidaeus (made **Philip III**), and
the unborn son of Roxane (**Alexander IV**), proclaimed joint kings. Real power was
parcelled among the marshals: [[perdiccas]] as chiliarch held the kings in Asia;
[[antipater]] governed Macedon and Greece; Craterus was nominal *prostates*. The
satrapies were divided — [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy]] taking Egypt,
[[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus]] western Anatolia, [[eumenes-of-cardia|Eumenes]]
Cappadocia, [[lysimachus]] Thrace, [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus]] (a little later,
at Triparadisus) Babylonia. The [[lamian-war-323|Lamian War]] in Greece (a failed
revolt led by Athens) broke out at once.

**Perdiccas to Triparadisus (323–321).** Perdiccas, championing the empire's unity
(for his own profit), alienated the satraps; his invasion of Egypt failed and he was
murdered by his officers (321). At **Triparadisus** the settlement was reorganised:
Antipater became regent and withdrew the kings to Macedon, while Antigonus was made
*strategos of Asia* and charged with destroying Eumenes — confirming the empire's
drift apart.

**Antigonus' bid for the whole (321–301).** After Antipater's death (319), the
regency was disputed between [[polyperchon]] (his chosen successor) and his son
[[cassander]]. Antigonus crushed Eumenes (316) and made himself master of Asia from
Anatolia to Iran, then drove Seleucus from Babylon — making Antigonus the new
champion of imperial unity and uniting the others against him. Through the
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemaic]] victory at Gaza (312), Seleucus recovered Babylon
(the epoch of the [[seleucid-era]]); a general peace (311) ratified the *status quo*
while Cassander quietly murdered the boy-king **Alexander IV** (c. 310), extinguishing
the Argead line. In **306–305** the rivals one by one assumed the royal title
(basileus) — Antigonus first, claiming Alexander's whole legacy; then Ptolemy,
Lysimachus, Seleucus, and Cassander, each asserting only his own sovereignty. This is
the legal birth of the [[hellenistic-monarchy|Hellenistic monarchies]].

**Ipsus and after (301).** In 301 the coalition of Lysimachus, Seleucus, Cassander,
and Ptolemy destroyed Antigonus at [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] in Phrygia (Seleucus'
Indian war-elephants decisive); the old man fell on the field. The spoils were
divided: Lysimachus took Anatolia to the Taurus, Seleucus took Syria (but Ptolemy
seized and held Coele-Syria, the origin of the [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]]),
[[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius]] survived with his fleet and footholds. The
unitary idea died with Antigonus.

## Causal Analysis

Will's reading (followed throughout the volume) is **structural**: Alexander's
empire `caused_by` nothing but his own person — "not a state but an artificial
aggregate of at least three states" (Macedon, Egypt, Asia) joined only by personal
union. His death therefore *necessarily* `produced` a succession crisis; the absence
of a competent Argead heir removed the one thing that might have preserved unity. The
wars are thus less a contingent struggle of personalities than the working-out of a
built-in instability — though personal ambition (Perdiccas', Antigonus') supplied the
energy and `contributed_to` the specific, bloody course events took.

## Consequence Analysis

- **The three-dynasty system**: [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]],
  [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]], [[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonids]] — the political
  frame of the entire [[hellenistic-world|Hellenistic world]].
- **Spear-won kingship**: legitimacy from victory, formalised in 306–305 — see
  [[hellenistic-monarchy]].
- **Extinction of the Argeads**: Philip III and Olympias (317), Alexander IV (c. 310),
  Heracles (309) — the dynasty of Philip and Alexander annihilated within fifteen
  years.
- **New cities and the [[seleucid-era]]**: Antioch and the Syrian tetrapolis,
  Seleuceia, Cassandreia, Lysimacheia; the first continuous era-count.

## Historiography

The narrative is almost entirely a reconstruction from [[diodorus-siculus|Diodorus]]
XVIII–XX (deriving from the lost, well-informed [[hieronymus-of-cardia|Hieronymus of
Cardia]]), eked out with Plutarch, Justin, Arrian's *Events after Alexander*, and
inscriptions (e.g. the Scepsis letter of Antigonus; the foundation treaty of the 302
[[league-of-corinth|Hellenic League]]). Chronology is insecure and much disputed
(e.g. "high" vs. "low" datings of the years 320–311). See
[[hellenistic-historiography]].
