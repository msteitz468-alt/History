# Wall Street Crash of 1929

The dramatic collapse of stock prices on the New York Stock Exchange in late October 1929 (Black Thursday, 24 October; Black Tuesday, 29 October), following months of speculative buildup. The immediate trigger for the worldwide Great Depression and a transformative event in American finance, politics, and culture.

## Narrative

Day-by-day reconstruction (drawing primarily on Sorkin 2025, using new access to Fed NY board minutes, Lamont papers, unpublished memoir material, trial transcripts, and contemporary reporting) shows the final months of the 1920s bull market, accelerating margin debt and bank securities-affiliate promotion, the prominent role of Charles E. Mitchell of National City Bank, and the crisis response coordinated around the House of Morgan.

**Buildup highlights (Feb–Sept 1929):** In early February, Thomas W. Lamont and Owen D. Young sailed on the Aquitania for European debt/Young Plan talks; Lamont worked shipboard and sent cables while maintaining the social schedule of the liner. The Fed issued its February 1929 “moral suasion” advisory against speculative credit; the New York Fed board held a five-hour session on 14 February keeping the discount rate at 5%. Mitchell, a National City director, participated in key NY Fed meetings. On 26–27 March, after a “landslide of selling” and spiking call rates, Mitchell publicly committed National City to continue lending “at the going rate, no matter how high that rate may go” — labeled “dynamite in a sentence” — triggering a vigorous rally but sharp criticism (Senator Glass called it a slap “in the face” to the Fed board).

Late September, after National City’s headline merger with Corn Exchange (forming the largest bank), Mitchell sailed for Europe, telling reporters at the dock (with Andrew Mellon present): “Money is all right” and “There is nothing to worry about.”

**Crash (Oct 1929):** On 24 October (Black Thursday) Lamont, astonished at the tape, organized a bankers’ support pool at the Morgan offices with an initial $120 million commitment from major banks. Richard Whitney (acting for the pool) executed dramatic floor purchases, including the famous “Ten thousand at two-oh-five.” Lamont told the press it was “a little distress selling” and that “We consider the situation” sound. Crowds packed Wall Street sidewalks; police wagons arrived; tickers ran nearly an hour late; one observer described “a kind of madness.” Rockefeller publicly backed the effort. Hoover issued a statement acknowledging “Severe disturbances in the stock market.”

On 29 October (Black Tuesday) more than 16,410,030 shares traded in the “most disastrous” day. Mitchell was summoned to a meeting at Bernard Baruch’s Fifth Avenue home after the close. Winston Churchill, visiting New York, walked the area expecting “pandemonium.”

The pool efforts and coordinated statements failed to arrest the collapse. Immediate paper losses reached billions on single days; margin calls and forced selling cascaded.

**Aftermath (to 1933):** The crash detonated the broader contraction. Mitchell became the central target of Senate investigations (Pecora Committee); he resigned National City leadership in February 1933, faced tax-evasion charges (acquitted June 1933 after dramatic testimony and courtroom ovation), and was widely labeled “Scapegoat of the Crash?” The Morgan partners’ 1933 testimony revealed the firm paid no income tax in 1931–32 and maintained a “preferred list” of influential clients, accelerating the political assault on the old Wall Street order. Richard Whitney (who had executed the 1929 pool buys and later became NYSE president) was exposed in 1938 for embezzlement and sentenced to Sing Sing. The regulatory revolution followed: SEC (1934), Glass-Steagall separations, and deposit insurance.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` combination of structural credit expansion (margin buying on unprecedented scale) and the specific decisions and public signaling of leading bankers and the Fed.
- `contributed_to:` absence of effective regulatory brakes on securities affiliates of commercial banks and lack of modern circuit breakers or disclosure rules.
- `preceded_by:` the long 1920s bull market and the easy-money environment after the 1920-21 recession.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` immediate destruction of paper wealth (estimates in the billions on single days), margin calls, forced selling, and loss of confidence that spread to the banking system.
- `produced:` the political and regulatory revolution of the 1930s (SEC 1934, Glass-Steagall, etc.).
- `enabled:` the Great Depression and its global political consequences (see [[events/great-depression]]).

## Historiography

Classic account: John Kenneth Galbraith, *The Great Crash 1929*. Sorkin (2025) supplies new archival granularity (especially Fed NY minutes and insider memoir material) focused on the motivations and private calculations of the principal bankers. Debates persist on the relative weight of monetary policy, margin practices, real-economy weaknesses, and pure psychology. The crash is universally accepted as the symbolic and practical detonator of the Depression even when scholars differ on deeper structural causes.
