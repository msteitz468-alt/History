# Domesday Book (1086)

## Narrative

**Domesday Book** is the record of the great survey of England commissioned by
**[[william-the-conqueror|William the Conqueror]]** at Christmas 1085 and largely
completed in 1086 — the most comprehensive administrative inquest of any pre-modern
European state. [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Robert Fossier, Ch. 2; Marjorie
Chibnall, Ch. 7) treats it as the supreme demonstration both of the
**[[norman-conquest-of-england-1066|Norman Conquest's]]** fusion of Norman lordship with
Anglo-Saxon administrative machinery and of the exceptional documentary wealth of
England: Fossier calls it the one source that fully "satisfies a demographer's
curiosity," unmatched anywhere in Europe before the fourteenth century.

Royal commissioners toured the shires, putting standardised questions to local juries
of hundred and vill about who held each estate in 1066 and in 1086, its value, its
ploughlands, population (villeins, bordars, cottars, slaves), mills, and resources.
From the testimony of some **276,000 informants** the survey recorded the holdings and
condition of around **1.5 million people**, organised by county and, within each, by
tenant-in-chief.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the **Norman Conquest** and the new king's need to record, value, and tax
the land he had redistributed to his followers.
`caused_by:` the **inherited strength of the Anglo-Saxon state** — the shire, hundred,
geld, and jury — which made so vast a survey administratively possible.
`contributed_to:` immediate **fiscal and military pressures** (a feared Danish invasion
in 1085) that prompted a full accounting of the kingdom's resources.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` an unparalleled snapshot of eleventh-century English society, landholding,
and economy — and a working instrument that consolidated Norman tenurial control by
fixing who held what of whom.
`enabled:` later medieval and modern historical scholarship: Domesday remains the
foundational source for the demography and economy of the
[[medieval-agricultural-revolution|eleventh-century European countryside]].
