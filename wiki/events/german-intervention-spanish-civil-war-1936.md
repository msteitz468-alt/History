# German Intervention in the Spanish Civil War (1936)

## Narrative

On the evening of 25 July 1936, after a performance of *Siegfried* conducted by Furtwängler at Bayreuth, Hitler decided to send aid to General Franco's nationalist rebels in Spain. The decision was taken against the advice of the Foreign Office and committed Germany to involvement in what rapidly became a rehearsal for wider European conflict.

The Spanish Right had refused to accept the narrow Popular Front victory of February 1936. By summer the country was descending into chaos with political murders, attacks on clergy, and burning churches. On 17 July army garrisons in Spanish Morocco rose; Franco took command the next day. A sailors' mutiny denied him transport to the mainland. He appealed to Mussolini and Hitler.

Initial request to German Foreign Office was coolly received. A German businessman, Johannes Bernhardt (Nazi Party Foreign Organization), mediated. With a written request from Franco, Bernhardt and a branch leader flew to Berlin on 24 July.

Hitler was briefed. The last report from Madrid warned of a long civil war and a Spanish soviet regime linked to France and the Soviets. Göring briefed on economic advantages (raw materials for rearmament).

At Haus Wahnfried, Bernhardt reinforced that Franco's cause was lost without German aid. Hitler hesitated when nominal sums were mentioned but, after a lengthy monologue praising Spanish nationalists and ranting about Bolshevism, decided to provide the requested aid. Ideological considerations (Bolshevik bloc sandwiching Germany) outweighed risks. He had already ordered aircraft before informing doubters.

Twenty Junkers Ju-52 transport planes (ten more than requested) plus six Heinkel fighters were sent, soon followed by more under barter (German equipment for Spanish raw materials). Initial assumption was quick decisive aid; significant military involvement (fighters, bombers, 6,500 personnel — future Legion Condor) began in October, driven by Göring.

Hitler told Goebbels the next day the intervention was 'not clear. Who knows what it's good for'. Publicly he framed it as defence against Bolshevik 'revolutionizing of the continent'.

## Analysis (Kershaw)

The decision was impulsive and ideological at root. Hitler saw a Bolshevik Spain as a dangerous complication before the inevitable showdown with the Soviet Union. Economic gains (Göring) played a supporting role but were not decisive for Hitler. The move was taken alone, presenting doubters with a fait accompli — a sign of his increased self-confidence and the weakened position of the Foreign Office.

It proved a 'baleful prelude' to wider catastrophe. Combined German-Italian operations (e.g. Guernica, April 1937) symbolized the new Axis threat. The intervention locked Germany into the Spanish quagmire longer than intended and sharpened the ideological framing of European politics.

## Verbatim / Direct (Kershaw range)

- Hitler to Goebbels (after decision): 'We're taking part a bit in Spain. Not clear. Who knows what it's good for.'
- Later (Dec 1936 cabinet): 'Spain had become the decisive issue... Germany can only wish that the crisis is deferred until we are ready... Rearm. Money can play no role.'
- Goebbels diary (15 Nov 1936): 'The showdown with Bolshevism is coming... Dominance in Europe for us is as good as certain... Therefore rearm.'

## Historiography (Kershaw)

Kershaw stresses that ideological impetus (Bolshevism) was genuine for Hitler and not mere cover for economic motives. The decision showed both his gambler's instinct and his ability to override institutional caution. Short-term gains were the premiss; long-term involvement developed under Göring's influence.
