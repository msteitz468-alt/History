# War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748)

## Narrative

On the death of Emperor Charles VI (October 1740), the Habsburg inheritance passed
to his daughter [[maria-theresa|Maria Theresa]] under the Pragmatic Sanction. In
December 1740 [[frederick-the-great|Frederick II]] of [[prussia|Prussia]] invaded
Silesia, advancing old Hohenzollern legal claims to several Silesian duchies;
Bavaria, Saxony, Spain, and France piled on with claims of their own, while
Britain backed Austria. Austria had not yet garrisoned Silesia; eighteen months
of maneuvering produced two pitched battles. At **Mollwitz (10 April 1741)** the
Austrians under Neipperg were surprised, and the Prussian infantry won the day
after Frederick himself had fled the field — a victory Frederick attributed to
"heureux hasard," happy chance. At **Chotusitz (May 1742)**, commanding in person
against Charles of Lorraine (Maria Theresa's brother-in-law, in what the press
cast as a monomachy of princely houses), Frederick won again — suffering *more*
casualties than the retreating Austrians and declining to pursue, to the fury of
his French allies (Belleisle, Valori) — yet all contemporaries, Voltaire and
Johnson included, called it a "complete victory" under the retreat rule. It was
legally sufficient: the **Peace of Breslau (July 1742)** ceded Silesia to
Prussia, which held it until 1919/1945.
Fighting continued elsewhere (Dettingen 1743; [[battle-of-fontenoy-1745|Fontenoy
1745]]; Frederick's Second Silesian War with Hohenfriedberg and Soor, 1745) until
the Peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748) restored the status quo ante — except for
Silesia, which stayed Prussian.

## Causal analysis

- caused_by: the disputed succession — a *legal* dispute over dynastic property,
  the paradigm case of [[proprietorial-warfare]].
- contributed_to: [[seven-years-war-1756]] — Maria Theresa's determination to
  recover Silesia produced the Diplomatic Revolution and the next war.

Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012]]) uses Frederick's seizure of Silesia
as his central case: what looks to moderns like naked aggression was conducted as
a legal action. Frederick's claims (through the Jägerndorf, Liegnitz, Brieg, and
Wohlau lines, against the Pragmatic Sanction his father had accepted in 1726 —
Frederick arguing his predecessor could not sign away his heirs' rights) were
treated by contemporaries (Rolt 1749, *Scots Magazine* 1741) as genuinely
"disputable" legal questions and debated in salons. He published the required
manifesto (late 1740) invoking succession law and "wars of precaution" — a
doctrine he had himself defended in the *Anti-Machiavel* — and the verdicts of
Mollwitz and Chotusitz were cashed out in treaty cessions under the
[[jus-victoriae]]. Even Korman's *The Right of Conquest*, which calls the
invasion "flagrant aggression," concedes in a footnote it "was not technically
an injuria under the international law of the eighteenth century."

## Consequence analysis

- produced: Prussian great-power status; the century-long Austro-Prussian contest
  for Germany.
- enabled: the mid-century refinement of limited cabinet warfare — armies,
  sieges, and battles operating within understood legal conventions
  ([[limited-war]]).

## Historiography

Traditional military history treats 1740 as Realpolitik aggression dressed in
legal pretext; Whitman's revisionist legal reading insists the pretexts *were* the
operative framework — Frederick's own writings agonize over the legal wager. The
war's sprawling multi-theater character also feeds the skeptical literature
(Jeremy Black) doubting that "rules" governed eighteenth-century war; see
[[jus-victoriae]] for the debate.
