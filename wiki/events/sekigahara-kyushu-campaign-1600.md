## Narrative

While the main armies of the [[eastern-coalition-sekigahara|Eastern]] and [[western-coalition-sekigahara|Western Coalitions]] converged for the decisive Battle of Sekigahara in central Japan (21 October 1600), the retired strategist [[kuroda-josui|Kuroda Josui]] (Yoshitaka) raised and financed an independent army in Kyushu, ostensibly in support of his son Kuroda Nagamasa's Eastern-aligned contingent at Sekigahara itself, but pursued with an ambition that went well beyond simple loyalty to Tokugawa Ieyasu.

Using his own wealth and local prestige, Josui quickly captured a string of castles and domains held by lords aligned with the Western Coalition across Kyushu, exploiting the absence of many daimyo and their forces who had marched north to the main theater of war. His rapid campaign of conquest across the island proceeded largely unopposed by any coordinated Western response, since Western Coalition attention and resources were consumed by the main Sekigahara theater.

News of the Eastern Coalition's decisive victory at Sekigahara reached Kyushu before Josui could complete a wider consolidation of the island under his own authority — an outcome some later historians speculate he may have hoped to parlay into greater personal power in the post-war settlement. With the war's outcome settled, Josui halted his campaign and submitted to the new Tokugawa order, and the Kuroda clan was rewarded with an enlarged domain (Fukuoka) for its service, though smaller than what an unchecked Josui might otherwise have carved out.

## Causal Analysis

The campaign was `enabled` by the vacuum of military attention in Kyushu during the main Sekigahara campaign and `contributed_to` by Kuroda Josui's personal ambition and resources; it was not itself a cause of the main battle's outcome, which was decided independently at Sekigahara.

## Consequence Analysis

The campaign secured Tokugawa authority in Kyushu in parallel with the main victory and elevated the Kuroda family's postwar standing, though it did not achieve the scale of independent power some accounts suggest Josui sought.

## Actors

[[kuroda-josui]] led the campaign; the [[eastern-coalition-sekigahara]] benefited from it; the [[western-coalition-sekigahara]]'s Kyushu-aligned lords were its targets.

## Historiography

Josui's Kyushu campaign is documented mainly through Kuroda-clan and Edo-period narrative histories, which vary in how far they credit him with ambitions of independent power versus simple loyal service; modern military historians (e.g., Sadler) treat it as a secondary theater whose outcome was ultimately overshadowed by, and subordinate to, the main battle's result.
