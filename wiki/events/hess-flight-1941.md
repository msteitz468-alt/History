# Rudolf Hess's Flight to Scotland (10 May 1941)

Deputy Führer Rudolf Hess's solo, self-directed flight from Germany to Scotland six weeks before the invasion of the USSR, in an attempt to broker a separate peace with Britain.

## Narrative

On 10 May 1941, Hess flew a Messerschmitt-110 solo from Augsburg to Scotland, parachuting near the Duke of Hamilton's estate. His stated mission, recorded in Ivone Kirkpatrick's interrogation report (later cited at Nuremberg), was to offer Germany "a completely free hand" in Europe in exchange for Britain retaining "a completely free hand in the Empire" — conditional on negotiating with "an English government other than the present one," i.e., not Churchill's.

Hitler's reaction, per Dr. Paul Schmidt and Wilhelm Keitel, was that the news hit him "as though a bomb had struck the Berghof"; he paced muttering that Hess "must have been crazy" and initially hoped Hess had drowned in the North Sea en route. Publicly, an official press communiqué branded Hess "a deluded, deranged and muddled idealist." Privately, Hitler ordered Hess shot if he ever returned to Germany, stripped him of all offices, and replaced him as Party deputy leader with Martin Bormann.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Hess's personal conviction — sincerely held, per the sources Shirer draws on — that Germany would win the war and that peace with Britain should be secured before her total defeat, combined with frustration at his own declining influence within the Nazi hierarchy as war-cabinet figures like Bormann and Göring eclipsed him. `contributed_to:` a documented personal belief in astrology; Hess told Nuremberg prison psychiatrist Dr. Douglas M. Kelley that an astrologer had read in the stars in late 1940 that he was "ordained to bring about peace." `caused_by:` [[unknown]] regarding any coordination with or foreknowledge of [[operation-barbarossa-1941]] — Kirkpatrick's interrogation notes suggest Hess either did not know of the coming invasion of the USSR or did not know it was imminent, though Soviet prosecutors at Nuremberg and Stalin himself suspected the flight was intended to free Germany for a one-front war against the USSR.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Hess's total removal from German political life and Bormann's elevation to Party deputy leader — a significant reshuffling of the innermost Nazi hierarchy on the eve of Barbarossa. `contributed_to:` a persistent, unresolved Stalinist suspicion of a British-German rapprochement aimed against the USSR, reportedly enduring for years despite Churchill's later denials — a minor but real irritant in wartime Anglo-Soviet trust. `enabled:` no discernible shift in British policy; the flight had essentially no operational effect on the war's course.

## Actors

Hess acted entirely alone and without authorization; Hitler's inner circle (Bormann, Ribbentrop, Schmidt, Keitel) managed the fallout. See [[adolf-hitler]], [[churchill-winston]].

## Historiography

[AMBIGUOUS/SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer treats Hess's motives as genuinely mixed and not fully resolved: sincere strategic conviction, personal frustration, and astrological belief all appear in the documentary and testimonial record, and Shirer does not adjudicate definitively among them. The Soviet suspicion that the flight was coordinated with British intelligence to enable a free hand against Russia is recorded by Shirer as a live historical question rather than settled fact — Kirkpatrick's contemporary interrogation notes lean against Hess having detailed foreknowledge of Barbarossa's date, but do not foreclose the broader strategic logic Stalin suspected. No consensus resolution of Hess's full motives has been reached in the historiography Shirer surveys.
