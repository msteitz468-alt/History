# The First Macedonian War (214–205 BCE)

A sideshow of the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Hannibalic War]] in which Rome,
with minimal force, kept **[[philip-v|Philip V of Macedon]]** pinned in Greece and
out of Italy — but in doing so was drawn for the first time into the internal
politics of the Greek states. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 4
(Errington).

## Narrative

The trigger was the **treaty between [[hannibal]] and Philip V (215)**, intercepted
at sea by a Roman patrol. Its terms were vague — mutual friendship, no immediate
obligations, Philip's interests to be secured in a future peace — but it confirmed
Philip's drift toward the Adriatic, where he had begun (on Demetrius of Pharos'
advice) to build an Illyrian-type fleet and threaten Rome's *amici* (Oricum,
Apollonia). Rome's response under **M. Valerius Laevinus** was limited but
effective: a small squadron repeatedly chased Philip off (he burned his own fleet
at the Aous, 214) while Philip took some inland Illyrian towns (the Parthini,
Dimallum, Lissus).

Unable to spare legions, Rome sought a **Greek proxy**: the **[[aetolian-league|
Aetolian League]]**, Philip's enemy from the Social War. The **Roman–Aetolian
treaty** (212/211) — with Rome taking moveable booty, Aetolia the conquered cities
— turned the war over to Aetolian strategy in central Greece, keeping Philip
"fully employed in the south." Attalus I of [[pergamon|Pergamon]] joined (buying
Aegina). But by 206, worn down and abandoned to a more aggressive Philip, the
Aetolians made a **separate peace** in breach of their Roman treaty. Rome,
victorious against Carthage in Spain and planning the African invasion, had no
wish to fight Philip alone, and concluded the **Peace of Phoenice (205)** — a
bilateral settlement over Illyrian possessions (Philip gave up the Parthini and
Dimallum), with lists of *adscripti* on each side.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **Hannibal–Philip treaty (215)** and Philip's Illyrian ambitions,
read by Rome as a threat to the Straits of Otranto while Hannibal was in Italy.
Errington stresses Rome's aims were **narrow and defensive** — to keep Philip out
of Italy, not to conquer in the Balkans.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **Peace of Phoenice (205)** — inconclusive, leaving the "stab in the
back" of 215 unavenged and the reckoning with Philip postponed to a time of Rome's
choosing.
enabled: the **broadening of Roman contacts** across central and southern Greece
and Asia Minor (the *adscripti* — Attalus, Athens, Sparta, Elis, Prusias of
Bithynia), the web of *amicitiae* that Rome would invoke when it returned east in
200 for the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|—]] **Second Macedonian War** (see
[[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 8).

## Historiography

[[polybius]] (via [[livy]]) is the spine; the **Hannibal–Philip treaty** survives
as a Greek text in Polybius (probably from the Macedonian archive plundered at
Pydna), against which Livy's Roman sources invented exaggerated terms (a 200-ship
invasion of Italy) to justify later severity toward Macedon. The Thyrrheum
inscription preserves part of the Roman–Aetolian treaty. The war is central to the
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism|debate]] over whether Rome's eastern involvement was
reactive (Errington, Briscoe) or systemically expansionist (Harris).
