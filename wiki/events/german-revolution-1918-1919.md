## Narrative

The German Revolution began not from below but from above: General Erich
Ludendorff, having told his government on September 29, 1918, that the war was
lost, forced the kaiser's government to seek an armistice on the basis of
President Wilson's Fourteen Points, and shifted political responsibility onto
the civilian parliamentarians he had sidelined in 1917 ("They made this soup.
Now let them eat it."). Prince Max of Baden became chancellor and instituted a
genuine constitutional revolution from above: making the chancellor
responsible to a parliamentary majority rather than to the kaiser, and ending
Prussia's antiquated three-class voting system.

Revolution from below followed within weeks. When Ludendorff briefly recovered
his nerve in late October and proposed continuing the war to hold
Alsace-Lorraine's mines, Prince Max removed him from command, but suspicion
that the kaiser also opposed an armistice deepened when Wilhelm II left Berlin
for army headquarters on October 29. Ordered to sea for one last engagement
with the British, sailors of the German High Seas Fleet mutinied at Kiel on
November 4; workers' and soldiers' councils spread rapidly to military depots
and war plants nationwide. On November 7 the socialist parliamentarian Kurt
Eisner led an uprising in Munich that expelled the last Wittelsbach king of
Bavaria — see [[munich-revolution-1918-1919]]. On November 9, mass
demonstrations in Berlin left the senior army command itself — Generals
Hindenburg and Groener — to persuade Wilhelm II to abdicate before all
authority collapsed.

Two rival proclamations followed that same afternoon: at 2:00 p.m. Philipp
Scheidemann of the majority Social Democratic Party (SPD) declared a
parliamentary republic from a Reichstag window; at 4:00 p.m. Karl Liebknecht
declared a revolutionary socialist republic from the royal palace, now held by
council delegations. A provisional executive of six people's commissars,
dominated by the SPD's Friedrich Ebert, emerged from the Berlin soldiers' and
workers' councils on November 10 — reformist in composition despite its
revolutionary origin.

Unlike Russia, Germany's councils remained under moderate SPD control: at the
all-German congress of councils in December 1918, only 10 of 488 delegates
were Spartacists (the revolutionary faction led by Liebknecht and Rosa
Luxemburg, who rejected Lenin's disciplined-party model in favor of
spontaneity and direct worker control). The councils endorsed elections to a
constitutional assembly rather than a Russian-style soviet state. Civil
society and local administration — illustrated by Hamburg, where the old
merchant families retained financial leverage over the councils — did not
collapse in Germany as it had in Russia in 1917; there was only a brief
grassroots power vacuum.

Ebert, fearing the Spartacists more than the old imperial establishment, struck
a fateful bargain with General Wilhelm Groener on November 9: the provisional
government would leave the Imperial Officer Corps intact in exchange for
military help suppressing the councils. When Ebert dismissed the
council-installed Berlin police chief in early January 1919, demonstrators
occupied public buildings; Liebknecht and Luxemburg, who had just founded the
German Communist Party (KPD) on December 30, reluctantly assumed leadership of
an uprising they doubted could succeed. Lacking troops loyal to the new
regime, Ebert relied on the Imperial Officer Corps and the Freikorps —
paramilitary volunteer units of demobilized frontline veterans. The Freikorps
crushed the Spartacist rising; Freikorps officers murdered Liebknecht and
Luxemburg while transferring them between prisons on January 15, 1919, and
dumped their bodies in a Berlin canal. Hundreds of Spartacists died in Berlin
in January, and roughly a thousand more in a second Berlin revolt in March.
Defense Minister Gustav Noske, nominally in command, said: "Somebody has to be
the bloodhound."

Bavaria proved the exception to rapid pacification. After Eisner's
assassination in February 1919, reformist socialists could not hold power;
during the week of April 7, 1919, a workers' council and a group of
intellectuals (including the playwright Ernst Toller) proclaimed a Munich
communist republic. The Freikorps destroyed it with particular savagery; see
[[munich-revolution-1918-1919]] for the episode's detail.

A constitutional assembly elected in January 1919 met at Weimar — chosen
deliberately for its distance from Prussian militarism — and produced the
Weimar Constitution, with proportional representation and women's suffrage.
But because Ebert had left the traditional bureaucracy, imperial army, great
industrial cartels, and Prussian landed estates intact, the resulting
[[weimar-republic]] combined the most advanced political structures then
devised with an unreconstructed imperial society beneath them. See
[[weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]] for the Republic's subsequent history.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by`: Ludendorff's admission on September 29, 1918, that the war
  could not be won, and his maneuver to transfer blame for defeat to civilian
  politicians.
- `caused_by`: the Kiel mutiny of November 4, 1918, which spread council
  formation nationwide within days.
- `contributed_to` (not `caused_by`): the demonstration effect of the
  [[russian-revolution-1917]] — Paxton notes the Bolshevik example showed
  "war-weary Habsburg [and German] subjects how fragile an ancient autocracy
  was," but Germany's revolution had independent domestic causes (military
  defeat, food shortages under Allied blockade, and constitutional paralysis)
  and diverged sharply in outcome.
- `preceded_by` (temporal, not causal): the July Peace Resolution of the
  German parliament (1917) and Prince Max's constitutional reforms of October
  1918 — genuine liberalizing steps that came too late to satisfy accumulated
  popular anger.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: the [[weimar-republic]] and its constitution.
- `enabled`: the survival, largely intact, of the Imperial Officer Corps,
  senior bureaucracy, judiciary, and industrial cartels — the "unreconstructed
  imperial society" that coexisted uneasily with Weimar democracy and
  contributed to its later vulnerabilities (see
  [[weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]]).
- `produced`: the permanent, bitter split of German (and by extension
  European) socialism into a reformist SPD and a revolutionary KPD, later
  formalized internationally by the Comintern's Twenty-One Points (1920).
- `contributed_to`: the postwar legend of the "stab in the back"
  (*Dolchstoßlegende*), which Ludendorff himself helped originate despite his
  own decisive role in requesting the armistice.
- `concurrent_with`: the [[munich-revolution-1918-1919]] and the broader wave
  of European postwar unrest treated comparatively in
  [[russian-revolution-1917]] and by Paxton's cross-national analysis (Italy,
  Hungary — see [[kun-bela]]).

## Historiography

Paxton frames the German Revolution as a structural counterpoint to the
[[russian-revolution-1917]]: identical triggering pattern (military strain,
mutiny, council formation, dual power) but a wholly different outcome, because
Germany lacked a land-hungry peasant mass and a disciplined revolutionary
party with Lenin's tactical audacity, while the SPD retained control of the
councils and moderate leadership at every level down to city administration
(Hamburg is Paxton's example, citing Richard A. Comfort, *Revolutionary
Hamburg: Labor Politics in the Early Weimar Republic* (1966)). Paxton's
account draws on the 1960s wave of German-revolution scholarship: A. J. Ryder,
*The German Revolution of 1918* (1967); Allan Mitchell, *Revolution in
Bavaria* (1965); Werner T. Angress, *Stillborn Revolution* (1963); and J. P.
Nettl's biography of Luxemburg (1989 abbreviated ed.).

The comparative-revolution question Paxton poses explicitly — why did
revolution succeed in Russia and fail everywhere else in 1917–1920? — treats
the German case as the paradigm "near miss": revolutionary pressure comparable
to Russia's but resolved into constitutional rather than social revolution.
This comparative framing is itself a historiographical stance worth flagging
for a `wiki/comparisons/` treatment alongside Hungary's Béla Kun regime (see
[[kun-bela]]) and Italy's 1919–1920 factory occupations.
