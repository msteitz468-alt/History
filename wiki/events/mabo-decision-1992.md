## Narrative

Eddie Koiki Mabo was born in 1936 on the island of Mer (Murray Islands, Torres Strait). As a community leader, traditional owner, and activist, he joined with Meriam leaders David Passi and James Rice to seek native title rights for the Mer Islanders. Following a ten-year legal battle, in 1992 the High Court of Australia ruled in their favour.

Justice Brennan stated:

> "The common law of this country would perpetuate an injustice if it were to continue to embrace the enlarged notion of terra nullius and to persist in characterising the indigenous inhabitants of the Australian colonies as people too low in the scale of social organisation to be acknowledged as possessing rights and interests in land."

The High Court found "the Meriam people were entitled as against the whole world to the possession, occupation, use and enjoyment" of their island. Tragically, Eddie Mabo died of cancer six months before the judgment was handed down.

## Consequence Analysis

The Mabo decision "required a legislative response from the Australian government since the doctrine of 'native title' applied all over Australia." The Native Title Act 1993 was negotiated between the Commonwealth, States, and a representative team of Indigenous negotiators. The Keating government simultaneously undertook a "social justice package" including a land acquisition fund (1995).

Paul Keating's Redfern Park speech (December 1992), delivered before the native title legislation was negotiated, acknowledged: "We took the traditional lands and smashed the traditional way of life. We brought the disasters. The alcohol. We committed the murders. We took the children from their mothers. We practised discrimination and exclusion. It was our ignorance and our prejudice."

The 1996 **Wik decision** extended the principle: the High Court found that a pastoral lease did not fully extinguish native title — "it was possible for native title and pastoral rights to coexist." The Howard government subsequently amended the Native Title Act to limit Wik's impact, abolishing some provisions but forced by the Senate to retain others.

## Historiography

Mabo is the single most consequential legal event in twentieth-century Australian Indigenous history. Historical debate has centred on the gap between the symbolic power of the decision and the narrow, heavily contested legislative implementation that followed — Lisa Strelein's *Compromised Jurisprudence* (2006) analyses how the Native Title Act and subsequent court decisions eroded the promise of Mabo. The tension between native title (individual or group property rights under common law) and the broader claim to Indigenous sovereignty and self-determination remains unresolved.
