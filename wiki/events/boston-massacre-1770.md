# Boston Massacre (1770)

## Narrative

### Prelude (late February–4 March 1770)

Four days after the funeral of **Christopher Seider**—an eleven-year-old boy shot by customs official Ebenezer Richardson after a crowd attacked Richardson’s house during an importer-boycott confrontation—midday brawling erupted near the ropewalks steps from [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]]’s door. Ropeworkers traded blows with grenadiers of the **Twenty-ninth Regiment** through four rounds (cutlasses, clubs, hemp sticks). Weekend scuffles and rumors of Monday blood followed; Adams later insisted soldiers had promised “blood running in the streets.”

[[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Schiff]] frames Seider’s **26 February** funeral under the Liberty Tree—about 500 schoolboys in pairs, six pallbearers, a half-mile procession, ~30 coaches, papers calling Seider “little hero and first martyr”—as political theater Adams stage-managed. [[hutchinson-thomas|Hutchinson]] complained that had the Sons power to resurrect the child they would not, “preferring the grand funeral.”

### King Street, evening of 5 March 1770

Under a slim moon early on the evening of **5 March 1770**, a confrontation on **King Street** near the customs house escalated from crowd taunts (snowballs, oyster shells, ice at a sentry) to the arrival of **Captain Thomas Preston** and eight men. Bayonets “electrified” the throng; soldiers backed to the customs-house wall amid shouts of “God damn you, fire and be damned, we know you dare not.” A stick felled a grenadier on the ice; shot(s) rang out; bells tolled; town drums called for militia; the crowd swelled past a thousand. **Five men** lay dead or dying (including a forty-seven-year-old Black sailor shot twice in the chest—**Crispus Attucks**—and a teenaged ship’s mate with two bullets in the back, dead on the spot); five more were wounded, mostly apprentices and immigrants. Crimson snow marked the street.

Hutchinson arrived amid sticks and cutlasses; from a balcony he pleaded for impartial inquiry. By **1:00 a.m.** regiments were ordered to barracks; by **2:00 a.m.** Preston was arrested and the eight soldiers imprisoned; by **4:00 a.m.** the town was quiet. Hutchinson recorded so many disparate accounts that he could not supply a single true narrative. Hours earlier, an ocean away, Parliament had begun debate on **Townshend** repeal.

### Immediate political outcome (6–27 March)

Invisible on the night of the 5th, Adams headed the selectmen’s committee the next morning demanding troop removal. A second visit asserted the acting governor’s charter power over the military; Adams warned of **10,000** country men and “the most terrible [night] that had ever been seen in America.” Hutchinson cited high treason. Adams’s ultimatum—“If you have power to remove one regiment, you have power to remove both”—and the threat of countryside descent forced Hutchinson to ask **Colonel Dalrymple** to remove **both** regiments to **Castle William**. Hancock announced the dual-regiment removal to Old South; for two weeks John and Samuel Adams, Hancock, Molineux, Warren, and others walked night watch with muskets until dawn. On **27 March 1770** Boston was free of soldiers save the nine imprisoned. Andrew Oliver groaned that “an unarmed multitude… gained a complete victory over two regiments”; Molineux escorted the redcoats to the wharf “to protect them… from the indignation of the people.”

Thursday’s funeral: four cortèges to King Street, bells for miles, crowd estimated at **~10,000**; a fifth victim died a week later. Hutchinson: every funeral “inflames them against the troops.”

### Trials and afterlife of the narrative

**Preston’s trial** (October 1770): sole question whether he ordered fire; [[adams-john|John Adams]] argued firing after assault by a “riotous mob” shouting “Kill them!” was not murder; Preston **acquitted 30 October**. Soldiers’ trial from **20 November**: none of twelve jurors lived in Boston; ~85 witnesses; John Adams’s **4 December** summation described “a motley rabble of saucy boys, Negroes and mulattos, Irish teagues and outlandish Jack Tars”; six **not guilty**, two convicted of manslaughter and branded “M” on the right thumb by Greenleaf.

Samuel appears first to have named the skirmish a “**horrid massacre**.” Within days he bundled the massacre, Seider’s murder, and the “intended assassination” of Otis as a customs-commissioner plot; **96 depositions** and *A Short Narrative of the Horrid Massacre in Boston* (Bowdoin ± Adams) went to London while local circulation was withheld pending trials—pages meant for imperial opinion. After the verdicts, Adams as **Vindex** ran ~10 *Gazette* essays (Dec–Jan) relitigating the night against Philanthrop (Jonathan Sewall). **March 5, 1771** commemorations—bells, Revere’s illuminated triptych, Young speaking from the Manufactory House—became annual until **1783**, when the calendar yielded to the Fourth of July.

[[revere-paul|Paul Revere]]’s engraving fixed the visual repertoire of martyrdom and kept public anger focused.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** occupation-era friction after 1768 troop landings and customs enforcement conflict in a garrison town.
- **contributed_to:** ropewalk and street brawls; Seider killing and funeral theater; billeting disputes that left troops “morally in the wrong” in town warehouses rather than Castle barracks.
- **preceded_by:** Townshend duties, nonimportation enforcement, and chronic civilian harassment of “lobster backs.”

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** five civilian dead, dual-regiment withdrawal to Castle Island, and a durable martyr narrative (*Short Narrative*, Revere engraving, March 5 calendar).
- **enabled:** further radicalization of Boston opinion and Adams’s demonstration that firmness could expel “exterior power” civil or military.
- **followed_by:** partial Townshend repeal with residual tea duty; quiet of 1771–72 then committees and tea crisis.

## Historiography

**Narrative murk is structural, not merely partisan.** Some heard Preston order fire, others not; snowballs versus bricks/clubs; thousands versus fifty; first victim with or without a stick; moon position disputed. Preston later: “scarcely possible for the soldiers to know who said fire, or don’t fire.” A dockworker: the word “was in everybody’s mouth.”

**Competing pamphlets** framed the fight for London: *A Short Narrative of the Horrid Massacre* versus *A Fair Account of the Late Unhappy Disturbance* (calling “massacre” a “gross abuse of language,” demoting the event to a “ridiculous fray,” soldiers passive as long as self-preservation allowed). Military depositions slipped out **16 March** on a man-of-war alleging Bostonians instigated the affair and plotted to raid the customs treasury.

**Cousins’ conflicting Attucks portraits** crystallize courtroom versus propaganda needs: Samuel—Attucks leaning on a stick, non-threatening, right to carry a stick; John in court—formidable “hero of the night” with clubs, “Kill them… Knock them over,” trying to “knock their brains out.”

John Adams took the Preston/soldiers defense (Quincy Jr. conferred with Samuel first; Samuel likely knew); Samuel helped select prosecution (Paine via Molineux) and withheld the *Short Narrative* from local circulation under a “humanity” rationale while pages were designed for London.

Numbers, provocation sequence, and the “massacre” label remain contested; Hinderaker and others reassess crowd and military perspectives. Schiff follows Adams’s **political use** of the event—naming, dual-regiment ultimatum, depositions, Vindex, commemorative calendar—more than pure courtroom micro-history.
