# Nivelle Offensive 1917 (Chemin des Dames / Aisne)

## Narrative

The Nivelle Offensive was the French attempt in spring 1917 under General Robert Nivelle to achieve a decisive breakthrough on the Western Front through a "strategy of decisive battle" employing a novel "formula" of artillery preparation and rapid continuous exploitation. Planning began under Joffre but was radically reshaped by Nivelle after his appointment in mid-December 1916 as commander of the armies of the north and northeast. The main attack fell on the Chemin des Dames ridge in the Aisne sector by Reserve Army Group (Micheler) with Fifth and Sixth Armies, supported by Central Army Group (Pétain) and a British supporting attack near Arras.

Nivelle's concept: fix the enemy with attacks on the flanks of the Noyon salient (British north of Somme/Arras area, Northern Army Group south), followed by a surprise "grand" penetration on the Aisne by Central/Reserve Army Group that would reach the enemy's heavy artillery in 24-48 hours, followed by "audacious lateral exploitation" and commitment of maneuver forces to destroy reserves in open battle. "The objective is the destruction of the principal mass of the enemy's forces on the Western Front. This can be attained only by a decisive battle, delivered against the reserves of the adversary and followed by an intensive exploitation." (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 327)

The offensive opened with British Arras attack 9 April; main French assault 16 April (Reserve Army Group) and 17 April (Central). Despite unprecedented artillery (5,350 guns firing over 11 million rounds by early May for Micheler's group alone), poor weather (fog, rain, snow, winds), German air superiority, reverse-slope positions on Chemin des Dames, and supply difficulties limited effects. Infantry encountered intact machine guns on reverse slopes and strong reserves. II Colonial Corps on Sixth Army's right reached the crest but was fixed by undamaged MGs: "They are welcomed and fixed in place by the deadly fire of numerous machine guns that, located on the [reverse side of the] slope, outside the reach of our projectiles, have remained undamaged." (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 350, quoting II Colonial Corps after-action)

Limited gains occurred (e.g., Sixth Army left advanced 6-7 km by 20 April capturing 5,300 prisoners), but no breakthrough. By 20 April Nivelle acknowledged failure and shifted to limited operations. Total French casualties 16-25 April estimated at 134,000 (30,000 killed, 100,000 wounded, 4,000 captured). Some 28,500 prisoners and 187 guns taken overall, but hopes for decisive victory were dashed.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[joffre-joseph]]` relief amid political discontent after Somme and Romania collapse; Chamber closed session Nov-Dec 1916; Roques report; reorganization giving Joffre only advisory role. `contributed_to: [[nivelle-robert]]` rapid rise and "can do" optimism after Verdun successes (capture Douaumont and Vaux Oct/Dec 1916) and development of rolling barrage/artillery methods; Nivelle promoted "We now have the formula." (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 324)

Nivelle abandoned Joffre's emphasis on successive methodical battles in favor of continuous thrusts and penetration/breakthrough. "Instead of emphasizing successive efforts in a methodical battle, however, Nivelle emphasized continuous thrusts. ... He also abandoned the idea of 'rupturing' an enemy position and instead favored penetration and breakthrough." (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 325) He insisted on "violence, brutality, and speed" and one single grand battle, not prolonged successive efforts.

Coalition frictions and German withdrawal to Hindenburg Line (Feb-Mar 1917, shortening front ~40 km and freeing 13-14 divisions) altered supporting attacks, concentrating power on main effort but also alerting and strengthening Germans in Aisne sector (French intel noted rise from ~4-5 to 9-10+ divisions + artillery emplacements). Loose security by Nivelle (discussing plans openly, captured orders) forfeited surprise.

Political and subordinate doubts (Painlevé, Micheler, Pétain) led to Compiègne conference 6 April: Nivelle offered resignation; compromise retained offensive but with understanding to halt if no early rupture. "An offensive battle followed by the prudent engagement of the reserves if a rupture of the enemy front is obtained, or the halting of the battle if a wide breach is not made in the enemy front in the first efforts." (Poincaré summary, A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 344) Ribot: "if the desired results are not attained after a brief period, we will not stubbornly continue the battle indefinitely as we did on the Somme." (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 344)

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` rapid recognition of failure; morale collapse ("the army was very fragile when it undertook the Nivelle offensive; it was even more fragile when the operation ended." A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 354); immediate shift to limited attacks to "drive the enemy back from Reims and complete the occupation of the Chemin des Dames."

`produced: [[petain-philippe]]` elevation (chief of staff of the army 29 April 1917, paving way for full command and "healing" strategy). `contributed_to:` French mutinies of spring 1917 (context of exhausted army, dashed hopes after high expectations). `enabled:` transition away from decisive-battle search toward Pétain's limited offensives, active defense, and preservation of forces for 1918.

Casualties exceeded any month since Nov 1914 in far shorter time; gains would have been hailed earlier but "the demeaning of extravagant hopes" produced sense of disaster. "The effect of Nivelle's offensive was not yet apparent, but the appointment of Pétain as chief of staff marked the beginning of new strategy and operational methods for France." (A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 354)

## Historiography

Doughty draws on AFGG and SHAT archives for detailed reconstruction of French high command debates, Nivelle's over-optimism, and political-military tensions. Contrasts Nivelle's certainty ("the experience is conclusive; our method has proved itself" at Verdun farewell, A Strategy of Decisive Battle, Early 1917 l 324) with subordinates' and politicians' reservations. Notes loss of surprise and German adaptations as critical. Positions the offensive as turning point from Joffre-era attrition search and "continuous battle" toward Pétain's more cautious "healing" and methodical approaches. Acknowledges French artillery gains but emphasizes fragility of the army exposed by failure.
