# The Italian Intervention Debate (August 1914 – May 1915)

## Narrative

The nine-month contest over Italian entry into the First World War, ending
in the "radiant days of May" 1915 and war on Austria-Hungary. Its
load-bearing feature for [[sternhell-thesis|Sternhell]] is **left
interventionism**: the revolutionary syndicalists reached near-unanimity for
war — the "myth of the revolutionary war" replacing the failed general
strike. Tullio Masotti called for defending "the revolution against
reaction" on 1 August 1914; [[de-ambris-alceste|De Ambris]]'s 18 August
speech attacked neutralism, blamed Fichtean "historical mysticism" for
German militarism, and equated the war with the French Revolution, splitting
the USI (Borghi's anarchist majority stayed neutralist);
[[panunzio-sergio|Panunzio]] demanded the destruction of "the German feudal
and military hegemony." The dissidents founded the **Fascio rivoluzionario
d'azione internazionalista** (Milan, 1 October 1914; manifesto 5 October,
signed by Corridoni, Michele Bianchi, Amilcare De Ambris, Olivetti, Cesare
Rossi, Libero Tancredi), whose manifesto declared that "major historical
contradictions are resolved not by being denied in ideological terms but by
being transcended in practice." Olivetti relaunched *Pagine libere* in its
support.

[[mussolini-benito|Mussolini]]'s break followed: his 18 October 1914
*Avanti!* article for "active and committed" neutrality, disavowal by the
party, resignation that evening, expulsion, and the launch of *Il Popolo
d'Italia* in November 1914. Of ~50 known revolutionary syndicalist leaders,
36 went to the front; within six months 9 were wounded and 6 killed
(Corridoni among the dead in 1915).

## Causal Analysis

- preceded_by: Red Week (June 1914) — the Ancona shootings and national
  general strike that revealed "revolutionary potential."
- contributed_to (per Sternhell, the crucial point): the interventionist
  crisis did **not create** the socialist-national synthesis — the ideology
  predated August 1914; the crisis gave it cadres and a mass cause,
  converting an intellectual convergence into a political force. The Libyan
  War controversy of 1911–12 had already "paved the way for the unanimity of
  the summer of 1914."

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: the interventionist fasci — organizational ancestor of the Fasci
  di Combattimento (March 1919) and thus of [[march-on-rome-1922]].
- enabled: [[fiume-expedition-1919]] (the interventionist veterans and the
  "mutilated victory" grievance).

## Historiography

Sternhell/Sznajder read left interventionism as the hinge of fascism's
prehistory; the De Felice school gives more weight to the war experience
itself and to postwar conditions than to pre-1914 doctrine. See
[[fascism-and-the-political-left]].
