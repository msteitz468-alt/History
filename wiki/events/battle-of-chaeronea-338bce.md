# The Battle of Chaeronea, 338 BCE

## Narrative

At **Chaeronea**, in Boeotia, in **338 BCE**, **[[philip-ii-of-macedon|Philip II of
Macedon]]** destroyed the combined armies of **[[places/athens|Athens]] and Thebes** and with them the
**independence of the Greek city-states**. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 15,
Ellis) sets the battle as the climax of Philip's drive into central Greece, after the
orator **Demosthenes** had at last forged an Athenian–Theban coalition to stop him. Philip
commanded the Macedonian right; his eighteen-year-old son **[[alexander-the-great|
Alexander]]** led the cavalry on the left, where he is said to have broken the Theban line
and **annihilated the elite Sacred Band**, which fell where it stood. The allied army was
routed.

## Settlement

Chaeronea was decisive but Philip's settlement was **calculatedly moderate**. He treated
**Athens** with leniency (returning its prisoners, seeking its goodwill for the coming
Persian war) while dealing harshly with **Thebes** (a garrison on the Cadmea, an exiled
oligarchy). In the Peloponnese he **warned and then invaded Sparta**, stripping it of
border districts in favour of **Argos, Megalopolis, Tegea and Messene** — "constricting the
Spartan state tightly within a circle of grateful beneficiaries." These dispositions were
then given binding legal form in the **[[league-of-corinth|League of Corinth]]** (337).

## Significance

Chaeronea is conventionally the **end of the free Greek polis** as the arbiter of its own
fate. The autonomous city-state, whose limits the whole fourth century had exposed (see
[[city-state]]), was now subordinated to a **Macedonian hegemony** — formalized in the
League of Corinth and harnessed to Philip's **panhellenic war on Persia**. The
[[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|tactical revolution]] begun by [[epaminondas]] reached its term
in the Macedonian combined-arms victory; the Sacred Band that had smashed Sparta at Leuctra
was itself destroyed at Chaeronea. It is the threshold of the **Hellenistic age**.

## Historiography

[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]] (Ch.
8) dates the battle precisely to **September 338** and confirms that Philip's cavalry,
commanded by the eighteen-year-old **[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]**, "delivered a
decisive victory in which enemy casualties were enormous" (exact figures are not securely
transmitted and should not be asserted). Philip forced the engagement only after failed
diplomatic pressure connected to the **Amphissa affair** and the Delphic Amphictyony —
part of the pretext, alongside genuine strategic necessity, for his advance into central
Greece. On the aftermath, Osborne confirms that in spring **337** the **League of
Corinth** was formed at Corinth, binding all signatories to enforce a new **Common Peace**
through synod decisions; its very first act was to vote **war on Persia**, "to punish
Persia for the destruction of their sanctuaries a hundred and fifty years before" — the
Panhellenist slogan of "freedom of the Greeks" (already 150 years old by Chaeronea) put to
one final, cynical use by a Macedonian king.

Osborne's Epilogue also frames the historiographical problem underlying the whole battle:
the primary surviving window onto the run-up to Chaeronea is **Demosthenes'** own
speeches (no independent history of Philip's reign survives), and Demosthenes cast
himself as the "champion of Greek freedom against a barbarian" — a self-portrait his
rival **Aeschines** and other opponents undercut, arguing Demosthenes' policy was driven
more by personal ambition and inconsistency than principled resistance. See
[[demosthenes-orator]].
