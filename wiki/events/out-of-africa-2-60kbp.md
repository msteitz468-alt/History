## Narrative

Out of Africa 2 is the dispersal of behaviorally modern *Homo sapiens* from
Africa into the rest of the world, beginning approximately 50,000–60,000 BP.
Unlike Out of Africa 1, which involved multiple archaic *Homo* species and
left little genetic trace in living humans, Out of Africa 2 is the event from
which all non-African modern human populations descend.

**Precursor: the Levant "false start" (~125,000–70,000 BP)**
Anatomically modern humans appear in the Levant around 125,000–70,000 BP,
during a warm interglacial. But they disappeared and were replaced by
Neanderthal populations as climate deteriorated. These early Levantine
populations apparently lacked the behavioral resilience of later fully modern
humans and seem not to have contributed to the main dispersal.

**Population bottleneck (~70,000 BP)**
Genetic evidence suggests human populations shrank to perhaps 10,000
individuals around 70,000 BP. The cause may have been the eruption of
Mt. Toba in Sumatra (~72,000 BP), one of the largest volcanic events of the
last million years, which may have triggered a "volcanic winter." Or the
bottleneck may reflect rapid growth within the founding population from which
all modern humans descended (making the bottleneck an artifact of genetic
reconstruction rather than a demographic catastrophe). The question is unresolved.

**Linguistic evidence for East African origin**
Comparative linguistics provides unexpected support: modern languages show a
clear trend away from complex consonantal systems as one moves outward from
eastern and southern Africa. This is consistent with all modern languages
deriving from a single origin population in East Africa around 70,000 BP.
Only in East Africa do we find all three major genetic groupings of modern
humans — further corroborating the origin.

**Main dispersal (~50,000–60,000 BP)**
The main dispersal left Africa through the Near Eastern bottleneck (the Sinai/
Levant corridor, or possibly via a southern route across the Bab-el-Mandeb).
Groups then spread in multiple directions:

**Asian evidence and its caveats (Dennell, Ch. 17)**: The earliest Levantine
*H. sapiens* (Skhul and Qafzeh, Israel) date ~125,000–70,000 BP but were displaced
by Neanderthals as MIS 4 cold conditions set in ~70,000 BP — the "false start."
The earliest Israeli communities used a Middle Paleolithic (Neanderthal-like)
toolkit; an Upper Paleolithic blade technology developed only after ~45,000 BP.
Indian early modern humans probably used a Middle Stone Age toolkit (skeletal
remains lacking). Southeast Asian populations (from ~40,000–50,000 BP) used a very
simple stone technology that nonetheless succeeded in tropical rainforests.
Dennell stresses three caveats: (1) almost no skeletal evidence from SW Asia
between 190,000 and 125,000–70,000 BP; (2) no skeletal evidence between the Levant
(~70,000 BP) and Tam Pa Ling (Laos, ~45,000 BP); (3) the vanished Levant population
left no modern genetic signature, so modern humans may have left Africa *before*
125,000 BP and crossed southern Asia before 60,000 BP — genetic estimates from
living populations may underestimate the true antiquity.

**In Europe**
Modern humans entered Europe ~42,000 BP from the Near East, through the
Caucasus or across the Bosphorus, probably during a warm period. The
proto-Aurignacian industry shows the diversity characteristic of modern humans.
Artefacts include eyed needles, hare traps, and rich artistic products
including the Löwenmensch ivory carving from Germany (~40,000 BP, possibly
depicting a shamanistic figure).

Neanderthals disappeared within ~10,000 years of modern human arrival, though
some interbreeding occurred — non-African modern humans carry ~1–4% Neanderthal
DNA. The divergence in material culture between Neanderthals (little change
over 200,000 years) and modern humans (rapid innovation) probably reflects
the linguistic capacity of modern humans and their ability to accumulate and
transmit cultural innovations across generations.

## Fagan & Durrani (2020) — Toba Revision and Dispersal Dates

Fagan & Durrani (2020) present two competing origin models — "regional
continuity" (multiregional, worldwide *H. erectus* populations independently
evolving to sapiens) vs. "Out of Africa" (single African origin, later
spread) — and note Out of Africa is "now almost universally accepted," though
modified: post-dispersal interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans
complicates a pure-replacement model.

**[CONTRADICTION] flag on the Toba bottleneck.** This page's existing
narrative treats the Mt. Toba eruption (~72,000 BP) as a live candidate cause
of the ~70,000 BP population bottleneck, calling the question "unresolved."
Fagan & Durrani (2020) go substantially further and treat the catastrophist
"volcanic winter" model as **largely rejected**: newer climate
reconstructions put actual post-eruption (~73,500 BP) cooling at only ~2.5°C
for a few years (locally as little as 1°C in India); Jwalapuram (India),
Lake Malawi sediment cores (2013), and a 2015 East African climate study
found no evidence of a severe volcanic winter or population disruption
coincident with the eruption. Fagan & Durrani date the genetic bottleneck
later, to ~50,000–20,000 BP, and attribute it to the small founder population
of the dispersal itself rather than to Toba. This should be read as a
2020-edition update superseding the more equivocal framing above; a
controversies page entry may be warranted if this directly conflicts with a
source the wiki weights more heavily for the bottleneck question.

**Refined dispersal chronology.** By 100,000 BP, anatomically modern humans
were already present in the Middle East (Qafzeh/Skhul); elsewhere outside
Africa there is little evidence until ~60,000–40,000 BP. A "Green Sahara"
humid corridor (before 100,000 BP to ~40,000 BP) is proposed as allowing
crossing via the Nile Valley/Sahara and via the Red Sea into Yemen/Arabia
toward South Asia. Small numbers of modern humans left Africa into the
Middle East by 50,000 BP; within roughly another 5,000 years they reached
Europe/Eurasia (~45,000 BP) and the Eurasian plains. South and Southeast Asia
were settled before 50,000 BP; Australia/New Guinea before 45,000 BP; the
timing for China is debated, "probably 50,000 bp." A contested early outlier:
**Fuyan Cave**, southern China, where 47 modern human teeth have been dated
to 120,000–80,000 BP — a potentially very early modern-human presence in
East Asia that Fagan & Durrani flag as contested/uncertain, consistent with
this page's own note (via Dennell) on sparse and contested Asian skeletal
evidence.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[behavioral-modernity]]` — The critical enabling factor was
the emergence of full modern language and the cognitive capacity for complex
social networking, cumulative cultural learning, and long-range planning.
Without this, the prior Levant dispersal failed; with it, the main dispersal
succeeded in occupying every continent.

`contributed_to: climate oscillations` — Warmer periods opened the Near
Eastern corridor and expanded habitable ranges. The pulses of the dispersal
correlate with climatic ameliorations.

`contributed_to: technological advantages` — Modern stone tool technologies,
composite tools, projectile weapons, and (eventually) cold-weather clothing
allowed colonization of environments inaccessible to archaic hominins.

## Consequence Analysis

The consequences of Out of Africa 2 are literally the foundation of all
subsequent human history. It produced:
- The entire geography of human genetic diversity (with African populations
  retaining the most diversity; non-African populations representing a subset
  of the African variation)
- The extinction of all other hominin species (Neanderthals, Denisovans,
  possibly late-surviving *Homo erectus* in East Asia)
- The colonization of Australasia and the Americas — the last major
  uninhabited continental areas
- The foundational Paleolithic cultural traditions that shape the deepest
  layers of regional histories

## Historiography

Out of Africa 2 is the best-supported major event in all of prehistory.
The genetic evidence is overwhelming: all non-African modern humans are
descended from a single small founding population that left Africa within the
last 100,000 years. The timing, exact routes, and number of dispersal waves
remain actively debated.

Key controversies:
- Whether there was one major dispersal or multiple waves (the southern coastal
  route hypothesis vs. the Levantine corridor hypothesis)
- The exact date of the main dispersal (genetic estimates: 50,000–70,000 BP;
  some archaeological evidence for earlier presence)
- The extent of interbreeding with archaic hominins en route
