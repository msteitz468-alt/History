# The Three Kingdoms Period (220–280 CE)

The era of formal tripartition that followed the collapse of the **[[han-dynasty|Eastern Han]]** —
when three regional powers each claimed the **[[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate of Heaven]]** but none
could force reunification. From [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]].

## Narrative

The Eastern Han's authority disintegrated after the **eunuch massacre of 189 CE** and the warlord
wars that followed the **Yellow Turban** religious rebellion. In **220 CE** the last Han emperor,
**Xian**, abdicated in favour of **Cao Pi** — son of the warlord **Cao Cao** — who founded **Wei**
in the north with its capital at [[luoyang|Luoyang]]. In **221** **Liu Bei**, claiming Han descent,
proclaimed himself Emperor Zhaolie of **Shu Han** in the southwest (Sichuan); in **229** **Sun
Quan** declared himself Emperor of **Wu** in the southeast (the lower Yangzi). The three "empires"
held a **rough balance of power**; intensive fighting declined after the 230s.

The division ended from the north: the **Sima** family displaced the Cao rulers of Wei, **conquered
Shu Han in 263 CE**, proclaimed the **Jin dynasty in 265**, and **reunified China by taking Wu in
280 CE.**

## Demographic Catastrophe

Major & Cook underline the human cost with the census record: the Han census of **2 CE** counted
**~57.7 million people in 12.4 million households**, but the Jin census of **280 CE** counted
**only ~16 million** — stark evidence of the mortality, displacement, and registration collapse of
the Eastern Han's fall and the Three Kingdoms wars.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

`caused_by:` [[han-dynasty|the structural failure of the late Eastern Han]] — child-emperors
manipulated by **consort clans and eunuchs**, fiscal decay, and the loss of central military
control to provincial warlords. `produced:` not reunified stability but, after the brief Jin
success, **four centuries of division** (the Northern and Southern Dynasties). `enabled:` the
deepening of **[[buddhism|Buddhism]]** and the rise of **organized [[daoism|Daoism]]** (the
Celestial Masters), which filled the vacuum left by the discredited Han imperial-Confucian order —
part of the formation of the wider [[east-asian-cultural-sphere|East Asian cultural sphere]].

## Historiography

The period's enduring image comes less from the histories than from the **14th-century novel
*Romance of the Three Kingdoms* (*Sanguo yanyi*)** by Luo Guanzhong, which canonized a heroic
revision — **Liu Bei** as tragic legitimate heir, **Zhuge Liang** as sage-strategist, **Cao Cao**
as brilliant villain. Major & Cook note it "continues to be a source of inspiration to modern
Chinese leaders" and a perennial font of film and television — a case study in how literary
memory overwrites the historical record.
