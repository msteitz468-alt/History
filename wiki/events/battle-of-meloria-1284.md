# The Battle of Meloria (6 August 1284)

At **Meloria**, off Porto Pisano, the fleet of **[[republic-of-genoa|Genoa]]** annihilated
that of **Pisa** — "the final disaster for Pisa." [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (John H.
Pryor, Ch. 14a) treats it as both the symptom and the decisive moment of Pisa's fall from
first-rank Mediterranean power.

## Narrative

The decades-long Genoese–Pisan struggle for the western Mediterranean — over Corsica, Sardinia
and Sicily — came to a head when the Corsican judge Sinucello of Cinarca rebelled against Genoa
and called on Pisa. Both republics mobilised everything: ninety-eight Genoese galleys met
seventy-four Pisan ones. The Genoese admirals **Oberto Doria** and **Benedetto Zaccaria** won a
crushing victory by superior tactics; fewer than twenty Pisan galleys escaped. Pisa's captain
general was killed and one *podestà* captured.

## Consequence Analysis

- **The ruin of Pisan manpower.** Thousands of Pisans were carried off to Genoese dungeons,
  where they "languished for years and from which few returned" — Genoa deliberately refusing
  ransom "to deprive Pisa of her most valuable asset: her manpower." (Among the prisoners was
  **Rustichello of Pisa**, to whom **Marco Polo**, captured at Curzola in 1298, would dictate
  his *Travels*.) It was "the ruin of the old aristocratic Pisan families."
- **Genoese dominance and Pisa's decline.** Pisa lost ground in Sardinia and Corsica; the harsh
  peace cost it Elba's iron mines and a huge indemnity. Though Pisans kept trading (at Ayas and
  Famagusta into the fourteenth century), Meloria was "the critical point at which [Pisa's]
  decline became undeniably manifest." Increasingly Pisans had to sail in others' ships, and the
  **Catalans** rose to become the second Latin trading power of the western Mediterranean.

The internal fallout was severe: a brief Guelf *signoria* under **Ugolino della Gherardesca**
gave way to a coup, and Ugolino was starved to death in a tower — an episode immortalised in
Dante's *Commedia*.
