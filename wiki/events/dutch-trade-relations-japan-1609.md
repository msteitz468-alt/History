# Establishment of Dutch Trade Relations with Japan (1609)

In 1609 the Dutch ship *Brack*, under Captain Spex, arrived at Hirado and Spex was received by
[[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] at Sumpu, initiating the commercial relationship between the Netherlands
and Japan that would last until 1853 — and that would be the sole European window into Japan
during the [[sakoku]] period after 1639. Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Narrative

Of the Dutchmen who had arrived aboard the *Liefde* in 1600, several were taken into service by
Matsuura Shigenobu of Hirado to make guns. In 1605 Captain Jan Quaeckernaeck and supercargo
Melchior van Santvoort obtained permission to leave Japan. Adams wished to accompany them but
Ieyasu refused. Quaeckernaeck was later killed in a fight with the Spaniards; Santvoort reached
Patani (on the Malay Peninsula) and sent a letter back to Japan carrying an invitation from Ieyasu
to Dutch merchants to come to Japan. Matsuura Shigenobu had also provided the junk that allowed
the Dutch to leave, and was rewarded with their return to *his* port.

In the seventh month of 1609 Spex was received by Ieyasu at Sumpu, presenting a letter from the
Stadtholder of the Netherlands with gifts of goblets, silk, lead, and ivory. Ieyasu replied with
a letter to "His Highness the Lord of Holland" (Maurice of Nassau) and simultaneously issued four
copies of a trading license to the four Dutch officers:

> "When Dutch ships come to Japan they are hereby authorized to enter any harbor they like, and
> according to this ordinance they may go wherever they like without let or hindrance. Let none
> disregard this. So be it."

This was effectively a red-seal (*shuinsen*) patent extended to a foreign power rather than a
Japanese merchant or daimyo.

**Portuguese and Spanish reaction.** They immediately accused the Dutch of being "an undesirable
race of pirates." Ieyasu took no notice, relying on [[adams-william|Adams]] for impartial
information on European affairs.

**Dutch factory at Hirado.** The Hollanders established their factory in Hirado despite Ieyasu's
preference for Uraga (closer to Edo). Hendrik Brouwer, who arrived in 1612 as factory chief with
a second letter from Maurice of Nassau, acknowledged Uraga's commercial advantages but explained
the Dutch had already invested heavily in Hirado buildings and goodwill with Matsuura. The Dutch
letter explicitly attacked the Iberian missionaries: "the intention of the Padres is to convert
all Japan to their faith by degrees, and as they hate other religions there is sure to be a
struggle between the sects and a great disturbance in the country."

**Commercial activities.** By 1613 Brouwer was recruiting Japanese laborers for the Dutch East
Indies — "Japanese are clever and their wages are low. They only need rice and salt fish to live
on" — sending sixty-eight workers including carpenters, smiths, plasterers, and soldiers, with
the shogun's written permit for any Japanese who wished to go abroad.

## Consequence Analysis

The 1609 agreement established the pattern that would outlast all other European presences in
Japan. When the [[sakoku]] edicts expelled the Portuguese (1639) and denied re-entry to Japanese
overseas, the Dutch — who had helped suppress the Shimabara Rebellion — were confined to the
artificial island of Deshima at Nagasaki, the single remaining European commercial conduit. This
arrangement persisted until Commodore Perry's arrival in 1853.

## Historiography

The Dutch-Japanese relationship is documented through VOC (Dutch East India Company) records and
the surviving Hirado factory correspondence, including Brouwer's letters to the Governor of the
Dutch East Indies. Sadler draws on these for commercial and diplomatic detail. The 1609 moment is
universally regarded in Tokugawa historiography as the pivot that began Protestant trade's
advantage over Catholic in Japan.
