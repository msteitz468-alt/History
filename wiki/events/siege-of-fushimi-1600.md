## Narrative

At the opening of the 1600 Sekigahara campaign, [[actors/tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]] entrusted Fushimi Castle — the last fortress built by Toyotomi Hideyoshi, comprising a main keep and five subsidiary forts — to his veteran companion [[actors/torii-mototada|Torii Mototada]], with a garrison of roughly 1,800 men, in what both understood as a deliberate delaying sacrifice. From the eighteenth to the twenty-eighth day of the seventh month (per the traditional Japanese calendar), the Western coalition army — including contingents under Shimazu Yoshihiro, Kobayakawa Hideaki, and [[actors/ukita-hideie|Ukita Hideie]], with [[actors/ishida-mitsunari|Ishida Mitsunari]] personally directing the siege — made little headway against the defense.

The castle fell only through betrayal: a Tokugawa retainer holding one of the subsidiary towers was blackmailed into treachery by threats against his family, set fire to his position, and opened a breach. In the desperate fighting that followed, most of the garrison's captains died fighting or by suicide; Mototada himself, reduced to roughly ten men after five counterattacks, took his own life when a final assault broke through, and some 350 men fell with him.

## Causal Analysis

The siege was a direct consequence (preceded_by) of the breakdown of the post-Hideyoshi regency into open war between the Eastern coalition under Ieyasu and the Western coalition operationally directed by Ishida Mitsunari; Mototada's defense was a deliberate strategic sacrifice ordered by Ieyasu to buy time while he marched his main army east.

## Consequence Analysis

Though a tactical defeat, the defense inflicted disproportionate losses (roughly 3,000 Western dead against 1,800 defenders) and, together with the roughly contemporaneous defenses of Tanabe and Otsu, held substantial Western forces away from the decisive field at Sekigahara, contributing materially to the Eastern victory there.

## Historiography

The principal detailed account in the wiki's collection derives from A.L. Sadler's narrative history of the Sekigahara campaign, which treats the siege as a canonical episode of loyal self-sacrifice in Japanese historical memory while also assessing its concrete strategic value ("a victory of doubtful value" for the Western side, given the disproportionate casualties and the time bought for Ieyasu).
