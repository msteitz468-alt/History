# Bronze Age Collapse

The Bronze Age Collapse is the roughly half-century interval, conventionally
dated **c. 1200–1150 BCE**, in which the integrated palace-and-empire system of
the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean disintegrated. Within a generation or
two the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]] ceased to exist, the
[[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] palace states of mainland Greece were burned
and abandoned, the great Syrian emporium of [[ugarit|Ugarit]] was destroyed and
never reoccupied, Cyprus and coastal Anatolia were ravaged, and
[[new-kingdom-egypt|Egypt]] — though it survived — emerged permanently diminished.
This is the standing **transition page** between the [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze
Age]] (period 10) and the [[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]] (period 12), with the
collapse itself constituting period 11.

> **Chronology note.** CAH II.2 uses an older "high" chronology placing the
> climactic events roughly 1200–1190 BCE; modern conventional dating puts
> [[ramesses-iii|Ramesses III]]'s great land-and-sea battle against the Sea Peoples
> in his Year 8, c. **1177 BCE**, which has become the conventional shorthand date
> for the collapse. Dates here follow the conventional system with the volume's
> higher figures noted where they differ.

## Narrative

In the space of about forty years the following centres were destroyed,
abandoned, or radically contracted:

- **Anatolia**: [[hittites|Ḫattuša]], the Hittite capital, was burned and
  abandoned; the empire fragmented into the [[unknown|Neo-Hittite]] successor
  states of northern Syria and the southeast Anatolian plateau. The royal line
  vanishes from the record after Šuppiluliuma II.
- **Syria–Levant**: [[ugarit|Ugarit]] was sacked and permanently abandoned, its
  last king Ammurapi appealing in vain for help; its destruction is fixed by the
  latest tablets, several of which describe enemy ships off the coast. Carchemish,
  Emar, Alalakh, and a string of Levantine towns were destroyed in the same
  horizon.
- **Cyprus** ([[cyprus|Alašiya]]): Enkomi, Kition, and Sinda show destruction and
  reoccupation layers; the island was both a victim and, in its reconstruction, a
  conduit of Aegean settlement.
- **The Aegean**: the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] palaces at
  [[mycenae|Mycenae]], Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes, Gla, Iolcus, and the Menelaion were
  destroyed c. 1200 (LH IIIB/IIIC transition). Pylos burned and was never rebuilt;
  its final Linear B tablets record emergency dispositions of "watchers" guarding
  the coast. Literacy in Linear B disappeared completely.
- **Egypt**: alone among the great powers, Egypt repelled the attackers — first
  under [[merneptah|Merneptah]] (Year 5, against a Libyan-led coalition including
  Sea Peoples, c. 1208 BCE) and decisively under [[ramesses-iii|Ramesses III]]
  (Year 8 land and sea battles in the Delta, c. 1177 BCE, recorded at Medinet
  Habu) — but lost its Asiatic empire and entered a long economic decline.

The destructions were not simultaneous to the year, but they cluster tightly
enough that contemporaries across several archives register a shared emergency:
grain shortages, appeals for relief, reports of hostile ships, and the movement
of displaced peoples by land and sea.

## Causal Analysis

CAH II.2 (chapters XXVII–XXVIII) frames the collapse primarily around the
[[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] — coalitions of displaced raiders and migrants named
in the Egyptian records (Peleset, Tjekker, Sherden, Shekelesh, Denyen, Weshesh,
and others) — and the migrations that both produced and followed their movements.
Modern scholarship treats the Sea Peoples as one symptom within a broader
**systems collapse**, in which several stresses reinforced one another:

- `caused_by:` [[sea-peoples|Sea Peoples]] — raiding and migration that physically
  destroyed coastal centres and severed the maritime trade routes the palace
  economies depended on. Whether cause or symptom is itself debated; the volume
  leans toward them as a prime mover, modern work toward them as a consequence of
  prior breakdown.
- `contributed_to:` drought, crop failure, and famine — attested directly in
  Hittite and Ugaritic correspondence (urgent Egyptian and Ugaritic grain
  shipments to a starving Ḫatti) and supported by later palaeoclimatic evidence
  for a multi-decade dry phase.
- `contributed_to:` the structural fragility of the
  [[mycenaean-civilization|redistributive palace economy]] — highly centralised,
  literate-bureaucratic systems whose collapse erased the administrative class and
  the very script, leaving no institutions to rebuild around.
- `contributed_to:` earthquakes — a seismic "storm" along the Aegean and Anatolian
  fault systems has been argued for several destruction layers (Mycenae, Tiryns,
  Midea), though it cannot account for the synchronised pan-regional pattern alone.
- `enabled:` the interdependence of the Late Bronze Age "international system" —
  the very connectivity (tin and copper supply chains, royal gift-exchange, treaty
  networks documented in the [[amarna-letters|Amarna]] generation) that made the
  system prosperous also made it brittle: the failure of any major node propagated
  to the others.

The causal weighting is a genuine scholarly controversy; see
**Historiography** below. This page asserts no single prime cause.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the [[early-iron-age|Early Iron Age]] — the disappearance of the
  palace systems opened the way to iron metallurgy (partly a response to disrupted
  long-distance tin supply), smaller territorial kingdoms, and alphabetic literacy.
- `produced:` the [[philistines|Philistine]] settlement of the southern Levantine
  coast (the Peleret/Peleset of the Egyptian texts), with their distinctive
  Aegean-derived material culture — one of the clearest archaeological fingerprints
  of the migrations.
- `produced:` the [[greek-dark-age|Greek Dark Age]] — depopulation, the loss of
  writing, monumental building, and figural art in the Aegean, and the migrations
  that the later Greeks remembered as the "return of the Herakleidai" / Dorian
  invasion and the Ionian settlement of Asia Minor.
- `enabled:` the rise of [[assyria-middle|Assyria]] and later the Neo-Hittite and
  Aramaean states into the power vacuum left in Syria and Mesopotamia; the
  political map of the Early Iron Age Near East is drawn over the ruins of the
  Bronze Age empires.
- `produced:` a permanent contraction of Egyptian power; the
  [[new-kingdom-egypt|New Kingdom]] limped through the later Ramessides to the end
  of the 20th Dynasty and the divided "Third Intermediate Period."

## Historiography

This is one of the standing controversies named in the project framework
("Causes of the Bronze Age Collapse").

- **Source quality.** Egyptian royal inscriptions (Medinet Habu, the Merneptah
  "Israel" stele, Papyrus Harris I) are propagandistic but contemporary and
  detailed on the Sea Peoples. The Ugaritic and Hittite archives give a
  contemporary view of the final crisis (grain shortage, ships off the coast) but
  end abruptly with the destructions. The Aegean has no contemporary narrative —
  only archaeology and the much later, problematic memory preserved in
  [[unknown|Homer]] and Greek legend. CAH II.2 is structurally Egyptocentric on
  the collapse because that is where the texts survive.
- **The "Sea Peoples" debate.** The volume (chapters XXVII–XXVIII) treats the Sea
  Peoples as a prime cause — a wave of migration and raiding that toppled the
  empires. Later scholarship (Drews, *The End of the Bronze Age*, 1993; and the
  "systems collapse" model after Renfrew) reverses or complicates this: the Sea
  Peoples become a *symptom* of prior breakdown — population displacement caused by
  the same drought, famine, and economic failure that independently undermined the
  palaces. Some recent work questions whether a coherent "Sea Peoples" phenomenon
  existed at all, versus an Egyptian rhetorical grouping of diverse opportunistic
  raiders.
- **Climate.** Palaeoclimatic research since the 1990s (pollen cores, isotope
  records from the Levant, Anatolia, and the Aegean) has strengthened the case for
  a severe multi-decade drought c. 1250–1100 BCE, lending the famine attested in
  the Hittite/Ugaritic letters a regional cause. This is the major post-volume
  revision: climate was barely available to the 1975 editors as hard evidence.
- **Single vs. multiple causation.** The dominant current view rejects any single
  cause (invasion, earthquake, drought, internal revolt, ironworking, system
  failure) in favour of a multi-causal cascade in which interlocking stresses on
  an over-connected, over-centralised system produced a runaway collapse. The
  question of *weighting* — which stress was decisive — remains open.
- **Collection coverage.** The Bibliotheca Alexandrina is strong on the
  Greco-Roman and Near Eastern textual side of this period and on the archaeology
  of the destructions (CAH II.2 itself, plus the prehistoric/archaeological
  section). It is weaker on the post-1990 climate-science literature and on the
  most recent revisionist syntheses, which postdate most of the collection.
