## Narrative

On the night of September 13, 1912, as Emperor Meiji's funeral cortege departed the Imperial
Palace for the Aoyama parade grounds, General Nogi Maresuke (1849–1912) and his wife Shizuko
committed *junshi* — ritual suicide following one's lord — at their Tokyo residence. The act
occurred simultaneously with the commencement of the funeral ceremony; word of the suicides
reached the Aoyama hall while the service was still in progress.

Nogi had prepared the scene carefully: a small desk beside a window facing the palace direction,
covered with white cloth, with a portrait of the late emperor and an offering of sakaki above it.
Both Nogi and his wife left poems of mourning. Nogi's farewell poem (*jisei*):

> *utsushiyo wo / kamisarimashishi / ōkami no / mi ato shitaite / ware wa yuku nari*
> ("In longing for / The great god who, as a god, / Has departed from / This transient world
> of ours, / I shall follow his traces.")

Nogi slit his abdomen with his military sword and then stabbed his throat. His wife stabbed her
heart with a dagger.

### Nogi's Stated Reasons

His farewell note explained three layers of obligation:
1. The loss of a regimental flag during the Satsuma Rebellion (1877) had caused unrelenting shame;
   he had courted death in battle ever since as expiation, without success.
2. During the Russo-Japanese War, tens of thousands of men — including his own two sons — had
   died in the attacks he ordered at Port Arthur; he had been "deeply ashamed about the loss of
   His Majesty's 'children.'"
3. Emperor Meiji, rather than blaming him, had appointed him to head the Gakushū-in; Nogi felt
   "the depth of the emperor's solicitude" and regretted he had "so little time left to repay
   this kindness."

This was not entirely spontaneous: years earlier, on his triumphal return from the Russo-Japanese
War, Nogi had asked the emperor's permission to commit seppuku. The emperor had refused, saying
"If you insist on killing yourself, let it be after I have departed this world." Nogi had
understood this as a conditional permission; the night of the funeral was his appointed hour.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[death-of-emperor-meiji-1912]]` — The direct trigger: the emperor's death on
  July 30, 1912 and the funeral ceremony of September 13 constituted the moment Nogi had held
  himself in reserve for.
- `caused_by: samurai junshi tradition` — Nogi drew explicitly on the samurai tradition of
  following one's lord in death, a practice that had been formally prohibited since 1782 (under
  the Laws for the Military Houses) but remained culturally potent.
- `caused_by: meiji military culture` — The culture of the Imperial Japanese Army valorized
  death in service to the emperor and the willingness to make the ultimate demonstration of loyalty.
- `contributed_to: nogi port arthur guilt` — His personal shame over Port Arthur casualties,
  including his two sons, had accumulated for years as unfulfilled debt.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: nogi cult imperial loyalty` — Within days of the suicide, Nogi was elevated to
  near-divine status as the incarnation of loyalty. By September 19 the *Tokyo Nichinichi Shimbun*
  compared him to Kusunoki Masashige; thereafter he was "a legendary hero whom it was impossible
  to criticize." He was later enshrined.
- `produced: mori ogai junshi literature` — Mori Ōgai wrote *Okitsu Yagoemon no isho* (The
  Last Testament of Okitsu Yagoemon) within four days, followed by *Abe no Ichizoku*, both
  exploring the theme of *junshi* with ambivalence that deepened over time.
- `enabled: natsume soseki kokoro 1914` — Sōseki's novel *Kokoro* (1914) drew directly on
  the cultural shock of Nogi's death; the novel's pivotal suicide is explicitly linked to the
  Meiji death.
- `produced: taisho princes without nogi` — Emperor Meiji had specifically appointed Nogi
  as president of Gakushū-in so that the imperial grandsons (including the future Shōwa emperor)
  would benefit from "a man of absolute rectitude." Nogi's death deprived them of this influence,
  a loss the court explicitly lamented.

## Reception

The immediate reaction was sharply divided:

**Admirers**: "Should the people worship him as a god? Yes, if he is not worshiped, who should
be worshiped? … Truly General Nogi was a god." (Kuroiwa Ruikō, journalist, September 16, 1912).
Those in the funeral hall were "stunned by his display of nobility and unswerving loyalty."

**Critics**:
- Katō Hiroyuki (last member of the Meirokusha enlightenment circle): called it an anachronism;
  asked why Nogi had not offered his loyalty to the new emperor.
- Mushakōji Saneatsu (Shirakaba school, former Gakushū-in student): "an act that could be
  praised only by the warped intelligence of men who have been nurtured on thought shaped by a
  warped age through a misuse of nature." Contrasted it with Van Gogh's suicide, which "revealed
  the nature of humanity."
- Shiga Naoya (diary, September 14): "What an idiot!" — and the next day: "a surrender to
  temptation."
- Nagai Ussai (kanshi poet, "Loyalty"): Sarcastic verse asking why a "mighty general" would
  "behave like a eunuch or a priest's concubine."
- Some newspapers: criticized him for failing to receive Prince Arthur of Connaught (a state
  guest); others for not serving the new emperor.

The military establishment attempted to suppress Nogi's stated motivation, attributing the
suicide to mental derangement — fearing other officers might emulate it.

## Historical Significance

Nogi's *junshi* was the moment at which Meiji-era martial culture began its transition from
lived reality to canonized myth. The act was simultaneously: (1) legally prohibited (by the
1782 Laws for the Military Houses); (2) culturally resonant to a generation raised on samurai
ethics; (3) indigestible to the modernist and humanist writers of the Shirakaba school; and
(4) politically appropriable by the emerging military nationalist culture that would dominate
Japan in the 1930s.

The Nogi cult became one of the primary vehicles through which prewar military ideology
constructed the ideal of self-sacrifice for the emperor. The *junshi* thus connects the
end of Meiji directly to the culture of the Pacific War.

## Historiography

Donald Keene's account draws on an unusually rich contemporary print record — newspaper reportage, private diaries (Shiga Naoya's), and near-immediate literary responses (Mori Ōgai, and later Natsume Sōseki) — making this one of the best-documented individual acts of the late Meiji period. That very immediacy, however, means the competing frames (heroic loyalty versus anachronistic barbarism) were fixed within days and have shaped memory of the event ever since. Nogi's suicide note is a first-person statement of motive but was also a carefully composed public document, and the military establishment's attempt to suppress or reframe it as mental derangement shows contested official memory-management even at the time. Scholars have since debated how far the *junshi* reflects genuine continuity with samurai-era practice as against a self-consciously constructed act of political theater aimed at the incoming Taishō era and the ascendant military-nationalist culture; Keene situates it as a hinge moment without fully resolving that tension.
