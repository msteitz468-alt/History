# The Dorian Invasion of the Peloponnese

The seizure of the [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] Peloponnese by the
[[dorians|Dorians]] under their Heraclid kings — remembered by the Greeks as the
**"Return of the Heracleidae"** (the descendants of Heracles reclaiming an ancestral
right) — and the cluster of migrations that accompanied it. It is the legendary
counterpart of the archaeological [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|collapse]] of the
Mycenaean world. [[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]] (Ch. XXXVI, Hammond)
reconstructs it almost entirely from the literary tradition — Thucydides (I.12),
Herodotus, Pausanias, and Strabo — which, Hammond argues, rests on genuine
**Mycenaean** folk-memory transmitted through local cults and royal genealogies.

## Narrative

After a first Heraclid attack a century earlier — when **Hyllus** advanced to the
Isthmus and was killed by **Echemus of Arcadia** (~1220 BCE), and the Heracleidae
swore not to return for a hundred years — the Dorians came again. According to
Thucydides the **Dorians with the Heracleidae took the Peloponnese eighty years after
the fall of Troy**; on Hammond's chronology (Troy ~1200) this is **c. 1120 BCE**.

- **The crossing.** Barred from the Isthmus by the Mycenaeans, the Dorians did **not**
  come overland but **crossed the Gulf of Corinth by ship**, from Naupactus to
  **Rhium** in the northwest Peloponnese — guided by **Oxylus the Aetolian**, who took
  Elis as his reward. (Where the Dorians learned seamanship — in the Gulf of Ambracia
  and Thesprotia — is itself a sign of their northwestern origin.)
- **The conquest of the citadels.** The three Heraclid commanders, the sons of
  Aristomachus, divided the spoils: **Temenus** took the **Argolid** (the chief seat
  of Mycenaean power), **Cresphontes** took **Messenia** (headquarters at
  Stenyclarus), and the twin sons of **Aristodemus** (Eurysthenes and Procles) took
  **Laconia**, defeating Tisamenus son of Orestes and the Achaeans. **Arcadia**,
  secured by a marriage alliance, stayed independent (and kept its archaic East-Greek
  dialect). The palace-citadels fell "one by one in isolation."
- **The overseas thrust.** Almost at once Dorian bands went by sea to **Melos and
  Thera** (Thucydides dates the Spartan seizure of Melos to ~1116 BCE), and on to
  **Crete, Rhodes, Cos, and the Dodecanese** — the same southerly route across the
  Aegean by which Dorian settlement reached Asia Minor (see
  [[ionian-migration-1050bce|the migrations to Asia Minor]]).
- **Consolidation.** The capture of the citadels was only the first stage; a long
  period of subduing the countryside, putting down revolts (Messenia rebelled and was
  restored only with Spartan and Argive help; Amyclae held out for generations), and
  internal Dorian war followed. The last mainland acquisition was the **Megarid**,
  annexed after the Dorians failed to take **Athens** (where their king Codrus fell)
  — securing a corridor to their kin in Boeotia and control of the Isthmus.

## The accompanying migrations

Hammond, following Thucydides, treats the invasion as the central wave of a longer
turbulence. Twenty years earlier the **Thessalians** had crossed from Thesprotia and
seized the plain that took their name (driving out Boeotians); the **Boeotians**
then occupied Cadmeis (renamed Boeotia, sixty years after Troy); and a train of
lesser tribes — Aenianes, Dolopes, Dryopes — was pushed ahead "like driftwood." The
ultimate impetus, Hammond argues, was pressure from the **Briges** (the European
Phryges) and other "Lausitz" peoples moving down through Macedonia and Epirus
(c. 1200–1150 BCE) — see [[phrygians|the Phrygians]].

### The Balkan homeland (CAH III.1, Ch. 15)

Hammond's later treatment of [[epirus|Illyris, Epirus and Macedonia]] in
[[boardman-cah-v3p1-1982|CAH Vol. III, Part 1]] fills in the **northern homeland** this
narrative presupposes. The South-West Balkans were a world of **transhumant shepherd
peoples** burying their dead under **tumuli** — a rite Hammond connects directly to the
heroic burials of the *Iliad* and treats as the diagnostic marker of the migrating
northern Greeks. The Dorians' seamanship was learned "in the Gulf of Ambracia and
Thesprotia," and the Thessalians crossed into Thessaly **from Thesprotia** — both
pointing to an [[epirus|Epirote]] staging-ground. The same chapter shows the
[[phrygians|Brygian/Phrygian]] pressure and the later [[illyrians|Illyrian]] expansion
that churned this region, reinforcing the picture of the Greek migrations as one
outflow from a turbulent Balkan reservoir.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the [[dorians|Dorian]] descent from the northwest, themselves driven
  (`caused_by:`) by overpopulation and **pressure from Balkan peoples** (the
  Briges/Lausitz movement into Macedonia and Epirus).
- `contributed_to:` the **decline of [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean power]]** —
  Thucydides' picture of post-Trojan-War Greece as a land of *stasis* and
  displacement, confirmed by the destruction layers at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Gla,
  and Crisa — which left the citadels vulnerable.
- The relation to the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|Bronze Age Collapse]] is the
  crux: the invasion is the **literary tradition's** account of the same upheaval the
  archaeology registers as the destruction of the palaces. Whether the Dorians
  *caused* the collapse or merely *occupied* the ground it cleared is unresolved.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **West-Greek (Doric) dialect map** of the historic Peloponnese,
  Crete, and the southeastern Aegean, and the rise of the Dorian states above all
  **Sparta**.
- `produced:` the [[ionian-migration-1050bce|Aeolian and Ionian migrations]] — the
  flight of dispossessed Mycenaean populations (the Neleidae of Pylos to Athens, the
  Ionians of Achaea) that carried Greek settlement and the Homeric tradition to Asia
  Minor.
- `enabled:` the [[greek-dark-age|Greek Dark Age]] and, in the long run, the
  conditions out of which the **polis** developed.

## Historiography

The invasion is the most fully reconstructed — and most disputed — event of the
Greek Dark Age. CAH II.2's confidence rests on Hammond's argument that the
genealogies (Tisamenus, Penthilus, Melanthus, Temenus all of one generation)
synchronize across independent local traditions in a way "no one could have imposed,"
and on the **dialect map**, which demands a West-Greek movement into the Peloponnese
even apart from the legends. Against this stands the **absence of any clear
archaeological Dorian horizon**: the destruction of the palaces and the
sub-Mycenaean/Protogeometric sequence show collapse and continuity, not a visible
conquering people. Much post-1975 scholarship therefore treats the "Dorian invasion"
as a back-projected charter-myth overlaid on a gradual infiltration and internal
collapse. This page records the tradition and Hammond's reconstruction; it does not
adjudicate. The "Great Man vs. structural causation" problem and the dating elasticity
(±50 years between pottery and genealogy) are intrinsic to the question.
