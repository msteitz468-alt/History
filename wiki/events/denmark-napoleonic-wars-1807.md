## Narrative

### Background: The Neutrality Policy and Its Limits

After the armed-neutrality crisis of 1801, Denmark had operated "a consistent policy of neutrality" — refusing to join the war against France while stationing its army in Holstein to guard the southern border (L29251–55). In the spring of 1807 the crown prince (acting as regent for the mentally ill Christian VII) even refrained from fitting out the naval squadron that normally guaranteed neutrality, "in order to avoid provoking Great Britain" (L29255–57).

The Treaty of Tilsit (7 July 1807) destroyed this equilibrium. Napoleon and Tsar Alexander I agreed that "the tsar and Napoleon together agreed to force Denmark and Norway to join the Continental System and take part in the war against Britain" (L29288–90). For Alexander, the benefit was "a free hand to conquer Finland from Sweden" (L29291–92).

### British Pre-emptive Strike: The Bombardment of Copenhagen, 1807

The British cabinet had been monitoring the Danish fleet — "a well-equipped and well-manned force of eighteen ships-of-the-line and sixteen frigates" (L29296–97) — with anxiety throughout the spring of 1807. On 13 July, after receiving intelligence reports that the tsar and Napoleon were preparing a maritime league including Denmark, the cabinet decided to demand either a Danish alliance or the surrender of the fleet as a pledge of neutrality (L29300–06).

In early August Admiral Gambier's fleet blockaded Zealand (Sjælland) to prevent the transfer of troops from Holstein to the capital's defence. Crown Prince Frederik "absolutely refused to countenance Britain's demands," and when British troops landed on Zealand on 16 August, immediately north of Copenhagen, he responded with a declaration of war on Britain (L29320–23).

The British invested Copenhagen by land and sea. Sir Arthur Wellesley scattered the peasant militias; General Cathcart demanded the fleet. The Danes refused and mounted sorties against the British batteries. "On the evening of 2 September the British began three nights of bombardment with mortars and the new Congreve rockets — the first terror bombardment of civilian targets in the history of warfare" (L29329–31). Parts of the capital caught fire; under pressure from the inhabitants the city's commandant capitulated. The terms of capitulation required the delivery of the whole fleet as British property. When the British fleet sailed on 20 October it took "a fully equipped fleet of fifteen ships-of-the-line, fifteen frigates and seven brigs, together with a number of smaller vessels" (L29337–38). A ship-of-the-line in dock and three on the stocks were destroyed.

### The Fontainebleau Alliance and the Onset of War

Having lost its fleet, Denmark had no effective means of resisting Swedish pressure on Norway, and Napoleon was the only power able to guarantee Danish territorial integrity. "Denmark, accordingly, did not really have any choice" (L29356–57). On 31 October 1807 Denmark and France signed the Treaty of Fontainebleau: Napoleon promised military assistance, compensation for the fleet, and a guarantee of territorial integrity; Denmark undertook to participate fully in the Continental System and "together with France and Russia, to declare war on Sweden and force the Swedes to join it" (L29364–66).

Denmark declared war on Sweden on 29 February 1808. However, the invasion of Sweden from Norway depended on a simultaneous French landing in Skåne. A Franco-Spanish auxiliary corps under Marshal Bernadotte moved into Jutland and over to Funen and Zealand, but the Danish straits did not freeze over as expected, the British fleet was present in Danish waters, and the Spanish troops rebelled in summer 1808 and were evacuated home by the British (L29411–15).

### The War at Sea and Denmark's Gunboat Strategy

Without a battle fleet, Denmark constructed a large number of gunboats and licensed privateers to impede British Baltic trade through the Danish straits. Vice-Admiral Sir James Saumarez commanded the British Baltic fleet (1808–12) from HMS Victory. The British responded to the gunboat threat by routing their convoys through the Great Belt rather than the Öresund (from 1809), organising large convoys — "typically 150 ships, but from time to time as many as 500" — that sailed eight months of the year (L29465–67). Danish gunboats captured several British merchant ships and ten small men-of-war, but "there is no doubt that the Royal Navy was quite capable of protecting the vital British Baltic trade which Napoleon had ordered to be brought to a standstill" (L29481–83).

Britain occupied the island of Anholt in 1809 and rebuilt its lighthouse as the fortified Fort York; a Danish attempt to recapture it in 1811 was repulsed (L29453–56).

### Geopolitical Realignment, 1809–1813

The Peace of Hamina (September 1809), by which Sweden ceded Finland to Russia, transformed the strategic situation. Russia now had an interest in Sweden acquiring Norway (to compensate for Finland), making Denmark's Russian alliance obsolete. Frederik VI nonetheless allied himself ever more closely to Napoleon. His foreign minister, Baron Rosenkrantz, urged the king to exploit the absence of French troops in Germany in 1812 to break with Napoleon and save the state; the king refused, retaining his belief in Napoleon's invincibility (L29516–25).

Karl Johan of Sweden (formerly Marshal Bernadotte, elected crown prince August 1810) made Norway his primary objective and systematically built the coalition needed to obtain it. In the Treaty of St Petersburg with Russia, Sweden acknowledged the loss of Finland while Russia pledged to support Swedish claims on Norway with military means if necessary (L29091–93). In March 1813 an Anglo-Swedish treaty committed Sweden to sending 30,000 soldiers to the Continent in exchange for British naval and diplomatic support for the invasion of Norway (L29104–06).

Denmark, attempting a last-minute realignment, allowed Danish troops to fight briefly alongside the Russians against the French in northern Germany in spring 1813, but Frederik VI's conditio sine qua non — retaining Norway — was unacceptable to the allies (L29530–40).

### The Endgame: Leipzig and Kiel, 1813–14

In May 1813 Frederik VI sent Crown Prince Christian Frederik to Norway as governor and military commander to strengthen Norwegian loyalty (L29542–44). On 10 July 1813 he bound Denmark to Napoleon by a formal treaty promising a corps of 12,000 men in northern Germany (L29554–58). War was formally resumed in August 1813; on 20 October Napoleon was comprehensively defeated at the Battle of Leipzig.

Karl Johan, as commander-in-chief of the allied northern army, chose to exploit the post-Leipzig confusion to turn against Denmark rather than pursue the French westward. His forces took Lübeck in December; the Danish corps was surrounded in the fortress of Rendsburg; Holstein and Schleswig were occupied; Jutland lay open to invasion; Norway faced famine. "Faced with this situation, Frederik VI capitulated" (L29579–80).

The Treaty of Kiel was concluded on 14 January 1814. Denmark surrendered Norway to Sweden and received Swedish Pomerania in exchange (subsequently transferred to Prussia for Lauenburg and a monetary sum). Norway was defined as mainland Norway; Greenland, the Faroe Islands, and Iceland remained Danish. Denmark's occupied colonies were returned; her navy and captured merchant ships were not, and Britain retained Helgoland.

The actual wording of the Norwegian surrender was formulated by Karl Johan, who "emphasised that Norway was not being integrated with Sweden but had been handed over to the Swedish king as a kingdom in union with Sweden" (L29593–95).

## Causal Analysis

The crisis had two immediate causes operating simultaneously:

- **Strategic (contributed_to):** The Treaty of Tilsit created an unavoidable geopolitical trap for Denmark by making it a target of both French pressure (to join the Continental System) and British pre-emption.
- **Causal (caused_by):** The British bombardment of Copenhagen and seizure of the Danish fleet in September 1807 removed the one strategic asset that had kept Denmark nominally independent, forcing the Fontainebleau alliance.

The deeper structural cause was Denmark's geographic position — controlling the entrance to the Baltic — which made it a point of maximum strategic sensitivity for both Britain and Napoleon the moment they entered total economic war (L29279–82). Napoleon's Continental System and Britain's counter-blockade together destroyed Denmark's profitable neutrality.

Frederik VI's "conditio sine qua non" of retaining Norway (L29539–40) drove every subsequent Danish decision and foreclosed any timely realignment with the allies. Denmark remained Napoleon's "most faithful ally right to the bitter end in 1814" not from sentiment but because "a break with Napoleon would result in an immediate occupation of Holstein, Schleswig and Jylland" and the loss of Norway through Swedish invasion (L29380–88).

## Consequence Analysis

The war's consequences were catastrophic and structural:

- **Military:** Denmark permanently lost its battle fleet and its position as a naval power.
- **Economic:** Seven years of British blockade, inflation from unrestricted paper-money issue, and Norwegian famine devastated both economies. "Expectations of Continental markets for Danish industrial products were dashed. Napoleon reserved such markets for French industry" (L29371–72).
- **Territorial:** Denmark lost Norway — roughly half its realm and population — though it retained the North Atlantic possessions.
- **Constitutional (Norway):** The Treaty of Kiel triggered rather than suppressed Norwegian independence movements. Crown Prince Christian Frederik convened the Eidsvold assembly, which on 17 May 1814 approved one of the most radical constitutions of the age, modelled on the French constitution of 1791 with a single-chamber storting as the main political institution (L29177–79). After a brief Swedish military campaign in July 1814, Christian Frederik accepted an armistice, abdicated (10 October), and the storting elected Karl XIII king of Norway under the real leadership of Karl Johan (L29219–25).
- **Regional order:** The Napoleonic Wars "created two states as new political entities" — Norway and Finland — both of which were "influenced less by internal factors than by relations of power in international politics and the aims of rulers to further their own dynastic and political aims" (L29234–37).

## Actors

**Frederik VI of Denmark** — regent from 1784, king from 1808. His absolute priority was retaining Norway; this determination made him Napoleon's most loyal ally and ultimately cost him the kingdom he was trying to preserve.

**Napoleon Bonaparte** — guaranteed Danish territorial integrity by the Fontainebleau treaty but reserved Continental markets for French industry and ultimately could not protect Denmark from the coalition.

**Karl Johan (Jean Baptiste Bernadotte)** — Swedish crown prince from August 1810, former Napoleonic marshal. Methodically built the diplomatic and military coalition needed to deliver Norway to Sweden; commanded the allied northern army at Leipzig; directed the final campaign against Denmark (December 1813–January 1814) and drafted the Kiel terms.

**Crown Prince Christian Frederik of Denmark** — sent to Norway as governor (May 1813) to mobilise resistance; convened the Eidsvold assembly and became Norway's first elected king before accepting armistice terms in August 1814.

**Vice-Admiral Sir James Saumarez** — commanded the British Baltic fleet 1808–12; his successful convoy system through the Danish straits ensured that the Continental System did not sever Britain's Baltic trade.

## Historiography

CHS Vol. II (Feldbæk, ch. 31) treats the 1807 British pre-emptive seizure of the
Danish fleet as the decision that forced an otherwise neutral Denmark–Norway into
Napoleon's camp, setting the chain that ended at the
[[treaty-of-kiel-1814|Treaty of Kiel]]. Danish historiography debates whether
genuine neutrality was ever available after Tilsit, and weighs Frederik VI's
fixation on retaining Norway as the misjudgement that cost him the union.
