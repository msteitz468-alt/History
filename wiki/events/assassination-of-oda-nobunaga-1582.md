## Narrative

On the thirtieth of the fifth month of 1582, Akechi Mitsuhide — one of Oda Nobunaga's senior generals, who had been supervisor of the entertainment for Tokugawa Ieyasu's recent visit to Azuchi — turned his army from its intended campaign against the Mōri and marched on the Honnōji temple in Kyoto where Nobunaga was lodging with a small guard. He set fire to the temple; Nobunaga, surrounded and with no means of escape, committed seppuku. His heir Nobutada was similarly surrounded in his lodgings and also killed himself after a fierce fight.

The news reached Ieyasu at Sakai via Chaya Shirojiro, who was returning on horseback: "This morning Akechi revolted and burst into the Honnōji where His Highness was staying and set fire to it so that he committed seppuku." Ieyasu, with only a small retinue, had to abandon any thought of immediate vengeance and instead flee through Iga back to Mikawa — the [[iga-flight-1582]].

[[toyotomi-hideyoshi]], who was campaigning against Mōri in the west, received news of the revolt, made a rapid truce with Mōri, and marched east at extraordinary speed. He defeated Akechi at the battle of Yamazaki thirteen days after the Honnōji Incident. Akechi's death at the hands of bandits during his flight ended the revolt.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Akechi Mitsuhide's revolt — motivated by personal grievance, political ambition, or both (see Historiography). Sadler records a banquet-humiliation story as one possible trigger.
- `contributed_to:` The structural vulnerability of the Oda system: Nobunaga's subordinates wielded vast military power without institutional checks on their loyalty.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[iga-flight-1582]]` — Ieyasu's dangerous journey through hostile territory back to his base.

## Historiography

The Honnōji Incident is one of the most extensively studied events in Japanese history. The motivation for Akechi's revolt — presented in many Tokugawa-era sources as a personal vendetta (the banquet humiliation story) — is almost certainly a simplification. Modern historians have proposed: pure political ambition; resentment at Nobunaga's treatment of the Ashikaga shogunate; conspiracy with Mōri or other outside parties; even the suggestion (without firm evidence) of Hideyoshi's advance knowledge or instigation. The speed and efficiency of Hideyoshi's response — a truce with Mōri, the rapid march east, the battle of Yamazaki — has fueled speculation that he was better informed than the standard narrative allows. The Honnōji Incident stands as the hinge event of the Sengoku period: without it, the Toyotomi and Tokugawa successions would not have occurred in their historical form.
