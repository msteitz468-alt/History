## Narrative

The political crisis of 1881 was a compound political emergency involving a governmental scandal, a ministerial purge, and the imperial promise of constitutional government. It is "often referred to as the 'political crisis of 1881'" in Keene's account, and it settled for nearly a decade the question of when Japan would have a parliament and what kind.

The crisis had two intertwined strands.

**The Hokkaidō Development Office Scandal.** Kuroda Kiyotaka, the director of the Hokkaidō Development Office, had arranged to sell government assets (factories, mines, and other properties) developed at public expense over eleven years — worth approximately 3 million yen — to a private company owned by Godai Tomoatsu, a former Satsuma official. The price was 300,000 yen, payable over thirty years without interest. When details were published, the discrepancy caused outrage. The fact that both Kuroda and Godai were from Satsuma made the proposed sale appear to be clan-based corruption. Newspapers and advocates of popular rights seized on the story during the emperor's junkō (imperial progress) to the north in the summer of 1881.

**The Ōkuma Memorial.** Simultaneously, Councillor Ōkuma Shigenobu had submitted a secret memorial to the emperor in March 1881 proposing radical constitutional change. The seven-article memorial called for: announcing an immediate timeline for parliament (opening by 1883); cabinet ministers to be drawn from the majority party in parliament; the emperor to rely on elected rather than appointed advisers. The proposal was effectively a call for British-style parliamentary government — far more radical than what Itō Hirobumi or Iwakura Tomomi envisioned. Despite Ōkuma's request for secrecy, the memorial was leaked to Itō, who was enraged and threatened to resign if it was accepted.

**The Resolution.** The Satsuma and Chōshū factions in the cabinet, normally at odds, united against Ōkuma (from Saga, and therefore an outsider). The emperor, prodded by Motoda Nagazane and persuaded by reports of an alleged conspiracy involving Ōkuma, the journalist Fukuzawa Yukichi, and the businessman Iwasaki Yatarō, was prevailed upon to dismiss Ōkuma. Itō Hirobumi and Saigō Tsugumichi were sent to Ōkuma, who "agreed without hesitation." As a quid pro quo, the emperor on October 12 cancelled the Hokkaidō property sale.

To mollify the Freedom and Popular Rights Movement and prevent further agitation, the emperor issued a rescript the same day (October 12, 1881) promising that a parliament would convene in 1890. This was announced "rather too soon for the advocates of gradualism" but offered the government nine years to prepare the constitutional framework on its own terms.

## Causal Analysis

The crisis was `caused_by` the convergence of two pressures: the mounting demand from the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement|Freedom and Popular Rights Movement]] for a concrete timeline for parliamentary government, and the internal government conflict over Ōkuma's radical proposal. It was `contributed_to` by the Hokkaidō scandal, which provided popular momentum and a specific grievance.

The oligarchs' response illustrates the pattern of Meiji-era political management: contain popular pressure through a calibrated concession (the 1890 promise) while removing the internal critic who threatened the oligarchs' control of the constitutional drafting process.

## Consequence Analysis

The crisis had consequences operating at three levels:

1. **Immediate.** Ōkuma was expelled from the government. The property sale was cancelled. The Jiyū-tō was formally constituted in December 1880 (already forming when the crisis peaked in October); Ōkuma subsequently founded the rival Rikken Kaishin-tō in 1882.

2. **Medium-term.** The emperor's rescript set a firm 1890 deadline, ending the indefinite postponement strategy favored by the gradualists. This forced Itō Hirobumi to actually draft a constitution, which he did by traveling to Europe in 1882 to study constitutional models.

3. **Long-term.** The framing of the crisis — a governmental act of corruption met by popular outrage and managed through an imperial promise — established the template for Japanese constitutional politics: the emperor as the source of constitutional authority (not popular sovereignty), with the parliament as a gift bestowed by the throne. The Prussian model that Itō eventually adopted (constitution granted by the sovereign) rather than the British model (constitution arising from popular pressure) was in part a direct response to the memory of 1881, when Ōkuma's British-style proposal had been forcibly rejected.

## Actors

- **[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]** — ultimately decisive; the crisis was resolved by his consent to dismiss Ōkuma and issue the constitutional promise. The emperor expressed personal distaste for most of the politicians involved, reportedly remarking in private of Kuroda: "He is utterly loathsome."
- **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** — spearheaded the coalition against Ōkuma; emerged as the dominant figure in constitutional planning
- **Ōkuma Shigenobu** — author of the radical memorial; his dismissal removed the most progressive voice from the inner government
- **Kuroda Kiyotaka** — central figure in the Hokkaidō scandal; despite involvement, he suffered no lasting disgrace and became prime minister in 1888
- **[[iwakura-tomomi|Iwakura Tomomi]]** — conservative adviser to the emperor; pressed for Ōkuma's removal
- **[[itagaki-taisuke|Itagaki Taisuke]]** — external pressure from the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement|Freedom and Popular Rights Movement]] he led made the October 12 promise politically necessary

## Historiography

Keene describes the political crisis of 1881 as "a peculiarly unsavory example of power politics." The factional intrigue — the coalition of Satsuma and Chōshū men against an outsider, the use of allegations of conspiracy without hard proof, the emperor's reluctant compliance — sits uneasily against the period's more elevated constitutional rhetoric.

Scholars have debated whether the crisis represented principled conservatism (maintaining imperial prerogatives against premature democratization) or naked factional self-interest. Keene's account, grounded in contemporary diary sources (especially Sasaki Takayuki and Motoda Nagazane), suggests the two were not distinguishable in the minds of the actors.

The October 12 rescript promising a 1890 parliament is treated by Keene as a strategic concession made under pressure rather than as a sincere advance toward constitutional government — though its consequences were real regardless of the motivation.
