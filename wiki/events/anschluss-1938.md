# Anschluss (1938)

## Narrative

The Anschluss — the incorporation of Austria into the German Reich — took place in March 1938. It was the first major territorial expansion of the Third Reich and marked the transition from revisionist coups to outright destruction of a sovereign state. For Hitler, it fulfilled a lifelong ideological and personal mission; for the regime, it provided immediate economic and strategic gains and dramatically strengthened Hitler's domestic and international position.

Since boyhood in Linz, Hitler had viewed the German-speaking population of Austria as belonging to a Greater Germany. *Mein Kampf* opened with the demand that 'German-Austria must return to the great German mother-country'. The post-1919 Austrian republic (a remnant of the Habsburg empire with only 7 million inhabitants) had initially favoured union, but Hitler's rise polarized Austrian politics between socialists, Catholic-conservatives, and pan-Germans (now absorbed into the banned Austrian NSDAP).

The July 1936 Austro-German Agreement and Italy's distraction in Abyssinia had already made Austria a German satellite in practice. Economic pressures from the Four-Year Plan made Austrian iron ore, gold reserves, and labour supplies highly attractive. Göring pushed hardest for rapid 'merger' throughout 1937. Hitler had signalled intent at the Hoßbach meeting (5 November 1937).

## The Berchtesgaden Meeting (12 February 1938)

Papen arranged the meeting. Schuschnigg arrived with high hopes of stabilizing Austrian independence. Hitler received him politely on the steps of the Berghof but immediately turned hostile inside: 'Yes, here my ideas mature. But we haven't come together to talk about the beautiful view and the weather.'

For hours Hitler ranted about Austria's 'treason'. 'I have a historic mission... Perhaps I'll appear some time overnight in Vienna; like a spring storm. Then you'll see something.' 

Ribbentrop presented the ultimatum to Guido Schmidt: end restrictions on Nazis, amnesty, Seyß-Inquart as Interior Minister (with police control), Glaise-Horstenau as War Minister, economic integration. Deadline 15 February (to fit Hitler's rescheduled Reichstag speech).

Schuschnigg resisted; Hitler threatened military invasion. Keitel was summoned with theatrical intent ('Nothing. Sit down'). Under pressure, Schuschnigg accepted the core demands, including Seyß-Inquart. The Austrian visitors left depressed and hungry.

Hitler was initially dissatisfied but accepted when Schuschnigg complied on 15 February. He told Goebbels: 'cannons always speak a good language'.

## Schuschnigg's Plebiscite Gamble and German Reaction

On 9 March Schuschnigg announced a referendum for 13 March on 'a free and German, independent and social, Christian and united Austria'. The wording was designed to produce a 'yes' for independence. Hitler, who had not been forewarned, was incredulous then enraged at the 'dirty trick' that betrayed Berchtesgaden.

The German leadership was unprepared. Ribbentrop was in London. Rapid improvisation followed. Hitler decided on military intervention ('Case Otto') if demands failed. Goebbels and Göring worked on propaganda and ultimatums.

On 10 March ultimatums demanded postponement of the plebiscite for a Saar-style vote, Schuschnigg's resignation, and Seyß-Inquart as Chancellor. Göring drove the pressure from Berlin by telephone. Schuschnigg resigned on 11 March; President Miklas initially refused to appoint Seyß. Göring threatened invasion; Miklas yielded late on 11 March.

At 8.48 p.m. Seyß refused to send a pre-drafted 'request for German help'. Göring told Keppler it did not matter; the telegram was sent anyway at 9.10 p.m. It was irrelevant. At 9.30 p.m. (approximately) Hitler ordered the Wehrmacht to march. Mussolini's acceptance (via Philipp of Hesse) reached Hitler shortly before 10.30 p.m.; Hitler was effusive: 'Please tell Mussolini I will never forget him for it... If he should ever need any help... do or die I shall stick by him.'

## Invasion and Incorporation (12–13 March)

German troops crossed at 5.30 a.m. on 12 March. They were met with flowers and jubilation. Hitler crossed the border at Braunau am Inn (his birthplace) around 4 p.m. on 12 March. Church bells rang; ecstatic crowds (mostly from outside) lined the streets. He did not linger for sentiment; propaganda value dictated the symbolic stop.

Progress was slowed by crowds. He reached Linz after dark. On the town hall balcony, tears on his cheeks, he declared Providence had singled him out: 'I have now fulfilled my mission.' He decided to stay overnight.

On 13 March (the day of the cancelled plebiscite), the decision for immediate full Anschluss crystallized. Stuckart drafted the 'Law for the Reunion of Austria with the German Reich'. The Austrian Ministerial Council accepted it in five minutes. Miklas resigned powers to Seyß. Hitler signed. Austria became a German province (Ostmark).

On 14 March Hitler entered Vienna to delirious crowds. Cardinal Innitzer ordered church bells to peal and swastika banners flown — an extraordinary gesture amid the Church struggle.

On 15 March, in beautiful weather, Hitler addressed an estimated 250,000 in the Heldenplatz: the 'new mission' of the Ostmark as 'bulwark' against 'storms of the east'. He later met Cardinal Innitzer, who with other bishops soon signed a declaration of support against 'godless Bolshevism', adding 'Heil Hitler' in his own hand.

Hitler flew back to Munich on 15 March and addressed the Reichstag on 18 March. New 'elections' (with Austria) on 10 April produced 99.08% (Old Reich) and 99.75% (Austria) 'yes' votes.

## Immediate Consequences

Repression was immediate and ferocious — worse than in Germany in 1933. Himmler and Heydrich arrived 12 March. 10,000–20,000 arrests (Socialists, Communists, Jews, supporters of the old regime). Austrian police records handed to Gestapo.

Jews suffered mob violence: shops plundered, individuals robbed in streets, forced 'cleaning squads' scrubbing pavements under kicks and jeers, 'Perish Judah!' chants. The Daily Telegraph correspondent described a 'witches' sabbath'. Thousands fled toward Czech border (turned back); suicides common.

Göring (who had pressed hardest for economic union) was astonished at the speed of full Anschluss. Hitler told Goebbels: 'First comes now Czechia... And drastically, at the next opportunity.'

## Analysis (per Kershaw)

The Anschluss was improvised at breakneck speed after Schuschnigg's gamble. It blended Hitler's personal mission with Four-Year Plan economic imperatives and Göring's bullying. The western powers again stood aside. The ecstatic reception in Linz and Vienna reinforced Hitler's sense of infallibility and destiny. 'Hitler had brought about the Anschluss by force; in spite of all warnings... his own methods had proved the most direct and successful... Hitler became impervious to the advice of all those who wished him to exercise moderation' (Papen, as cited by Kershaw).

Domestically, the triumph defused the Blomberg–Fritsch scandal overnight. The repression and plunder in Austria demonstrated the radicalizing effect of new 'opportunities'. The Anschluss was the watershed that made the Great Germanic Reich seem achievable in Hitler's lifetime and accelerated the drive toward Czechoslovakia and war.

## Historiography (from source)

Kershaw stresses the mixture of Hitler's ideological drive, economic pressures (Göring/Four-Year Plan office), and improvisation after Schuschnigg's miscalculation. The mass enthusiasm was genuine in significant part (though orchestrated and with terror against opponents). The passivity of Britain and France confirmed Hitler's contempt for the western powers. The event is presented as the point where 'vision' began to overcome calculation, with the radical dynamic now carrying Hitler along.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich in Power* (2005)

Evans (ingested 2026-07-02) adds the persecution aftermath in full. Vienna's
170,000 Jews faced a compressed, telescoped version of five years of Reich
persecution within weeks: forced "scrubbing parties," ~21,000 arrests on the
night of 12–13 March, murders of prominent Jews (Franz Rothenberg, Isidor
Pollack), 7,000 of 33,000 Jewish businesses closed by May and 23,000 more by
August, Burgenland expulsions leaving Jews stranded on a Danube islet.
Eichmann's **Central Agency for Jewish Emigration** (20 August 1938) built
the coerced "assembly-line" emigration model — ~100,000 Austrian Jews out by
May 1939 — that the SD later generalized. Mauthausen was founded near Linz as
the harshest camp in the pre-war system; Göring's Four-Year Plan absorbed the
Austrian economy within two days (100,000 Austrian workers compulsorily
transferred to the Old Reich by 1939). Evans's causal claim: "Without the
Austrian example... it is impossible to understand the upsurge of violence
towards Jews that swept across Germany in the summer of 1938 and culminated
in the pogrom of 9–10 November" ([[kristallnacht-1938]]). Gestapo reports
suggest only a quarter to a third of Viennese voters were genuinely committed
behind the 99.75% plebiscite figure. Source:
[[evans-third-reich-in-power-2005]]

## Historiography: "Opportunist," Not "Master Planner" (Paxton and Hessler)

Paxton and Hessler add background on the years between Dollfuss and the
Anschluss: after [[dollfuss-engelbert|Dollfuss]]'s 1934 murder, his
successor Kurt Schuschnigg balanced the Heimwehr, under Prince Ernst
Rüdiger von Starhemberg, against a resurgent Austrian Nazi Party;
Schuschnigg brought the pro-Anschluss lawyer Arthur Seyss-Inquart into
government in late 1937 in an attempt to divide Nazi sympathizers while
cracking down on illegal Nazi activity. This event is `preceded_by:
[[dollfuss-engelbert]]`'s death, not `caused_by` it — four years separate
the two, and Schuschnigg's own balancing policy, not a direct chain from
Dollfuss's murder, shaped the run-up to 1938.

The contemporary and later assumption that Hitler had long been planning
the invasion is contested. Captured evidence instead suggests Hitler's
decision was a spur-of-the-moment reaction to Schuschnigg's March 9
plebiscite announcement (scheduled for March 13, on the question of
Austrian independence) rather than the execution of a long-prepared plan —
an "opportunist," not a "master planner" (Jürgen Gehl, *Austria, Germany,
and the Anschluss, 1931–38*, 1979). On this reading the event is `caused_by:`
Schuschnigg's plebiscite announcement, `contributed_to`
by Mussolini's acquiescence — Italy's 1934 Brenner Pass mobilization had
previously blocked Anschluss, and its reversal by 1938 removed the one
external check that had constrained Hitler on the Austrian question.
Germany's population reached 80 million with the annexation; contemporaries
framed the popular enthusiasm in Linz and Vienna as, arguably, an exercise
in Wilsonian self-determination. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
