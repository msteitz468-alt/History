## Narrative

The Global Financial Crisis (GFC) — originating in the US subprime mortgage market collapse and spreading through global credit markets in 2007–08 — struck Australia as a sharp growth slowdown but not a recession. "In 2006 gross domestic product (GDP) grew at nearly 5 per cent and in 2007 at about 3 per cent, but in 2008 growth dropped to about 1 per cent. From 2009 annual growth averaged about 2 per cent" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

The Rudd government responded with a major fiscal stimulus package: "$14.7 billion for school buildings, cash handouts costing $12.7 billion, $6.6 billion for public housing, $2.7 billion for home insulation, and a first home buyers' grant — though the size of the package as well as its composition remained controversial" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). Australia was the only one of the world's 34 rich countries "to have experienced 20 years of uninterrupted economic growth" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

The fiscal stimulus's effectiveness remains debated. The Rudd government went into debt to fund it: "net public debt as a proportion of GDP was low compared with the member countries of the OECD" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8). Critics disputed the composition (cash handouts vs. capital works) and the home insulation scheme became a safety and administrative scandal.

## Consequence Analysis

**For the Rudd government**: The stimulus shielded the economy but did not protect Rudd politically. The Resources Super Profits Tax proposal in 2010 — intended to extract a fair share of mining profits for the public — triggered an industry advertising campaign that contributed to his removal by the Labor caucus in June 2010. "Although his economic policies had helped shield Australia from global recession, his popularity with the public and among his close colleagues had plummeted" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

**For household behaviour**: "In the aftermath of the GFC, savings increased sharply and the proportion of households owning shares (34 per cent in 2010) fell. Housing prices also fell, though not sharply" ([[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]], ch. 8).

**For debt politics**: "While government debt fell, household debt rose — though not nearly as rapidly as the value of household assets." Public debt became an index of government competence; both Labor and Coalition vied in promising minimal debt, skewing fiscal policy toward surplus targets regardless of economic conditions.

**For inequality**: The GFC reduced the top 1 per cent's share of national income from about 10 per cent before the GFC to 8.6 per cent. Inequality increased most in Western Australia; the crisis temporarily compressed income distribution at the top.
