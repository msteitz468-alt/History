# Liberian Settler "Rebellion" (1823–1824)

The 1823–24 conflict (often called a "rebellion" by white observers) between African American settlers and white agents of the American Colonization Society (ACS) at the Cape Mesurado settlement (future Monrovia, Liberia). It arose from practical governance failures and deeper contradictions in a project that combined anti-slavery colonization rhetoric with colonial control.

## Narrative

Per Eric Burin in Blaakman et al. (2023), white colonial agents and governors were supposed to lead, but high mortality and departures meant settlers often governed day-to-day. Disputes over land distribution and inadequate supplies made the situation untenable. Settlers protested; the outcome was a negotiated 1824 constitution that accorded the settlers significantly more self-governance rights than Black Americans enjoyed in the United States at the time.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

**Causes:** Structural (racial hierarchy built into ACS governance) + immediate material hardship. The colony was itself an imperial outpost in West Africa, entangled with British and U.S. interests, the slave trade suppression, and racial removal fantasies.

**Consequences:** A pragmatic redefinition of Black freedom within the colonial framework; demonstrated that "colonization" could produce settler self-assertion against the sponsoring society's control. Contributed to the complex legacy of Liberia as both refuge and site of new hierarchies. In the volume's argument, it exemplifies early U.S. imperial projection via non-state and quasi-state actors and the racial contradictions of "benevolent empire" visions (cf. Paul Cuffe and Martin Delany chapters).

## Historiography

This episode is documented mainly through American Colonization Society correspondence and the reports of its white agents — sources that inevitably frame settler resistance as "rebellion" against legitimate authority rather than a contest over self-governance, and that give only filtered access to settler perspectives. Blaakman et al.'s 2023 volume represents part of a broader historiographical turn treating the ACS and Liberia colonization as an early exercise in American extraterritorial and quasi-imperial governance, rather than treating it in isolation as a humanitarian or philanthropic episode as older accounts did. Given the small and fragmentary settler-side documentary record (a young colony with high mortality and irregular record-keeping), much of the detail here rests on a single secondary synthesis; direct settler testimony, the indigenous African perspective on the surrounding land, and Liberian national historiography of the episode are not yet represented in this collection.
