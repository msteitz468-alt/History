# July Plot (20 July 1944 Assassination Attempt on Hitler)

**Event page grounded in Ian Kershaw, *Hitler 1936–1945: Nemesis* (2000), primarily Range 05/06 cache extraction (lines ~22801–34200).**

## Narrative

On 20 July 1944, Colonel Claus Schenk Graf von Stauffenberg planted a time-bomb in his briefcase under the oaken conference table at Hitler’s midday military briefing in the wooden barracks at the Wolf’s Lair (Wolfsschanze) near Rastenburg in East Prussia. The briefing began unusually early at 12.30 p.m. Fifteen minutes later the bomb exploded.

Stauffenberg, chief of staff to Colonel-General Friedrich Fromm (Commander-in-Chief of the reserve army), had attended critical conferences on mobilizing the 'home army' (Heimatheer) at the Berghof on 6 and 11 July and at the Wolf’s Lair the day after Hitler’s return on 9 July. The question of new divisions was again on the agenda for the 20 July conference. Stauffenberg used the access to place the device.

The plot had a lengthy prehistory. Colonel (later Major-General) Henning von Tresckow, a driving force since the Stalingrad crisis, had revitalized resistance thinking at Army Group Centre from late 1942. Earlier attempts included a plane bomb on 13 March 1943 (fuse failed in intense cold) and Gersdorff’s suicide attempt with a ten-minute fuse at the Heroes’ Memorial Day exhibition in Berlin on 21 March 1943 (Hitler 'raced through the exhibition... and was outside within two minutes'). The Swabian joiner Georg Elser had acted alone in 1939 at the Bürgerbräukeller. Civilian-military networks (Goerdeler group, Kreisau Circle around Helmuth James Graf von Moltke and Peter Graf Yorck von Wartenburg) had formed but lacked arms or direct access; the army was essential for any strike.

By summer 1944 the conspirators recognized the moment for altering the war’s outcome had passed. Tresckow: 'It’s not a matter any more of the practical aim, but of showing the world and history that the German resistance movement at risk of life has dared the decisive stroke. Everything else is a matter of indifference alongside that.' Stauffenberg (July 1944): 'It is now time that something was done. But the man who has the courage to do something must do it in the knowledge that he will go down in German history as a traitor. If he does not do it, however, he will be a traitor to his own conscience.'

Hitler survived with relatively minor injuries. The plot’s follow-on 'Valkyrie' coup plan in Berlin and elsewhere was thwarted. Hitler’s immediate public response (21 July): 'A tiny clique of ambitious, unconscionable, and at the same time criminal, stupid officers has forged a plot to eliminate me and at the same time to eradicate with me the staff practically of the German armed forces’ leadership.'

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` cumulative military disasters (Stalingrad encirclement and destruction after Romanian collapse; loss of Ukraine and Crimea; Army Group Centre catastrophe in late June 1944 with 'colossal' losses; Normandy consolidation) combined with Hitler’s unbending 'stand fast' orders, scapegoating of generals, and refusal of any capitulation or negotiated peace.

`contributed_to:` ethical/moral revulsion at Nazi crimes (especially in east); recognition that Hitler was the chief obstacle to ending the war; generational shift (younger officers like Stauffenberg stepping in where field-marshals would not); 'last chance' before total destruction.

`preceded_by:` earlier abortive plots (1938 Sudeten, 1939–40, 1943 attempts); Abwehr arrests (Oster, Dohnanyi 1943; Canaris house arrest 1944); 'Prussian field-marshals do not mutiny' (Manstein); wavering by Kluge.

The regime’s structure (polycracy + charismatic authority) and the 'working towards the Führer' dynamic that persisted in defeat made coordinated internal overthrow exceptionally difficult until military collapse was advanced.

## Consequence Analysis

Immediate: failed assassination and coup; savage purge of army leadership and civilians (executions, forced suicides including Tresckow); intensified terror and repression on the home front.

Longer-term: further radicalization of the regime in its death throes; Hitler’s command purges (Busch, Rundstedt, Zeitzler dismissed or sidelined in preceding weeks) accelerated; no alteration to 'no capitulation' stance or 'Providence'/miracle hopes. Demonstrated existence of 'another Germany' to Allies and posterity but came too late to split the coalition or shorten the war.

`produced:` deeper isolation of Hitler; 'collective suicide' dynamic of the regime 'like a mortally wounded wild beast at bay'.

`enabled:` (by failure) prolongation of fighting and maximized death/destruction until May 1945.

## Actors

- **Primary**: Stauffenberg (planner and executor of the strike); Hitler (target and survivor); Tresckow (ideological and operational driving force from 1942).
- **Secondary**: Fromm (Stauffenberg’s superior); Kluge (wavering earlier contact, replaced Rundstedt); Model (brought in as 'Hitler’s fireman'); Guderian (appointed Chief of General Staff 21 July after Zeitzler breakdown).

Linked: [[actors/adolf-hitler]], resistance figures in civilian/military networks.

## Historiography

Kershaw emphasizes the ethical complexity, personal courage, and internal divisions of the resisters alongside the structural barriers (loyalty oaths, fear of stab-in-the-back legend, lack of popular support, regime’s terror capacity, and the indispensability of Hitler’s authority to every facet of rule). The plot was the 'last chance the Germans themselves had to put an end to the Nazi regime'. Its failure sealed the path to total nemesis. Primary sources: Goebbels diaries, Below, military records, post-war testimony (critical); Stauffenberg/Tresckow statements preserved in the record.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

**The conspiracy's long gestation.** Rooted in the 1938 officer opposition; sustained by the Foreign Ministry circle (Weizsäcker, Trott, Haeften), Goerdeler, Popitz, Beck, and Canaris/Oster's Military Intelligence (with Bonhoeffer); radicalized on the Eastern Front by the atrocities — Hassell's diary already on 8 October 1940: "the Jews are systematically being exterminated." Prior attempts failed narrowly: Tresckow's cognac-bottle bomb on Hitler's plane (13 March 1943, fuse killed by cold) and Gersdorff's aborted suicide bombing at the trophy exhibition (21 March 1943). Casablanca's unconditional-surrender demand gutted the negotiating rationale; arrests (Oster, Bonhoeffer 1943; Canaris's agency absorbed into the SS and Moltke arrested, January 1944) closed in. Stauffenberg — maimed in North Africa, a former enthusiast turned opponent by the SS's crimes — joined Olbricht's Valkyrie machinery; Tresckow supplied the categorical imperative: "The assassination must be attempted at any cost . . . We must prove to the world and to future generations that the men of the German resistance movement dared to take the decisive step."

**20 July.** His injuries let Stauffenberg prime only one of the two bombs; the oak map table deflected the blast (four dead, Hitler lightly hurt). In Berlin the coup died of half-cut communications, Fromm's fence-sitting, and Goebbels's phone call putting Remer through to the living Hitler. Fromm executed Olbricht, Stauffenberg ("Long live sanctified Germany!"), Haeften, and Mertz that night to bury his own complicity; Beck botched his suicide and was finished off. In Paris Stülpnagel had actually arrested 1,000+ SS men before Kluge reversed it; the affair was smoothed over with champagne at the Hôtel Raphaël, but Stülpnagel's own suicide attempt left him blinded for Freisler's court.

**Reprisals.** ~5,000 arrested, ~1,000 dead including the suicides of Tresckow ("Hitler is the arch-enemy not only of Germany but of the whole world"), Kluge, and — offered the choice of poison-with-state-funeral or trial — Rommel. Freisler's People's Court humiliated the defendants (no belts or braces); hangings at Plötzensee used thin rope for slow strangulation and were filmed for Hitler's viewing. Sippenhaft, justified by Himmler as "old Germanic tradition," imprisoned the conspirators' families; Stauffenberg's children were renamed in an orphanage.

**Evans's assessment.** The failure was both contingent (one bomb, uncut wires, Fromm and Kluge wavering) and structural: a tiny base, the oath, fear of a second stab-in-the-back legend, a backward-looking program without popular appeal, and Allies who would not have negotiated (London read the news as an internal Nazi squabble). He is equally firm on the non-hagiographic record — Stülpnagel, Schulenburg, Helldorf, and Nebe were prior perpetrators, and Goerdeler's 1942 memorandum still proposed racial classification of surviving Jews (by 1944 he condemned the persecution outright) — and on the moral bottom line: success might have shortened the war, "saving millions of lives . . . This alone was more than enough justification for the undertaking." Yorck von Wartenburg's last letter: "My actions were influenced solely by my patriotic feeling, my concern for my Germany as it has grown over the past two thousand years."
