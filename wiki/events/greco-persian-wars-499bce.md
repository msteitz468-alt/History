# The Greco-Persian Wars (499–479 BCE)

The conflict between the **Achaemenid Persian Empire** and an alliance of mainland **Greek
city-states**, in which the small, poor and disunited Greeks improbably repelled the largest empire
the world had yet seen — and which is the central subject of **[[herodotus-histories|Herodotus's
*Histories*]]**, our principal source. As Carolyn Dewald frames it: "The Greeks, not the Persians, won
the Persian Wars," and the fifty years that followed made Athens "the dominant cultural and political
centre in Greece."

## Narrative

- **The Ionian Revolt (499–494 BCE).** The Greek cities of [[ionia|Ionia]] on the coast of Asia Minor,
  under Persian rule since Cyrus's conquest of [[lydia|Lydia]] (546), rose against Darius I, led by
  Aristagoras of Miletus. **Athens and Eretria sent help** (and burned Sardis), but the revolt was
  crushed at the naval battle of **Lade** and the sack of Miletus (494). See
  [[ionian-revolt-499bce|the Ionian Revolt]].
- **The first invasion — Marathon (490 BCE).** In retribution, **Darius** sent a seaborne punitive
  expedition that sacked Eretria and landed in Attica, where the Athenians (with Plataeans) defeated it
  at **[[battle-of-marathon-490bce|Marathon]]** — Herodotus's figures: 192 Greek dead to 6,400
  Persian.
- **The great invasion — Xerxes (480–479 BCE).** Darius's son **Xerxes** led a massive combined
  land-and-sea invasion across a bridged Hellespont. A sequence of battles defined its course:
  - **[[battle-of-thermopylae-480bce|Thermopylae]]** and the simultaneous naval **Artemisium** (480):
    the Spartan king **Leonidas** and his 300 (with allies) died holding the pass; the Greek fleet
    fought the Persians to a standstill before withdrawing.
  - **[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]** (480): **Themistocles** lured the Persian fleet into the
    narrows off Salamis and destroyed it — the decisive turn, after which Xerxes withdrew to Asia,
    leaving his general **Mardonius** in Greece.
  - **[[battle-of-plataea-479bce|Plataea]]** and the naval **Mycale** (479): the allied Greek army
    under the Spartan regent **Pausanias** destroyed Mardonius's land army at Plataea, while a Greek
    fleet burned the Persian ships at Mycale on the Ionian coast — ending the invasion.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the relentless expansion of the **[[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid empire]]**, which had a
*nomos* (custom) of conquest (Herodotus 7.8); `contributed_to:` the **[[ionian-revolt-499bce|Ionian
Revolt]]** and the fateful **Athenian and Eretrian intervention**, which drew Persian retribution
westward; and the dynastic momentum by which each king (Darius, then Xerxes) was bound to extend his
predecessor's conquests. Herodotus also reads deeper causes into the clash — the overreaching of
autocratic power beyond its natural limits (`unknown` as to how far this moral schema reflects
real causation).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` (1) the **survival and independence of mainland Greece**, preserving the conditions for
classical Greek civilization; (2) the **rise of Athens** — its navy, built on Themistocles's policy,
became the basis of the **[[delian-league|Delian League]]** and the Athenian empire, and underwrote
the cultural and democratic flowering of the fifth century (the "Greek enlightenment"); (3) a lasting
**ideological polarity** of free Greek *polis* versus Persian "despotism" that shaped Greek
self-understanding; and (4), through Herodotus's effort to commemorate the moment "when Greeks for
once worked together," the **invention of history-writing** itself. The Greek victory also set the
stage for the [[peloponnesian-war|fratricidal Peloponnesian War]] between Athens and Sparta a
generation later.

## Historiography

The wars are known almost entirely through **[[herodotus-histories|Herodotus]]** (with later
supplements), which makes the event inseparable from the **[[herodotus-reliability|debate over his
reliability]]** — his vast and probably inflated Persian numbers, his invented speeches and motives,
his pro-Hellenic and at times pro-Athenian colouring. Modern scholarship treats his narrative as a
critically-sifted but indispensable foundation. The wars' memory was itself immediately mythologized
(Aeschylus's *Persians*, 472 BCE; the Marathon and Thermopylae legends) as a charter of Greek freedom.

### Osborne (2000) — narrative frame and the "wooden walls"

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed., 2000)]] (Ch. 7, Kallet) gives the
standard fifth-century-narrative version: Persian westward expansion had been under way
since 513, when Darius crossed into Europe and subdued Thrace and Macedon; the Ionian
Revolt (499–494) drew in Athenian and Eretrian help, and when Athenians and Eretrians
accidentally burned a temple at Sardis, Darius vowed revenge, reportedly ordering a slave to
repeat "Master, do not forget the Athenians" before every meal. The first campaign (490)
burned and sacked Eretria before landing at Marathon. For the second invasion, Herodotus's
estimate of five million for Xerxes' combined army and support train is judged implausible;
Osborne puts the true figure closer to 200,000. Only 31 of some 700 Greek poleis sent
delegates to the council at the Isthmus of Corinth that formed the Hellenic League under
Spartan leadership. The Delphic oracle first told the Athenians to "flee to the ends of the
earth," then moderated the message to permit the "wooden walls" reading; **Themistocles**
persuaded the Athenians this meant ships, leading to the evacuation of Athens and the naval
strategy that produced Salamis — a strategy resting on his earlier use of Laurium
silver-mine revenue to build a 200-ship fleet. Boeotia "medized" (collaborated with Persia)
once the Persians broke through at Thermopylae. Osborne closes the narrative by noting that
Athens and Sparta were respectively chiefly responsible for the victories at Salamis and
Plataea — a division of glory that meant Sparta would henceforth have to **share** its
former military pre-eminence in Greece with Athens.

### Hall (2014) — Excursus IV frame: Lydia, limited resistance, Himera

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] (esp. Excursus IV and the book’s Persian-war chapter) reframes causes, scale, and periodization:

**Lydian–Persian path.** Persian-war “causes” in Herodotus run back through Lydian pressure: Gyges attacked Miletus/Smyrna and took Colophon; under Croesus remaining coastal cities (not offshore islands) were conquered and made tributary before Cyrus destroyed Croesus’s empire and incorporated Asiatic Greeks. Traditional 547/6 for Cyrus’s conquest is questioned, but Sardis mid-sixth destruction is archaeological; Phocaea half-abandoned for Corsica after Croesus’s fall.

**Herodotus as commemorative source.** Writing ~half a century later without unmediated Persian experience, Herodotus remains primary narrative for 480–479; “events” relatively uncontroversial but **emphasis and motivations may tell more about later Greek commemoration than Persian foreign policy**. Strictly speaking the *Histories* are a secondary source for Archaic events.

**Beyond revenge for 499.** Against Herodotus’s “beginning of evils” (Athenian/Eretrian ships) as sufficient cause: Darius’s Scythian ambitions, Samos intervention, Artaphernes’s Cyclades expedition, Mardonius’s 492 vassalage in Thrace/Macedonia/Thasos; 490 targets included Naxos, Eretria, Athens (possible punishment for ca. 507 earth-and-water submission violation). From Persian perspective Marathon was not a major catastrophe but irked the court; Xerxes prepared a larger invasion (not Herodotus’s 1.7 million).

**Hellenic League and limited participation.** In 481 Spartans assembled Peloponnesian allies plus non-mediatizing cities including Athens; failed to get Argos/Syracuse/Corcyra/Crete. **Delphi victory dedication (Serpent Column, ML 27) listed only thirty-one allies** — Hall repeatedly stresses that only a small minority of poleis fought Persia, undermining totalizing “Greek vs barbarian” memory. Invention of the barbarian after 480–479 is overwhelmingly Athenian evidence (Aeschylus *Persians* 472); Thebes/Argos/Sparta show weaker or ambiguous anti-eastern hostility.

**Himera not coordinated with Persia.** Hall discounts Ephorus’s claim of Carthaginian–Persian coordination and Herodotus’s speech implying long Gelon–Carthage war; Sicilian trigger was Anaxilas–Terillos vs Theron–Gelon; Himera victory synchronized by tradition with Salamis or Thermopylae but was not a planned dual front.

**Period break weak.** Conventional Archaic lower terminus 480–479 is artificial: Cleisthenic order already decades old; only 31 poleis resisted; many cities (Argos, etc.) stood aside; without knowing the Persian invasion the 470s would not look especially distinctive.

### OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Forrest, Parker, Hornblower

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]] (Ch.1 Forrest; Ch.11 Parker; Ch.6 Hornblower outline) gives a compact narrative and interpretive frame:

**Persian “nutcracker” and Ionian Revolt (Forrest).** From c.546 Persia absorbed Asia Minor Greeks under tighter control and compliant tyrants; 525 Egypt/N Africa; 514 Europe with Thrace–Macedon influence; Carthage pressed western Greeks—mainland Greece between “teeth of a mighty nutcracker.” 499 partial Ionian revolt (Histiaeus/Aristagoras); Sparta refused, Athens helped hesitantly; after 494 suppression, 490 Marathon: ~10,000 Athenian+Plataean hoplites claimed 6,000+ Persian vs ~200 Greek dead. Results: hostility/superiority ideology toward “barbarians,” proof of hoplite superiority, and (per Aristotle) popular political confidence (first ostracisms).

**480–479 campaign (Forrest).** Hellenic congress at Sparta 481 / Corinth spring 480 gave Sparta land and (diplomatically) sea command; Thermopylae lost via mountain track (Leonidas’ 300 and Thespians die); [[themistocles|Themistocles]] forced Salamis narrows battle, breaking Xerxes’ fleet; traditional synchronism with Syracusan victory over Carthage at Himera; 479 Plataea (Pausanias) and Mycale cleared the Aegean and began Asiatic Greek liberation—setting dual supremacy and oligarchy/democracy patterns. “The distinction between Greek and barbarian (foreigner) became one between Greek and Barbarian (national enemy).”

**Wooden walls as lay supremacy over seers (Parker).** When Delphi advised trust in “wooden walls,” professional interpreters urged staying behind city walls; Themistocles argued the god meant the fleet; the citizen assembly adopted his reading—illustrating secular political control of cult expertise.

**Post-war hegemony path (Hornblower).** The 478 foundation of the Athenian naval empire, Cimon’s Eurymedon, and Peace of Callias (~449) are treated as the wars’ institutional aftermath on the path to the pentekontaetia and the Great Peloponnesian War.
