# First Battle of El Alamein (July 1942)

**Source grounding:** All claims, quotes, and details below are taken exclusively from Robert M. Citino, *Death of the Wehrmacht: The German Campaigns of 1942* (2007), Chapter 6, "Coming to a Halt: North Africa," specifically the sections "Heading to El Alamein" and "The End of Movement: The First Battle of El Alamein (July 1942)."

## Narrative

By late June 1942, after the fall of Tobruk and the breakthrough at Mersa Matruh, Erwin Rommel's Panzerarmee Afrika had conducted one of the swiftest pursuits of the war, advancing some 350 miles in ten days and overrunning numerous British supply dumps. Rommel arrived at the western edge of the British position at El Alamein on June 30 with the intention of taking it "on the run."

The El Alamein position offered a narrow front of roughly forty miles between the Mediterranean Sea and the impassable Qattara Depression. It was essentially flat tank country with minimal natural obstacles (Deir el Shein only 40 meters elevation, Ruweisat Ridge 66 meters, Alam Halfa 132 meters). The British force assembling there included a mix of mauled veteran units (1st Armoured, 50th Division) and fresh formations (51st Highland, 44th Home Counties, 18th Indian Brigade, 9th Australian Division). Crucially, 8th Army commander Claude Auchinleck centralized virtually all artillery under army control.

Rommel entered the battle on July 1 with only fifty-two serviceable tanks, nearly all Mark IIIs. His plan mirrored the recent success at Mersa Matruh: 90th Light Division would penetrate south of the El Alamein box, wheel left to the sea to isolate it; Afrika Korps would follow, wheel right, and encircle the British armor.

The assault began at 3:15 A.M. on July 1. 90th Light advanced slowly through shifting dunes and immediately encountered "a hornet’s nest of massed British artillery fire" from the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd South African Brigades. By 7:30 the advance halted. A noon attempt to slide south met the same fire. A panic broke out in 90th Light around 1530 hours:

> A panic breaks out in the Division (1530 hours) which is stopped just in time by the energetic action of the Divisional Commander and Chief of Staff. Supply columns and even parts of fighting units rush back under the ever-increasing enemy artillery fire. The commanders of the battle groups, however, succeed in keeping the majority of their units facing the enemy and bring back the troops which have taken to flight.

Rommel, moving forward to observe, was pinned for two hours:

> Again heavy British fire struck our ranks. From the north, east, and south British shells screamed down on us. Tracer-shells from British flak went whizzing through the unit. The attack came to a standstill in the heaviest possible fire.

Afrika Korps arrived three hours late due to sandstorm and escarpments. It stormed the unexpected 18th Indian Brigade box at Deir el Shein, taking 1,200 prisoners but losing eighteen tanks—35 percent of Rommel’s total armored strength of fifty-two.

On July 2, Afrika Korps attempted to widen the break-in ("den Einbruch zum Durchbruch zu erweitern") along Ruweisat Ridge but ran into 1st Armoured Division and Robcol (a mobile column from 10th Indian Division). It gained four miles but failed to wheel north. 90th Light made no progress.

On July 3, Rommel concentrated five mobile divisions as a battering ram. The 2nd New Zealand Division’s mobile columns from the south smashed Ariete Division in one hour, overrunning its artillery and most tanks. Afrika Korps again hit a "brick wall" on Ruweisat.

With British forces north, east, and south and skies filled with British aircraft, Rommel halted the advance, extricated his remaining twenty-six tanks under cover of night, and replaced them with Italian infantry divisions. The war of movement had come to a halt.

Auchinleck then counterattacked. On July 10, the 9th Australian Division, supported by hurricane bombardment, destroyed the Italian Sabratha division at Tel el Eisa, overrunning its artillery and occupying the ridge. Subsequent attacks smashed Trieste, Brescia, and Pavia divisions. Operation Splendour (July 22) failed disastrously for the British (New Zealanders overrun; 23rd Armoured Brigade lost 100 of 106 tanks in a minefield and antitank fire), but the cumulative effect forced Rommel to abandon breakthrough plans to rescue his Italian allies.

## Causal Analysis

The halt resulted from the interaction of:

- **Logistical and material culmination**: After Gazala and the pursuit, the Panzerarmee fielded only ~50 tanks and divisions reduced to 1,500–2,000 men. It had outrun its air support. Captured British trucks and fuel postponed but did not eliminate the problem. Citino notes Clausewitz’s "culmination point" had been reached.
- **British recovery and adaptation**: Auchinleck’s centralized artillery control and influx of fresh troops (unaffected by "Rommel mystique") plus superior matériel (Grants, 6-pounders) produced concentrated defensive fire the Germans had not faced before. The position’s narrowness was less decisive than the men and guns manning it.
- **Failure of maneuver**: Classic German operational pattern (penetrate then wheel for Kesselschlacht) could not be executed against a prepared, artillery-heavy defense in "good tank country" where the defender refused to be outflanked easily. Rommel’s habit of attacking regardless of circumstances met its limit.
- `contributed_to:` [[concepts/auftragstaktik]] independence at lower levels could not overcome the loss of operational mobility and air parity.

Rommel himself later described the shift:

> The front had now solidified. In operational terms, the British were in their element, for their strength was in conducting a method of warfare that corresponded to the modern forms of infantry battle and Stellungskrieg. ... In this Stellungskrieg, the decision went to the side that fired off more ammunition.

## Consequence Analysis

- Immediate: Axis advance into Egypt stopped. Panzerarmee went over to the defensive in a dangerous salient. Italian divisions took the brunt of subsequent British spoiling attacks.
- Operational: The "signal moment" in the desert war. The presence of Italian infantry divisions holding the front line "could mean only one thing: the war of movement had come to a halt."
- Broader: Shifted initiative to the British in North Africa. Enabled the logistical and matériel buildup (including American aid) that would make Alam Halfa and Second El Alamein possible. Demonstrated that British 8th Army could recover from repeated disasters and impose attrition.
- `produced:` conditions for [[events/battle-of-alam-halfa-1942]]
- `part_of:` the larger collapse of classic Wehrmacht Bewegungskrieg in 1942 across theaters.

## Actors

- **Rommel and Panzerarmee staff**: Continued aggressive leadership from the front but increasingly recognized limits. Rommel and Bayerlein pinned under fire; Nehring wounded earlier at Mersa Matruh context.
- **Auchinleck**: Masterful use of centralized artillery and exploitation of Italian weakness to force Rommel onto the defensive.
- **New Zealanders and Australians**: Elite performance; New Zealanders overran Ariete and later contributed to pinning actions.

## Historiography

Citino presents First El Alamein as the point where the traditional Prussian-German operational art (Bewegungskrieg and Kesselschlacht) met the emerging Allied combination of material superiority, air power, and sound defensive management. It was not merely geography or "fighting the last battle" but a systemic mismatch: the attacker had worn down while the defender had recovered cohesion and firepower. The battle is portrayed as the end of Rommel’s ability to dictate operational tempo through mobility alone. No major interpretive controversies are detailed in this source range; emphasis is on the continuity with earlier German campaigns where aggression succeeded until culmination and enemy adaptation intervened.
