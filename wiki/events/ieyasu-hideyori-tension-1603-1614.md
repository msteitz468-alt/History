# Ieyasu-Hideyori Tensions and the Road to the Osaka Campaigns (1603–1614)

The decade from Ieyasu's appointment as shogun (1603) to the outbreak of the Osaka Winter Campaign
(1614) was defined by his methodical effort to eliminate Toyotomi Hideyori as a potential rival
center of power, first through financial attrition and diplomatic isolation, then through a
manufactured casus belli. Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Narrative

**The political situation at the outset.** Hideyori resided in Osaka castle with an income of
637,400 koku. After the death of Maeda Toshiie in 1599, Katagiri Katsumoto had become his
guardian. In 1601 Ieyasu built the castle of Nijo to dominate Kyoto, already flanked by Fushimi
and Zese. In 1603 Hideyori was married to Sen-hime, daughter of Shogun Hidetada — a dynastic
binding that simultaneously acknowledged the Toyotomi and subordinated them to the Tokugawa.

**The 1605 succession.** When Ieyasu retired in favor of Hidetada in 1605, Hidetada came to Kyoto
with an army of seventy thousand men to receive his commission from the Emperor. Ieyasu tried to
get Hideyori to come to Edo to visit his father-in-law, but Yodogimi "saw through the plan and
objected strongly, declaring that she would cut her son open herself rather than that he should
leave Osaka." The refusal confirmed the castle at Osaka as the one remaining center of potential
resistance.

**Death of the pro-Toyotomi generation.** Between 1611 and 1614, the senior daimyo with ties of
loyalty to Hideyoshi and hence potential sympathy for Hideyori died in quick succession: Asano
Nagamasa (1611, aged 65), Kato Kiyomasa (1611, aged 53), Horio Yoshiharu (1611, aged 69), Ikeda
Terumasa (1613, aged 50), Asano Yukinaga (1613, aged 38), and Maeda Toshinaga (1614, aged 53).
Ieyasu was accused of poisoning them at banquets — a charge Sadler regards as implausible given
the time elapsed, noting that Kato, Asano, Ikeda, and Okubo Nagayasu are all elsewhere reported
to have died of "the Chinese pox" (syphilis). "Ieyasu was lucky as usual. Even the spirochaete was
on his side."

**Systematic isolation.** Ieyasu issued orders to western and central daimyo not to stop at Osaka
and pay respects to Hideyori before visiting Sumpu or Edo, not to bring large escort, and not to
"wear so much beard." Kato Kiyomasa objected that this would be "unchivalrous," a rare act of
resistance.

**The 1611 Nijo meeting.** Hideyori was invited to Nijo castle where Ieyasu was staying. Ieyasu's
seventh and eighth sons (Yoshinao and Yorinobu) were given as hostages to Hideyori's generals to
persuade him to come. Ieyasu "was considerably impressed, and probably not very delighted with his
cleverness" — Katagiri had represented Hideyori as dull and incapable.

**The Great Buddha strategy.** Ieyasu persuaded Hideyori and Yodogimi that it was proper for
Hideyori to rebuild the Great Buddha at Kyoto (Hideyoshi's had burned in 1602). This was a
deliberate drain on Hideyori's treasury. When Yodogimi had earlier asked Ieyasu's wife to help
fund it, Ieyasu had burst out: "This Buddha was a piece of eccentricity of Hideyoshi's, and there
was no need to rebuild it at all. If they thought there was they could do it themselves." He was
simply ensuring that Hideyori would do it at Hideyori's own expense.

**The bell inscription pretext (1614).** By spring 1614 the new Great Buddha and temple were
complete, and a great bell was cast with an inscription by Seikan, priest of Nanzenji. The Kyoto
Governor (Itakura), almost certainly prompted by the monk Tenkai — a Tokugawa ally who resented
Seikan's prominence — interposed to demand postponement of the inauguration ceremonies on the
grounds that the inscription insulted Ieyasu. The specific objections were:

- The character *an* in the phrase *Kokka ankō* ("the country at peace") split the characters of
  Ieyasu's name (*Ie-an-su*), an act of lese-majesty.
- The phrase *Hōshin-kun-shison-i-shō-raku* ("Rulers and subjects prosperous... all their
  progeny happy") was parsed as "Rejoicing in the resplendent glory of the progeny of the house of
  Toyotomi as our rulers."
- "In the East it greets the pale moon, and in the West bids farewell to the setting sun" was
  interpreted as making Ieyasu (the eastern lord) the lesser luminary.

These interpretations were deliberately forced. Katagiri was summoned to Shizuoka by Honda
Masazumi and told to find a settlement: erase the inscription *and* do something more. But Honda
refused to say what. To Yodogimi's ladies Ieyasu said nothing about the bell, asking only that
Hideyori refrain from military preparations and from attracting unemployed samurai (*ronin*).

**The Katagiri crisis.** Katagiri told Ieyasu's men that Hideyori should either move to another
province or Yodogimi should go to Edo as a hostage. The ladies reported this to Yodogimi as
Katagiri's own plot; Ono Harunaga (Yodogimi's secondary husband, jealous of Katagiri) ordered
his assassination, which the generals refused. Katagiri tried to explain he was only buying time
until Ieyasu died. But his position at Osaka was destroyed, and he eventually retired to his fief.
In both Osaka campaigns he fought for Ieyasu; he later committed suicide for failing to save
Hideyori.

**The final provocation.** "Ieyasu kept on circulating rumors of Hideyori's intention to rebel
against the Shogunate till at last the Osaka leaders, seeing that a clash was inevitable, no
longer hid their defensive preparations, which Ieyasu then declared to be a casus belli and
attacked. It was he who forced on the rupture of relations, and was active in preparations for the
conflict, as becomes evident from the accounts of the English Company, which describes a strong
demand for their guns and ammunition from Ieyasu long before Osaka showed any signs of wanting any."

## Consequence Analysis

The 1614 bell-inscription crisis was the immediate pretext for the Osaka Winter Campaign later that
year, followed by the Osaka Summer Campaign of 1615. Osaka castle fell, Hideyori and Yodogimi
committed suicide, and the Toyotomi line was extinguished. The Tokugawa monopoly on power was
thereafter unchallenged until 1868.

## Actors

- **[[tokugawa-ieyasu]]** — architect of the whole sequence; used financial attrition, diplomatic
  isolation, family intermarriage, and manufactured insult as successive tools
- **Toyotomi Hideyori** — Toyotomi heir; intelligent (surprising Ieyasu at their 1611 meeting)
  but outmaneuvered at every turn
- **Yodogimi (Lady Yodo)** — Hideyori's mother; the decisive voice against sending him to Edo as
  hostage; her refusal preserved the confrontation but ultimately could not prevent it
- **Katagiri Katsumoto** — Hideyori's guardian, caught between loyalty and the reality of Tokugawa
  power; ultimately sacrificed by both sides; committed suicide
- **Ono Harunaga** — Yodogimi's secondary husband; faction rival to Katagiri; contributed to
  Katagiri's expulsion from the Osaka camp
- **Tenkai** — Tokugawa-allied Buddhist monk; almost certainly the instigator of the bell
  inscription controversy

## Historiography

Sadler's source, drawing on Tokutomi's biography of Ieyasu, is explicit that Ieyasu manufactured
the final crisis: "It was he who forced on the rupture of relations." This interpretation — that
the bell controversy was a pretextual move in a long-planned campaign — is the dominant view in
Tokugawa historiography. The accusation of poisoning Ieyasu's enemies is recorded but considered
implausible by most historians; the syphilis attribution for multiple deaths is a separate
historical curiosity. The strategic logic of the whole decade 1603–1614 is uniformly read as
Ieyasu's deliberate preparation for the elimination of the Toyotomi.

**Collection coverage**: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]] provides the best single-volume
narrative account. Specialist studies of the Osaka campaigns are not yet ingested.
