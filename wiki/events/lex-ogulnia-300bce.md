# Lex Ogulnia (300 BCE)

A Roman law proposed by the plebeian tribunes Gnaeus and Quintus Ogulnius that increased the membership of the college of pontiffs from four to eight and the college of augurs from four (or six) to nine, with the new positions reserved for plebeians — breaking the patrician monopoly over Rome's two most important priestly colleges.

The law came near the end of the long Conflict of the Orders, the centuries-long plebeian struggle for legal and political equality with the patrician class, following earlier plebeian gains in access to the consulship (Licinian-Sextian laws, 367 BCE) and other magistracies. Religious office had remained one of the patricians' last exclusive preserves, since control of augury and pontifical law carried real political power (the ability to declare omens unfavorable, interpret religious law, and control the calendar). Its passage effectively completed plebeian access to the core institutions of Roman public authority, both civil and religious.

## Causal Analysis

The Lex Ogulnia was **contributed to** by decades of plebeian political mobilization achieving cumulative gains in civil magistracies, which made continued patrician exclusivity over priestly office increasingly anomalous and politically untenable.

## Consequence Analysis

The law **produced** a lasting change in the composition of Rome's priestly colleges and **enabled** further blurring of the patrician-plebeian distinction as a meaningful political category over the following generation, as wealthy plebeian families increasingly intermarried with patricians and held both civil and religious office.

## Historiography

Known primarily through Livy's brief narrative account (10.6-9), which frames it as part of the culminating drama of the Conflict of the Orders. Modern scholarship generally treats the episode as broadly reliable in outline, though Livy's dramatic framing of Struggle-of-the-Orders debates should be read with some caution as retrospective political narrative construction.
