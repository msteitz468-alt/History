# The Sicilian Vespers (1282)

The **Sicilian Vespers** was the rebellion that erupted in Palermo on 30 March 1282, tore the
island of Sicily from **[[charles-of-anjou|Charles of Anjou]]**, and triggered the twenty-year
**War of the Vespers** that permanently split the [[kingdom-of-sicily|kingdom of Sicily]].
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (David Abulafia, Ch. 15e) treats it as the turning point that
"wrecked" the Angevin Mediterranean empire — and one of the era's most consequential revolts.

## Narrative

On the evening of Easter Monday 1282, a French trooper was thought to have molested a Sicilian
woman at the church of **Santo Spirito** outside Palermo; a riot erupted, the French garrison was
massacred, "and within a few days much of Sicily had risen against the house of Anjou" — within a
month even **Messina**, home of the fleet Charles was assembling against Byzantium. The rebels
first appealed to **Pope Martin IV** to recognise Sicilian **self-government** under papal
suzerainty (on the model of the central-Italian communes); but Martin, Charles's close ally,
condemned them outright. The Sicilian parliament then invited **Peter III of Aragon** — whose
wife **Constance** was the daughter of King Manfred and "virtually the last survivor of Frederick
II's dynasty" — to take the crown. Peter, his fleet conveniently at the ready off North Africa,
landed at Trapani in September 1282.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **long tradition of tight-fisted Angevin government**, "dedicated to the
  maximisation of royal revenue to pay for foreign wars" (Charles's Balkan and Byzantine
  ambitions), which "generated intense ill-feeling among the Sicilian elites."
- `contributed_to:` Charles's **reliance on non-Sicilian officials** (Provençals, north
  Italians, the "Amalfitan" bureaucrats); his **absenteeism** (he ruled from Naples and visited
  Sicily only briefly); and **structural change** — the decline of the old Muslim/Greek
  population and the influx of Latin settlers with frontier-society expectations of low taxes.
- `enabled:` the **Angevin–Aragonese rivalry** — a long sequence of clashes over Tunis,
  Sardinia, crusades and Provence — and lingering **Hohenstaufen loyalism**. Exiles like
  **Giovanni da Procida** agitated for Peter, though Abulafia rejects the legend that he was
  "the secret architect" of the revolt; the Sicilians' own preference was for a federation of
  free communes, not any king.

## Consequence Analysis

The Vespers **split the kingdom permanently**: the **island of Sicily** went to the Aragonese
line, **mainland southern Italy** (the kingdom of **Naples**) to the Angevins. The war ruined
Charles's grander designs — his planned conquest of **Constantinople** (with Venice and Martin
IV) had to be abandoned, to the great benefit of **Michael VIII Palaiologos**. Peter III's victory
held despite a French "crusade" against Aragon (1285); the deaths of Charles I, Peter III and
Martin IV in 1285 did not end it. After two decades, the **Peace of Caltabellotta (1302)** left
**Frederick III** (Peter's younger son) as king of the island ("Trinacria"), the Angevins keeping
the mainland. Abulafia stresses the **structural effects**: two separate Sicilian kingdoms, a
Naples bound ever more tightly to Guelf [[florence|Florence]] and its bankers, and an island
economy damaged by war.

## Historiography

Abulafia weighs the romantic "conspiracy" tradition (Giovanni da Procida as mastermind, dramatised
by later writers and Verdi's opera) against the structural explanation — overgovernment, fiscal
exhaustion, settler grievance, and the Angevin–Aragonese contest. He sides with structure: the
revolt's speed and uncontrollability ("as in any violent revolution") show a society "pushed to
the edge," not a plot executed to plan.
