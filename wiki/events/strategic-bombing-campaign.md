# The Strategic Bombing Campaign (1940–1945)

The **strategic bombing campaign** of the Second World War was the sustained Allied (and earlier
Axis) effort to win the war from the air by attacking the enemy's **war economy and civilian
morale** behind the front — the fullest expression of [[total-war]]'s erasure of the line between
combatant and civilian. From the [[cambridge-history-second-world-war-v1|Cambridge History of the
Second World War]] (Vol. I, Anglo-American strategic bombing; Vol. III on its economic effects and
the destruction of cities).

## Narrative

Pre-war air-power theory (Douhet, Trenchard, the US "industrial web" school) held that fleets of
bombers could be decisive by themselves. After the German *Blitz* on British cities (1940–41) and
the discovery that bombers could not hit precise targets by night, **RAF Bomber Command** turned to
**area bombing** of German cities (the dehousing of workers and the breaking of morale), while the
**US Army Air Forces** pursued daylight "precision" bombing of key industries (oil, ball-bearings,
aircraft, transport). The combined offensive grew enormously from 1943 — Hamburg (the 1943
firestorm), the Ruhr, Berlin, and **Dresden** (February 1945) — and in the Pacific culminated in
the **firebombing of Japanese cities** (Tokyo, March 1945) and the **atomic bombings of Hiroshima
and Nagasaki** (August 1945).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the inter-war doctrine that **air power could win wars independently** by hitting the
  enemy's economy and will.
- `contributed_to:` the **technical inaccuracy** of early bombing, which drove the RAF from
  precision to area (city) attacks; and the [[total-war]] logic that made the enemy's whole society
  a legitimate target.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` immense **civilian death and urban destruction** — roughly 600,000 German and several
  hundred thousand Japanese civilians killed, and many cities largely destroyed.
- `produced:` by 1944, **severe disruption of the German war economy** — the oil and transport
  campaigns, and the diversion of huge German resources to air defence (flak, fighters, the "second
  front" in the air). Adam Tooze and the official surveys argue bombing's decisive economic effect
  came **late but was real**, capping German production at the moment of crisis (see
  [[war-economy]]).
- `produced:` the **atomic age** (Hiroshima/Nagasaki) and a **lasting moral controversy** over the
  deliberate killing of civilians.

## Actors

**RAF Bomber Command** (Arthur Harris) and the **US Army Air Forces** against the cities and
industries of **Germany** and **Japan**; the **civilian populations** and war-workers who bore the
cost. See [[second-world-war-1939]].

## Historiography

The campaign is among the most debated aspects of the war. Disputes turn on **effectiveness** (did
it justify the vast resources it consumed? — sceptics vs. those, like Tooze, who stress its real
late impact on oil, transport, and air defence) and **morality** (the deliberate targeting of
civilians in area bombing, Dresden, and the atomic bombings). The [[cambridge-history-second-world-war-v1|CHSWW]]
treats it both as a military instrument (Vol. I) and as part of the war's catastrophic destruction
of urban society and its [[war-memory-and-mourning|memory]] (Vol. III).
