# Settlement of Iceland (Landnám)

## Narrative

Near the close of the eighth century A.D., Nordic pirates, traders, and settlers began the expansion from their Scandinavian homelands. Iceland was traditionally settled c. 874. Colonists drawn from both Scandinavia and the British Isles undertook the dangerous business of *landnám* (land taking, or first settlement) across diverse island ecosystems. In Iceland, humanity was either entirely absent or represented by a few (soon departing) hermetical monks, presenting an essentially virgin landscape.

Archaeological evidence for early settlement appears in many areas, both along the south coast and in the northern coast and interior. A nearly complete ninth-century longhouse has been excavated on Aðalstraeði in the center of modern Reykjavík. Multiple early sites in the more arctic northern interior around Lake Mývatn have been documented, including extensive boundary walls, charcoal-burning sites, pagan burials, and the site at Hofstaðir (somewhat controversially identified as a pagan temple in older literature).

By A.D. 930 the Icelanders had set up a self-governing system of local and national *things* (assembly places) intended to regulate competition among chieftains and adjudicate disputes among farmers. The assemblies voted to adopt Christianity as the official religion (although allowing some pagan practice) in 1000.

## Causal Analysis

**Environmental and technological drivers.** As Norse settlers moved from the long coast of Norway to Iceland they cut diagonally across the North Atlantic Drift. The south coast of Iceland is affected by the North Atlantic drift and is wet and comparatively warm in winter, but the north coast is low arctic. The period of initial landnám was warmer and probably more stable than average. The deceptive similarity of the western North Atlantic islands (fjords, valleys covered with dwarf willow, birch, grasses familiar from home) concealed that these plants were much closer to their biological limits in subarctic Iceland.

Rapid deforestation followed first settlement. Pollen studies suggest that 90 percent of the dwarf birch and willow forests present at landnám were removed in the first century of settlement. In some areas rapid soil erosion took place soon after.

**Social and political drivers.** First settlers had their pick of the best land but faced a true wilderness without established farms, fields, roads, or local farming expertise. Domestic animals and human labor were desperately scarce in the early years. Saga accounts mention failed landnám attempts. Long open-water voyages were always dangerous.

A "Skallagrim strategy" of diversified resource use and wide initial claims (including tenant farms) is reflected in both saga accounts and the archaeological record of rapid widespread population dispersal into all potentially habitable sites.

## Consequence Analysis

The settlement created a durable kingless society of chieftains (*goðar*) and assemblies that lasted until submission to the Norwegian crown in 1264. Environmental impacts (deforestation, erosion) created lasting vulnerabilities. The society produced the rich saga literature and legal traditions that later shaped perceptions of the Viking Age. Fishing later became central to the economy.

Barley growing was initially practiced but largely discontinued due to climate change and soil nutrient depletion; most Icelanders depended on milk, meat, fish, bird’s eggs, and gathered plants.

## Actors

- Chieftains (*goðar*) and free farmers.
- Settlers of mixed Scandinavian and British Isles genetic heritage (modern Icelanders show strong British Isles component).
- Specific figures such as Erik the Red (linked to Greenland) and the legendary Skallagrim.

## Historiography

Prior to the 1970s, discussion was dominated by philologists and the later saga literature (post-dating events by several hundred years). Since the mid-1970s, archaeology, paleoecology, tephrochronology (volcanic ash layers), and radiocarbon dating have dominated. The written accounts are reinterpreted in light of fresh evidence. The Icelandic population fluctuated around fifty thousand throughout most of the Middle Ages, surviving epidemic disease, volcanic eruption, climate cooling, and repeated famine.
