# Nazi Seizure of Power (Machtergreifung) 1933

## Narrative

On 30 January 1933, Adolf Hitler was appointed Reich Chancellor by President Paul von Hindenburg in a presidential cabinet that conservatives believed they could control. The appointment followed backroom negotiations involving Franz von Papen and others who thought they had ‘hired’ or ‘boxed in’ Hitler. The day was marked by a massive torchlight procession of SA and SS through Berlin, which Hindenburg watched from his window while Hitler received wild acclaim from the marching columns. Contemporaries immediately stylized it as the ‘day of the national uprising’, though Hitler preferred ‘takeover of power’ to emphasize formal legality. Power had been handed to him in the constitutional manner of his predecessors, not seized in a putsch. 

Within a month, civil liberties protected under the Weimar Constitution had been extinguished. The Reichstag fire of 27 February 1933 provided the immediate pretext for emergency decrees suspending civil liberties. 

The ‘Day of Potsdam’ on 21 March 1933 staged a symbolic union of Prussian conservatism and the new regime in the Garnisonkirche, with Hindenburg in field-marshal uniform saluting the empty throne of the exiled Kaiser and Hitler appearing in morning-suit as humble servant. Two days later, on the afternoon of 23 March 1933, in the Kroll Opera House surrounded by SA, SS and Stahlhelm guards, Hitler addressed the Reichstag to secure the Enabling Act (‘Act for the Removal of Distress from People and Reich’). 

In his speech he promised far-reaching moral renewal, praised the Christian denominations as ‘the most important factors for upholding our nationhood’, assured that the existence of the Reichstag, Reichsrat, position of the Reich President, the Länder and the rights of the Churches would remain untouched. The Zentrum (Catholic Centre Party), after divisions and under the shadow of terror and the implicit blackmail of the armed guards, voted for the bill following verbal assurances from Hitler. The SPD leader Otto Wels spoke courageously in defence of humanity, justice, freedom and socialism; Hitler replied savagely, to storms of applause from Nazi deputies: ‘Germany will become free, but not through you.’

With 441 votes to the ninety-four of the Social Democrats, the Reichstag voted itself out of existence. The Enabling Act went into effect the next day. Hitler had obtained the two-thirds majority through the absence of arrested Communist deputies (and procedural manipulation counting absentees as present) and Centre Party support. As he told the cabinet, it granted powers that ‘we could have taken anyway’. 

During the spring and summer of 1933 Germany underwent *Gleichschaltung* — the ‘coordination’ of institutions and organizations. Nazi activists from below forced the pace, but many bodies rushed voluntarily to align themselves with the new era. One initiative from Hitler was the creation of Reich Governors (Reichsstatthalter) under the ‘Second Law for the Coordination of the Länder with the Reich’ of 7 April 1933, decisively undermining the sovereignty of the individual states. In Prussia Hitler reserved the governorship for himself, removing purpose from Papen’s role as Reich Commissioner. By autumn, the Nazi dictatorship and Hitler’s power at its head had been enormously strengthened. What is striking, according to the account, is not how much but how little Hitler needed to do. Pressure from below and anticipatory self-coordination by organizations did most of the work. 

Within four months the once-powerful trade unions were dissolved. In less than six months all opposition parties had been suppressed or gone into voluntary liquidation, leaving the NSDAP as the only remaining party. 

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the structural collapse of Weimar democracy and conservative miscalculation in handing the Chancellorship to Hitler in a ‘cabinet of struggle’ they believed they could dominate.

`contributed_to:` the Reichstag fire and the resulting emergency decrees that suspended civil liberties and created the atmosphere of terror and intimidation in which the Enabling Act was passed.

`enabled:` the rapid *Gleichschaltung* through a combination of pseudo-legal measures, terror, manipulation and willing collaboration by institutions and individuals.

Hitler’s tactical flexibility — broad promises in the Enabling Act speech, propaganda spectacles like the Day of Potsdam — masked the destruction of constitutional constraints. The Zentrum’s vote was critical in lending pseudo-legality; fear of civil war and open repression played a major role.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the legal foundation for one-party dictatorship; Hitler no longer reliant on Reichstag or (for most purposes) the Reich President.

`enabled:` the full process of coordination of state and society under Nazi control within months.

`preceded_by:` the appointment of 30 January 1933 and the violent street politics of the SA in the preceding years.

`followed_by:` further consolidation in 1934 via the Röhm purge and the death of Hindenburg.

The rapidity of the transformation — civil liberties gone within a month, Reichstag powers surrendered within two, trade unions dissolved within four, opposition parties eliminated within six, Länder sovereignty formally abolished in January 1934 — astonished contemporaries.

## Actors

- [[actors/adolf-hitler|Adolf Hitler]]: central figure; combined tactical moderation in public with ruthless exploitation of opportunities; master of propaganda and intimidation.
- Conservative elites (Papen, Hugenberg, Hindenburg circle): initially believed they could contain Hitler; rapidly marginalised.
- Zentrum deputies: provided the crucial votes for the Enabling Act under duress and after verbal assurances later broken.
- SA and SS: provided the menacing armed presence around the Reichstag and the street-level terror driving arrests and ‘protective custody’.
- Joseph Goebbels: orchestrated the Day of Potsdam propaganda spectacle.

## Historiography

Kershaw emphasises the combination of pseudo-legality, terror from below, and willing self-coordination rather than a fully pre-planned masterstroke by Hitler alone. The Enabling Act vote marked the point at which the Reichstag as a democratic body destroyed itself. The account stresses both Hitler’s finely tuned instinct for power and propaganda and the structural conditions (Weimar crisis legacies, conservative miscalculation, widespread desire for ‘national community’) that made the speed of the takeover possible. The process was not solely top-down; activists and opportunistic alignment from below accelerated *Gleichschaltung* far beyond what Hitler needed to initiate directly.

**Source grounding:** All narrative and analysis drawn exclusively from the designated range of Ian Kershaw, *Hitler 1889–1936: Hubris* (1998), lines 16501–22000 (primarily the sections on the appointment, Enabling Act passage, Day of Potsdam, and spring/summer 1933 coordination).

### The "backstairs conspiracy" and the comparative frame (Paxton, 2004)

[[paxton-anatomy-of-fascism-2004|*The Anatomy of Fascism*]] (ch. 4) supplies
the comparative mechanics behind the appointment. The Nazis **never won an
electoral majority**: 37.2% (July 1932) → 33.1% (November 1932) → 43.9% even
in March 1933 with full state resources — "More than one German in two voted
against Nazi candidates… in the teeth of intimidation by Storm Troopers."
After November 1932 the party was in genuine trouble — vote dropping,
treasury empty, Gregor Strasser negotiating independently with Chancellor
Schleicher: "The movement might have ended as a footnote to history had it
not been saved in the opening days of 1933 by conservative politicians who
wanted to pilfer its following." Papen, bitter at his replacement by
Schleicher, brokered the Hitler-chancellor/Papen-deputy deal (banker Kurt
von Schröder as go-between); Hindenburg, persuaded that Schleicher planned a
military dictatorship, appointed the government on 30 January 1933 — Hitler,
in Bullock's phrase Paxton endorses, "hoist into office by a backstairs
conspiracy." Paxton also stresses the Left's paralysis: misled by the myth
of the Italian "coup" ([[march-on-rome-1922]]), both SPD and KPD braced for
a Nazi putsch that never came, while the KPD dismissed the SPD as "social
fascists" and even joined the Nazis in the November 1932 Berlin transport
strike. On the Reichstag fire, Paxton records the current consensus that van
der Lubbe really set it and that Hitler's circle, taken by surprise,
believed a communist rising had begun — which made the emergency pretext no
less effective. The general law he draws: "the fascist route to power has
always passed through cooperation with conservative elites"
([[generic-fascism]]).

### Evans (2015): the 1933 terror wave against organized labor

Evans's British Academy lecture (in [[evans-third-reich-history-memory-2015]],
ch. 7) supplies the coercive mechanics against readings of a consensual
"handover": ~100,000 in "protective custody" without trial in 1933, 600+
deaths in custody; 3,000 SPD leaders arrested/tortured after the 21 June
ban; the Köpenick blood-week (91 SPD members tortured to death);
Mecklenburg SPD Minister-President Johannes Stelling tortured to death
and dumped in a river with 12 colleagues; Stassfurt's SPD mayor shot (5
Feb); union headquarters raided nationwide 2 May (four officials beaten
to death in Duisburg); 500 municipal administrators and 70 mayors removed
by end of May. The Enabling Act itself passed illegally (Göring declined
to count elected KPD deputies), and coercion reached Catholics, liberals
and the Nationalist coalition partners before culminating in
[[night-of-the-long-knives-1934]]. Full argument at
[[coercion-and-consent-nazi-germany]].

### Payne's account (1995): concatenation of crises

[[payne-history-of-fascism-1995|Payne]] frames the German catastrophe
against determinism: "the keys... do not lie in grasping any innate or
inevitable tendencies in German life" but in "the unique chain of crises and
traumas" 1914–33 (defeat, Versailles, revolution scare, hyperinflation,
Depression, parliamentary paralysis from mid-1930) — "though National
Socialism was not merely the product of this succession of unprecedented
traumas, it would have been impossible without them." His data points:
NSDAP vote 2.6% (1928, the failed urban worker strategy) → 18.3%/107 seats
(Sept 1930, over half the new votes from former middle-class liberal
voters) → 37%/230 (July 1932) → 33%/196 (Nov 1932, the first reverse);
membership 450,000 by end-1932, cross-class (about a quarter blue-collar,
with the SA roughly half working-class). He judges unemployment alone
non-determinative (German rates matched Norway's, which stayed democratic),
finds big business "consistently advised against" a Hitler government (the
party was member-financed), and documents that Comintern strategy "was aimed
not at preventing a Nazi triumph but at weakening the Socialists." The
mechanics thereafter: Reichstag fire decree of 28 February as "the real
beginning of the Nazi police state," the Enabling Act (444–94), ~100,000
arrests and ~500 killed February–September 1933, sole-party decree 14 July,
the Blood Purge of 30 June 1934 (~100 murdered, crushing the SA "second
revolution" and reassuring the army), and the merger of presidency and
chancellorship at Hindenburg's death (2 August 1934).

### The fiscal-collapse reading (Flynn, 1944 — attributed)

A contemporary structural account: [[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|Flynn]]
argues the dictatorship's apparatus was built by Weimar's own chancellors
before Hitler — Article 48, drafted for public-safety emergencies, stretched
by Luther to enforce tax measures and used systematically by Brüning from 1930
(after the Reichstag failed 236–225 to force withdrawal of his decrees), with
the electoral escalation following each dissolution: 6.5M Nazi votes/107 seats
(Sept 1930), 14M/230 (July 1932), falling to 11.5M in the election immediately
preceding the appointment. His counterfactual: suppressing Hitler by force
would have made Brüning "the very phenomenon which Hitler symbolized," and no
"if" (Thyssen withholding money, Papen/Schleicher not miscalculating) "would
have saved Germany from Hitlerism, by which I mean fascism" — candidates
Röhm, Strasser, Papen, Schleicher, "some general." **[CONTRADICTION — minor
factual]:** Flynn's text dates Hitler's summons to the chancellorship
January 25, 1933; the documented date is January 30, 1933 (Kershaw, Paxton).
Flynn's reading converges with Paxton's "backstairs conspiracy" on elite
miscalculation, but grounds it in fiscal breakdown rather than conservative
politics.

### Griffin's structural-plus-contingency frame (1991)

[[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991|Griffin]] runs the event through his
four-precondition retrodictive model (native ultra-nationalist tradition;
political space under structural crisis; inadequate liberal consensus;
favourable contingency — see [[palingenetic-ultranationalism]]), itemizing
seven Weimar crisis factors (humiliation and imperial collapse, Versailles,
the Berlin/Munich communist revolutions, hyperinflation, the Depression
compounding 1918–23 wounds, Weimar liberalism's perceived alienness, visible
governmental impotence) and then insisting on the residue of contingency —
Hindenburg's decision could have gone otherwise, as could Victor Emmanuel's
in 1922; Hindenburg's death in 1934 enabled the merger of offices. He quotes
Bracher: "Mishaps and errors, consequence and accident, became an almost
inextricable mass of causes of the National Socialist seizure of power."

### Evans (2003): The Terror Mechanics of Spring 1933

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]], Part 5) supplies the granular
machinery, extracted at full fidelity:

- **Neutralizing the army**: Blomberg (Defence, the army's man, "putty in the
  hands of someone like Hitler") and Reichenau isolated the contemptuous
  C-in-C Hammerstein; Hitler's 3 February 1933 address to senior officers
  promised conscription, destruction of Marxism, and "the intoxicating
  long-term prospect of invading Eastern Europe and 'Germanizing' it by
  expelling scores of millions of native Slav inhabitants" — with the assurance
  that the "internal struggle" was "not your business."
- **Unleashing the SA**: Göring ordered the Prussian police to stop watching
  the Nazis and support them (15–17 February), then created the SA/SS/Steel
  Helmet "auxiliary police" (22 February) — "the green light to go on the
  rampage."
- **The election of 5 March**: fought under terror and with business money
  (Schacht's 3 million RM collection of 20 February; Göring told the
  industrialists this election would be the last "probably for the next
  hundred" years), the Nazis still took only 43.9% — "the complete failure of
  the Nazis, even under conditions of semi-dictatorship, to win over a
  majority" (Evans). Yet nearly two-thirds of voters backed openly
  anti-Weimar parties; in 1919 three-quarters had backed the Weimar
  coalition — "It had taken only fourteen short years for this situation to
  be effectively reversed."
- **The nod-and-wink mechanism** (load-bearing for decision-mechanics debates):
  "a dialectical process... the leadership announced in extreme but unspecific
  terms that action was to be taken, and the lower echelons... translated this
  in their own terms into specific, violent action" — a party custom since the
  1920s of leaders "not say[ing] everything and just hint[ing] at what he
  wants to achieve with the order." Evans: "This pattern of decision-making...
  was to be repeated on many occasions subsequently, most notably in the
  Nazis' policy towards the Jews" (cross-ref
  [[coercion-and-consent-nazi-germany]], [[the-holocaust]]).
- **Coordination of the states** (6–15 March): swastikas raised by "auxiliary
  police," ministers resigning under SA siege, Frick installing commissioners
  — with the precedent chain Papen 1932 ← Ebert's Saxony/Thuringia 1923. "The
  public disorder that supplied the reason for declaring a state of emergency
  was overwhelmingly the creation of the Nazis themselves."
- **The terror in numbers**: ≥25,000 arrests in Prussia alone in March–April
  (excluding Berlin and "wild" SA arrests); ~10,000 in Bavaria by end April,
  double by June; 27,000 in "protective custody" nationwide at end July — a
  rolling population, so throughput was far higher; the KPD claimed 130,000
  arrests and 2,500 members murdered by year's end (Evans: "probably something
  of an exaggeration"); the most conservative quasi-official count gives
  >100,000 political arrests in 1933 and ~600 deaths in custody. Post-election
  atrocities included the Königsberg SPD headquarters turned torture center
  (KPD deputy Walter Schütz beaten to death), Wilhelm Sollmann tortured and
  forced to drink castor oil and urine, and the murder of Chemnitz SPD press
  chief Georg Landgraf for refusing to reveal party funds. Looting was
  pervasive enough that the Hesse police commissioner circulated a formal
  condemnation (19 April 1933).
- **The purge given legal form**: the Law for the Restoration of a
  Professional Civil Service (7 April 1933) — dismissal of post-1918
  appointees, "non-Aryans" (defined 11 April as one Jewish grandparent), and
  the politically unreliable; ~12.5% of senior Prussian civil servants
  dismissed. The "March Fallen": 1.6 million joined the party between 30
  January and 1 May 1933. Of ~45,000 Prussian judicial officials, only ~300
  were dismissed politically — and 2,250 prosecutions of SA men were
  suspended or abandoned by early 1934. Haffner's diagnosis, quoted by Evans:
  "The simplest... nearly always the most basic reason was fear. Join the
  thugs to avoid being beaten up."

## Griffin (2007): Seizure of Power as Resolution of a Nomic Crisis

*Modernism and Fascism* adds the primordialist layer: the Depression triggered "not just an economic and political crisis but a nomic crisis, intensifying liminoid conditions in Germany to a point where millions now felt compelled to 'jump ship'" from Weimar (NSDAP 2.6% May 1928 → 37.3% July 1932; over 50% for revolutionary anti-democratic parties). Hitler had rebuilt the post-putsch NSDAP as a [[revitalization-movement]] — a *nationale Erhebung*, not a party — offering communitas against Spengler's "quagmire." Griffin stresses, with Paxton, that "it was the collusion or acquiescence of political elites prepared to lever the fascist leader into power that played the decisive role" — contingency defying any evolutionary law of fascism. Safranski's line stands for what followed: "To Heidegger the National Socialist seizure of power was... a new act of the history of Being... Hitler, to him, meant a new era." (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
