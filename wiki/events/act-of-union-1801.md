# The Act of Union (1801)

The legislation that abolished the separate Kingdom of Ireland's Parliament and merged Ireland into a
new **United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland** from 1 January 1801. Pushed through in the wake of
the [[irish-rebellion-1798|1798 rebellion]] — partly by patronage and bribery — it was promoted by
Pitt with an implied promise of [[catholic-emancipation-1829|Catholic Emancipation]] that George III blocked,
poisoning the Union at its birth.

For the next 120 years the Union defined Irish politics: the constitutional question of whether Ireland
should be governed from Westminster or have its own parliament ran through [[daniel-o-connell|O'Connell's]]
repeal campaign, the [[home-rule-movement|Home Rule]] movement, and ultimately the revolution that
partitioned the island in 1921.

## Historiography

Traditionally narrated within Irish national history as the extinction of an Irish
parliament by bribery and coercion, the Union is increasingly read through the
[[four-nations-british-history|four-nations]] and
[[ireland-and-empire|Ireland-and-empire]] frames as one episode in the repeated
remaking of the composite British state and as a colonial as well as a
constitutional act. The poisoning of the Union by the failure of
[[catholic-emancipation-1829|Catholic Emancipation]] remains central to all
accounts; debate turns on whether 1801 is best seen as integration, annexation, or
a defensive imperial reflex after [[irish-rebellion-1798|1798]]. See the
[[british-national-identity-historiography]] controversy.

## Davies, *The Isles* (1999) — the second remaking of the United Kingdom

[[davies-the-isles-1999|Norman Davies]] uses 1801 as a prime exhibit for the
[[four-nations-british-history|four-nations]] argument that the names and territory
of the state have changed repeatedly and that "Britain" is no fixed container: the
[[union-with-scotland-1707|1707]] "Kingdom of Great Britain" lasted unaltered only
until 1801, when this Act created the larger "United Kingdom of Great Britain and
*Ireland*" — which in turn lasted only until the 1922 secession produced the
present "United Kingdom of Great Britain and *Northern Ireland*." Davies notes that
standard reference works (he singles out the OED) often fail to register these
nineteenth- and twentieth-century changes, perpetuating exactly the conflation of
England / Great Britain / United Kingdom that the four-nations historians attack.
See [[british-isles]] and the [[british-national-identity-historiography]]
controversy.
