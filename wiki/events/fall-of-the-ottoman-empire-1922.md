# The Fall of the Ottoman Empire (1908–1922)

The dissolution of the six-century Ottoman Empire — from the Young Turk revolution of 1908,
through the First World War and the Armenian catastrophe, to the abolition of the sultanate in
1922 and the founding of the Turkish Republic. From Caroline Finkel's
[[finkel-osmans-dream|*Osman's Dream*]] (chs 15–16 + epilogue).

## The Young Turks and the last crises

The **Young Turk revolution of 1908** — led by the **Committee of Union and Progress (CUP)** —
forced [[tanzimat-reforms|Abdülhamid II]] to restore the 1876 constitution; a counter-coup led
to his deposition (1909). But the constitutional era opened amid catastrophe abroad: Italy took
Libya (1911), and the **Balkan Wars (1912–13)** expelled the Ottomans from almost all of
Europe (the recapture of Edirne in 1913 the one defiant success). By 1914 a CUP triumvirate —
**Enver, Talat, and Cemal Pasha** — dominated the state.

## The First World War and the Armenian genocide

The CUP took the empire into the **[[first-world-war-1914|First World War]]** on the German side
(October 1914). The Ottomans fought on four fronts: they repelled the Allies at **Gallipoli
(1915)**, but lost the Arab provinces amid the British-backed **Arab Revolt** and the secret
Allied partition plans (the Sykes–Picot Agreement). Total war devastated the society. Its
gravest event was the **Armenian genocide**: from the **deportation order of 24 May 1915**, the
CUP government's expulsion and massacre of the Ottoman Armenians of Anatolia killed perhaps a
million people — Finkel calls "the Armenian question" the controversy in Ottoman history "least
open to detached debate," and frames the killings against the 1948 Genocide Convention while
noting the role of the clandestine "Special Organization." Defeat came with the collapse of
Bulgaria; the empire signed the **Armistice of Mudros (October 1918)**, and the Allies occupied
Istanbul.

## Resistance, abolition, and the Republic

The punitive **Treaty of Sèvres (1920)** would have reduced the empire to a rump of Istanbul
and northern Anatolia, with Greek, Armenian, and Kurdish zones and restored capitulations. It
provoked a **nationalist resistance** under **Mustafa Kemal**, organized through the Erzurum
and Sivas congresses and the **Grand National Assembly at Ankara (1920)**, fighting the
**War of Independence (1919–22)** against Greek invasion (and Armenian and French forces). The
nationalist victory and the **Armistice of Mudanya (1922)** made the Istanbul sultanate
redundant: in November 1922 the Assembly **abolished the sultanate** (the last sultan, Mehmed
VI, fleeing), the **Treaty of Lausanne (1923)** won Turkey its modern borders and full
sovereignty, and on **29 October 1923 the Republic of Turkey** was founded with Mustafa Kemal
as president. The **caliphate was abolished in 1924**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the long [[eastern-question|Eastern Question]] retreat and the catastrophic
  **Balkan Wars (1912–13)**, which shattered the empire's European base and radicalized the CUP.
- `caused_by:` the **decision to enter WWI on the German side** — the gamble that lost the empire.
- `contributed_to:` the contradictions of [[tanzimat-reforms|reform and autocracy]] — Ottomanism's
  failure to contain Christian nationalisms, and Abdülhamid's pan-Islamic autocracy.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Armenian genocide** and the vast population catastrophes of 1915–22
  (Armenian, Greek, and Muslim).
- `produced:` the **loss of the Arab provinces** and the **modern Middle East state system**
  drawn by Britain and France (the mandates of Syria, Iraq, Palestine, Lebanon, Transjordan).
- `produced:` the **Turkish Republic** — Finkel stresses the **continuities** between empire
  and republic (the centralized, secularizing, bureaucratic state of the
  [[tanzimat-reforms|Tanzimat]] radicalized by Atatürk), against the myth of a *tabula rasa*.
- `enabled:` the persistence of the "[[eastern-question|Sèvres syndrome]]" — a lasting Turkish
  fear of foreign dismemberment.

## Asian Reverberations of the Caliphate's Abolition (Formichi)

Chiara Formichi's [[formichi-islam-and-asia-2020|*Islam and Asia: A History*]] (2020) traces how
the **abolition of the Caliphate in March 1924** — separating religion from politics in the new
Turkish Republic — sent shockwaves across Asian Muslim communities far from Anatolia. In British
India the [[khilafat-movement|Khilafat Movement]] (All-India Khilafat Committee, founded 1919) had
already mobilized Sunni, Shi'i, Ahmadi, and even sympathetic Hindu participants around defending
the Ottoman Caliphate, feeding Gandhi's non-cooperation campaign (1919–21); its momentum collapsed
with the abolition it had fought to prevent. In the Dutch East Indies, [[sarekat-islam|Sarekat
Islam]]'s Central Caliphate Committee (Surabaya, October 1924) drafted a proposal for a
consultative, elected Caliphate for the 1924 Cairo congress, but Indonesian delegates were
conspicuously silent at the 1926 Mecca congress. In Soviet Central Asia, the former Young Turk
leader **Enver Pasha** briefly led the anti-Soviet [[basmachi-movement|Basmachi]] resistance in the
name of pan-Turkic/pan-Islamic revival before his death in battle (August 1922). The 1926 Cairo and
Mecca congresses — with delegates from the Dutch East Indies, India, and the Soviet Union — never
agreed on a successor Caliph, marking the practical end of the transnational Caliphate as an
organizing framework for 20th-century Muslim Asia.

## Significance

The fall of the Ottoman Empire is one of the great **transitions** of the modern world: the end
of the last of the early-modern Islamic empires and the Sunni caliphate, the birth of a secular
nation-state in Turkey, and the creation of the contested **state system of the modern Middle
East**. Finkel ends her "new narrative" not at 1922 but at Atatürk's 1927 speech, insisting on
how much of the empire survived in the republic that disowned it.
