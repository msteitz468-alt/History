# The War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714)

The last great war of **Louis XIV** and the defining coalition conflict of the age of
**[[limited-war|limited war]]** — fought over whether the vast Spanish inheritance would fall to a
Bourbon and unite France and Spain. The **Grand Alliance** of Britain, the Dutch Republic, and Austria
formed to prevent it, in the name of the European **[[balance-of-power|balance of power]]**.

## Narrative

The war ranged across the Low Countries, the Danube, Italy, and Iberia. Its decisive military feature
was the partnership of **[[marlborough-john-churchill|the Duke of Marlborough]]** and **Prince Eugene of
Savoy**, whose victories — above all **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/war-of-the-spanish-succession/battle-of-blenheim|Blenheim]]** (1704), followed by Ramillies, Oudenarde, and Malplaquet — broke the
myth of French invincibility and exhausted Louis XIV's France. The **Peace of Utrecht (1713)** left a
Bourbon on the Spanish throne but **barred the union of the two crowns**, ceded Gibraltar and commercial
privileges to Britain, and confirmed a new balance of power.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **disputed Spanish succession** on the death of the childless Charles II, and the fear
  that a Bourbon Spain would make France hegemonic.
- `contributed_to:` the doctrine of the **[[balance-of-power|balance of power]]** that drove the Grand
  Alliance.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Peace of Utrecht** — the separation of the French and Spanish crowns, **British naval
  and commercial ascendancy** (Gibraltar, the *asiento*), and the checking of French dominance.
- militarily, it was the high point of Doughty & Gruber's argument that **decisive battle could be sought
  within limited war** — see the [[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/war-of-the-spanish-succession/war-of-the-spanish-succession|operational hub]].

## Historiography

The war is exceptionally well documented through Marlborough's own dispatches, parliamentary and diplomatic records, and a long tradition of military history running from contemporary campaign accounts to modern operational studies (Doughty & Gruber's *Warfare in the Western World*, cited here, situates it within the era of "limited war"). A central historiographical debate concerns exactly how "limited" this warfare truly was — the scale of Blenheim, Malplaquet, and the sieges involved casualties and mobilization comparable to later "total" wars, and some historians argue the "limited war" framework, contrasted with the Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars that followed, understates the period's destructiveness. Historians also debate Marlborough's relative credit versus Eugene of Savoy's, and how far the Peace of Utrecht represented a genuine, considered balance-of-power settlement versus a war-weary compromise driven by domestic British politics (the fall of the Whig war ministry).
