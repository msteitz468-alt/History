# Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address (4 March 1865)

Abraham Lincoln's second inaugural speech at the U.S. Capitol — brief, theological, and among the most analyzed texts in American political literature. Ellsworth notes John Wilkes Booth among those present in the throng, knitting the moral climax of Union war rhetoric to the assassination arc.

## Narrative

With Union victory near, Lincoln refused triumphalism. He cast the war as divine judgment for American slavery, shared by North and South, and closed with "malice toward none, with charity for all." The speech set expectations for a reconstruction of national bonds rather than pure vengeance — expectations soon tested by his murder and Johnson's presidency.

- **preceded_by / contributed_to by:** [[events/us-presidential-election-1864]]; military success after Atlanta and continuous pressure on Lee.

## Historiography

Literary and religious readings dominate; political historians debate how far the speech predicted Lincoln's practical Reconstruction policy. Ellsworth's contribution is situational (Booth proximity; wartime capital atmosphere), not a new textual edition.


## Ellsworth Detail (*Midnight on the Potomac*, 2025)

4 March 1865: stormy mud; procession included a full USCT regiment; first inaugural with substantial Black spectators ("Thousands of colored folk… mingled for the first time," Chambrun). Address: 26 sentences; Bible-shared nation; possible divine judgment until slavery's blood-debt paid; closed **"With malice toward none; with charity for all…"** Booth, with a pass via Lucy Lambert Hale, stood **<25 feet** away and later told Chester "what a splendid chance I had to kill the President on the 4th of March."
