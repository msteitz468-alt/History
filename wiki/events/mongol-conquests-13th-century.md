# The Mongol Conquests (13th century)

The **Mongol Conquests** were the campaigns (c. 1206–1279) by which [[genghis-khan|Chinggis
Khan]] and his successors built the **[[mongol-empire|largest contiguous land empire in
history]]** and, in doing so, redrew the political map of Eurasia within two generations.
They are one of the wiki's standing **transition** events: [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy
May]] argues the "Mongol moment" is the dividing line between the pre-modern and modern
ages — the conquests served as the **catalyst** that, via the [[pax-mongolica|Pax
Mongolica]] and the [[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis Exchange]], integrated Afro-Eurasia at
fullest extent.

## Narrative

From his unification of the steppe (1206), Chinggis Khan turned outward — first against
the **Tangut Xixia** and the Jurchen **Jin** in north China, then, after the massacre of
his sponsored caravan at **Otrar** (1218), in the catastrophic destruction of the
**Khwarazmian Empire** (1219–1221). Under **Ögödei** (r. 1229–41) the conquests resumed
on every front: the final defeat of the Jin (1234); **Batu's** great western campaign,
which subdued the **[[rus-principalities|Rus′]]** (Kiev, 1240) and struck into Europe in
the **[[mongol-invasion-of-europe-1241|invasion of 1241]]** (Legnica, Mohi) before its
sudden retreat. Under **Möngke** the empire reached its furthest extent: **Hülegü** sacked
**[[baghdad|Baghdad]]** and ended the [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid Caliphate]] (1258),
while **Khubilai** completed the conquest of the **[[song-dynasty|Song]]** (1279). The
expansion met its limits at the [[mamluk-sultanate|Mamluk]] victory at
**[[battle-of-ain-jalut-1260|Ain Jalut]]** (1260) and in the failed seaborne assaults on
**[[mongol-invasions-of-japan-1274-1281|Japan]]**, Vietnam, and Java.

More than **twenty states vanished** — Xixia, Jin, Song, Kara Khitai, Khwarazm, the
Ismaili "Assassin" state, the Abbasid Caliphate, the Seljuks of Rum, Vladimir-Suzdal,
the Kipchak confederacy, and many more.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[genghis-khan]]` — the organizational and charismatic achievement that
  made a unified steppe army possible (a paradigm case for the Great Man vs. structural
  debate; see [[genghis-khan]]).
- `contributed_to: [[pastoral-nomadism]]` — the steppe military system (decimal army,
  horse-archers, mobility) the Mongols perfected.
- `caused_by: "the decomposition of steppe order"` — the collapse of Liao and the weak
  Jin grip on Mongolia created the "unsettled conditions on a strategic periphery"
  (Arnason) in which a unifier could arise.
- Security and trade motives recur in May's account: several campaigns (Xixia, Otrar)
  were driven by the protection of Mongolia and of [[pax-mongolica|trade]] rather than
  by a master plan of world conquest.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[mongol-empire]]` and its **dissolution into four khanates** (1260–65) —
  the Yuan, the [[ilkhanate|Ilkhanate]], the [[chaghatayid-khanate|Chaghatayid Khanate]],
  and the [[golden-horde|Jochid Khanate/Golden Horde]].
- `produced: [[pax-mongolica]]` → `produced: [[chinggis-exchange]]` — the peace and the
  continental exchange of trade, technology, religion, peoples, and art.
- `enabled: [[black-death]]` — the plague carried west along the routes the conquests
  secured.
- A permanently altered Eurasia: the Turkicization of Anatolia and the western steppe;
  the long Mongol overlordship of the [[rus-principalities|Rus′]] (the rise of Moscow);
  a model of rule claimed by the Timurids, Mughals, and Qing. May: "after the dust
  settled, the world had irrefutably changed and could never return to the way it once was."

## Historiography

The conquests' reputation runs "the gamut from the 'Tatar Yoke' to the Pax Mongolica."
The destruction was real and large (Nishapur, Merv, Baghdad), and nationalist histories
(the Russian "Mongol Yoke," Middle Eastern and Chinese decline narratives) long blamed
the Mongols for arrested development. Recent integrative scholarship — **Allsen**,
**Biran**, **Jackson**, and May himself — stresses instead the constructive legacy of
exchange and connectivity, while warning against the opposite excess of "Mongol mania."
The wiki's fuller historiographical discussion is on [[mongol-empire]].
