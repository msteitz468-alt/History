# Formation of the Triple Alliance (1428)

## Narrative

The **Triple Alliance** of [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]], Texcoco, and Tlacopan (Tacuba) — the political core of the [[aztec-empire|Aztec Empire]] — took shape in the late 1420s out of the collapse of **Tepanec** (Azcapotzalco) hegemony in the [[basin-of-mexico|Basin of Mexico]].

Thomas H. Charlton ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-1|CHNPA II.1]] Ch. 11), following Bray's periodization, places the event at the hinge between **Part I** (c. 1150/1200–1428: post-Tula city-state landscape) and **Part II** (1428–1519: imperial expansion). After the fall of [[tula-hidalgo|Tula]] (c. 1150), Central Mexico fragmented into [[altepetl|city-states]]. From about **1350**, those units were drawn into larger confederacies and tributary empires: the **Tepaneca** at Azcapotzalco and Tenayuca (western Basin) and the **Acolhua** initially from Huexotla and Coatlinchan, later **Texcoco** (east). Mid-fourteenth-century expansion reached beyond the Basin (Azcapotzalco into Mezquital, Toluca, Morelos; Texcoco into eastern Mezquital and initially eastern Morelos), but Davies's judgment is that no region yet could "burst its bounds" to conquer the others; the major structural result was the **conditions** for a new alliance.

Within the Basin, the Acolhua of Texcoco and their allies were **defeated by Azcapotzalco in 1418**. From Tenochtitlan's founding until that war, the **Mexica** had acted as **allies and subordinates of the Tepaneca**, sharing conquest benefits without directing them. When **Tezozomoc**, the long-lived Tepanec ruler, **died in 1427**, war broke out. Allied with the Tepanec tributary **Tlacopan**, the defeated Acolhua, and Acolhua allies **Tlaxcala** and **Huejotzingo**, the Mexica conquered the Tepaneca a year later (**c. 1428**). Out of that victory emerged the Triple Alliance that, almost immediately, launched large-scale public works in the valley and expansionary conquests beyond.

## Causal Analysis

- *preceded_by:* fall of [[tula-hidalgo|Tula]] (c. 1150) and the three-phase Part I city-state landscape (migrations c. 1150–1250; consolidation c. 1250–1350; Acolhua–Tepanec dual expansion c. 1350–1428).
- *contributed_to:* long Tepanec overlordship that trained Mexica military capacity while leaving them junior partners until 1427–28.
- *caused_by:* succession crisis and war after Tezozomoc's death (1427); prior destruction of local noble houses and endemic succession disputes under Azcapotzalco's growth and decline, which reduced capacity for anti-Alliance resistance (Brumfiel).
- *contributed_to:* Early Aztec demographic rebound (Basin ~92–120k → ~250k) and southern [[chinampa-agriculture|chinampa]] intensification as a **necessary threshold**, not a sufficient cause (Charlton).
- *contributed_to:* ideology of war for sacrificial victims (Conrad & Demarest 1984), cited among factors significant for expansion 1430–1519.
- *contrasts_with:* earlier fragile tributary "empires" that foundered on elite competition and shifting loyalties — until 1428, city-state hegemony rested on an "unstable complex of payoffs and alliances" (Brumfiel).

Charlton's synthesis is **multi-causal**: no single prime mover. Particularistic succession problems by 1428 provided the context in which Mexica and allies could capitalize on military, demographic, and organizational structure to fulfill, on a grand scale, the model of a militaristic tributary city-state with Toltec antecedents.

## Consequence Analysis

- *produced:* the Triple Alliance (Tenochtitlan–Texcoco–Tlacopan) as a durable imperial confederacy lasting until the [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|Spanish conquest]].
- *produced:* shift from succession of fragile ephemeral tributary systems to **continued expansion with few setbacks** from 1428 to 1519.
- *enabled:* consolidation of the rest of the Basin (including southern chinampa lands), then tributary relations over large areas of Central and Southern Mexico over ~90 years.
- *produced:* organizational reforms — leadership selection at Tenochtitlan shifting from strictly hereditary to **election within a defined group**; wealth-sharing among nobles adjusted to reduce internal competition; calpixque-supervised **indirect rule** over provinces (local *señores* left in place).
- *produced:* ceramic and economic signatures of post-1428 unity (Aztec III production/distribution consistent with Basin political unity; core–periphery restructuring of craft vs. agriculture within ~30–35 km of Tenochtitlan).
- *followed_by:* Tenochtitlan's rise to **primus inter pares** and effective dominance within the Alliance by 1519; ongoing expansion still incomplete when Spaniards arrived.
- *enabled:* encirclement strategies against strong enemies (Hassig): by 1519 eastern independents fully encircled; similar grander-scale pressure possibly begun against the [[purepecha|Tarascans/Purépecha]].

## Historiography

Charlton's account is primarily **ethnohistoric**, cross-checked against Basin archaeology (Sanders, Parsons, Santley settlement patterns; Early Aztec I/II ceramics reflecting a dual Acolhua–Tepanec landscape **c. 1400**, not 1350; Aztec III as post-unity horizon). Absolute dates are **provisional** and more reliable nearer 1519. He insists that native historical traditions (migrations after Tollan's fall, dynastic wars) are not precise objective histories but can support reconstruction when carefully used, and that **history and archaeology are alternative valid portrayals** that need not fully cohere.

Key secondary authorities he channels: Bray (periodization), Brumfiel (payoffs/alliances; succession crises), Davies (limits of pre-1428 expansion), Conrad & Demarest (sacrifice ideology and empire), Hassig (warfare, perception of power, encirclement), Sanders/Parsons/Santley (settlement and demography). Berdan and other monographs already in the wiki supply ruler-by-ruler detail (Itzcoatl et al.) and the 2/5–2/5–1/5 tribute split not emphasized in Charlton's chapter.

Archaeological caution: the Aztec empire can appear "ephemeral" in the material record of distant provinces (Umberger & Klein) — indirect rule leaves thin imperial footprints outside the Basin core, analogous to debates over Toltec "horizon" control.
