# The Black Death (1347–1351)

The **Black Death** was the catastrophic pandemic that struck Europe and the Mediterranean in
1347–1351, killing perhaps a third or more of the population and inaugurating "a period
characterised by the deadly and repeated attacks of what contemporaries interpreted as a sign of
divine anger." [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Christiane Klapisch-Zuber, Ch. 6; Paul Freedman,
Ch. 4; Jean-Pierre Leguay, Ch. 5) makes it "the most important event" of the fourteenth century,
the hinge of its demographic, economic and social history.

## Narrative

By c. 1300 the European population had "attained its maximum and reached a ceiling" after almost
three centuries of [[medieval-agricultural-revolution|expansion]]. The first half of the century
brought the signs of crisis — above all the **Great Famine** of 1315–1322, when bad weather
ravaged northern Europe (Bruges and Ypres losing perhaps a tenth of their people to hunger in
1316). Then, in mid-century, "the terrible knell of the Black Death sounded." Carried along the
Mongol-era trade routes from Central Asia, the plague reached the Mediterranean ports (the Genoese
and Venetian networks acting as vectors) and swept across the continent in 1347–1351. Some
chroniclers fell silent "as if terror and stupefaction had stopped his speech"; later figures for
the losses are "impossible to credit... other than as a gauge of their fear." The disease then
became **endemic**, returning in repeated outbreaks (notably 1361, and another major epidemic
closing the century) that prevented demographic recovery for generations.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **plague pathogen** carried west from Central Asia along the trade routes that
  the Mongol *Pax* had opened (the "distant yet palpable impact of Asia on fourteenth-century
  Europe," in Jones's framing). [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy May]] traces the specific
  vector: probably out of the central-Eurasian steppe (Issyk Kul, 1338–9), the plague reached the
  [[golden-horde|Jochid]] army besieging the Genoese colony of **Kaffa** in the Crimea (1346),
  where the Mongols — in an early instance of biological warfare — catapulted plague corpses into
  the town; Genoese ships then carried it through the Bosphorus to Messina and the Mediterranean
  (1347–8). For May the plague is "the most deadly result of the Mongol Empire" and the deadliest
  cargo of the **[[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis Exchange]]** — and, recurring in waves, a prime cause
  of the [[mongol-empire|empire's]] own collapse.
- `contributed_to:` a population **already at its ceiling** and, in many regions, **already
  declining before 1347** — Tuscany's population fell "very markedly" in the second quarter of the
  century; northern France and Italy show pre-plague stagnation — so that the plague struck a
  society weakened by the **Great Famine (1315–1322)** and recurrent dearth.

## Consequence Analysis

The Black Death "stimulated profound upheavals in modes of production, living and feeling":

- **Demographic collapse.** A sudden, radical loss of population (commonly estimated at a third or
  more), not recovered until the later fifteenth or sixteenth century.
- **The late-medieval agrarian crisis.** Freedman links the plague to agricultural depression,
  abandoned villages, and the **ruin of much of the aristocracy** as rents and grain prices fell
  while labour grew scarce and dear.
- **Divergent social outcomes.** Labour scarcity raised **real wages** and weakened lordship in
  the west — the **decline of serfdom in England** — while in **eastern Europe** servitude was
  *strengthened* (the "second serfdom"). Attempts to hold wages down (and the resulting tensions)
  fed the **peasant revolts** of the later century (the Jacquerie, the English Rising of 1381).
- **Urban contraction.** Towns suffered "a sharp drop in population levels, barely compensated for
  by the influx of refugees from the countryside" (Leguay), reversing the [[urbanization|thirteenth-century
  urban apogee]].
- **A culture of death.** The *Dance of Death*, the cadaver tomb, and a heightened, anxious piety
  (see [[late-medieval-lay-religion]]).

## Historiography

Klapisch-Zuber and Freedman frame the central debate: whether the plague was an **autonomous,
"providential" accident** that "subverted or renewed the structures of feudal society," or whether
it merely **accelerated a crisis already underway** (the Neo-Malthusian thesis of M.M. Postan: a
society that had outgrown its resources). The two readings shape how the whole century is
understood — and how much of the late-medieval transformation (the end of western serfdom, the
crisis of the Church, the agrarian depression) can be ascribed to the disease versus to
**systemic** factors (climate, the lord–peasant relation, inheritance custom). The chapter
stresses the **fragmentary, elite-biased sources** (wills, hearth-taxes, manorial rolls) that make
medieval demography "high-risk."
