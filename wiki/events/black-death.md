# The Black Death (1347–1351)

The **Black Death** was the catastrophic pandemic that struck Europe and the Mediterranean in
1347–1351, killing perhaps a third or more of the population and inaugurating "a period
characterised by the deadly and repeated attacks of what contemporaries interpreted as a sign of
divine anger." [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Christiane Klapisch-Zuber, Ch. 6; Paul Freedman,
Ch. 4; Jean-Pierre Leguay, Ch. 5) makes it "the most important event" of the fourteenth century,
the hinge of its demographic, economic and social history.

## Narrative

By c. 1300 the European population had "attained its maximum and reached a ceiling" after almost
three centuries of [[medieval-agricultural-revolution|expansion]]. The first half of the century
brought the signs of crisis — above all the **Great Famine** of 1315–1322, when bad weather
ravaged northern Europe (Bruges and Ypres losing perhaps a tenth of their people to hunger in
1316). Then, in mid-century, "the terrible knell of the Black Death sounded." Carried along the
Mongol-era trade routes from Central Asia, the plague reached the Mediterranean ports (the Genoese
and Venetian networks acting as vectors) and swept across the continent in 1347–1351. Some
chroniclers fell silent "as if terror and stupefaction had stopped his speech"; later figures for
the losses are "impossible to credit... other than as a gauge of their fear." The disease then
became **endemic**, returning in repeated outbreaks (notably 1361, and another major epidemic
closing the century) that prevented demographic recovery for generations.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **plague pathogen** carried west from Central Asia along the trade routes that
  the Mongol *Pax* had opened (the "distant yet palpable impact of Asia on fourteenth-century
  Europe," in Jones's framing). [[may-mongol-conquests-2012|Timothy May]] traces the specific
  vector: probably out of the central-Eurasian steppe (Issyk Kul, 1338–9), the plague reached the
  [[golden-horde|Jochid]] army besieging the Genoese colony of **Kaffa** in the Crimea (1346),
  where the Mongols — in an early instance of biological warfare — catapulted plague corpses into
  the town; Genoese ships then carried it through the Bosphorus to Messina and the Mediterranean
  (1347–8). For May the plague is "the most deadly result of the Mongol Empire" and the deadliest
  cargo of the **[[chinggis-exchange|Chinggis Exchange]]** — and, recurring in waves, a prime cause
  of the [[mongol-empire|empire's]] own collapse.
- `contributed_to:` a population **already at its ceiling** and, in many regions, **already
  declining before 1347** — Tuscany's population fell "very markedly" in the second quarter of the
  century; northern France and Italy show pre-plague stagnation — so that the plague struck a
  society weakened by the **Great Famine (1315–1322)** and recurrent dearth.

## Consequence Analysis

The Black Death "stimulated profound upheavals in modes of production, living and feeling":

- **Demographic collapse.** A sudden, radical loss of population (commonly estimated at a third or
  more), not recovered until the later fifteenth or sixteenth century.
- **The late-medieval agrarian crisis.** Freedman links the plague to agricultural depression,
  abandoned villages, and the **ruin of much of the aristocracy** as rents and grain prices fell
  while labour grew scarce and dear.
- **Divergent social outcomes.** Labour scarcity raised **real wages** and weakened lordship in
  the west — the **decline of serfdom in England** — while in **eastern Europe** servitude was
  *strengthened* (the "second serfdom"). Attempts to hold wages down (and the resulting tensions)
  fed the **peasant revolts** of the later century (the Jacquerie, the English Rising of 1381).
- **Urban contraction.** Towns suffered "a sharp drop in population levels, barely compensated for
  by the influx of refugees from the countryside" (Leguay), reversing the [[urbanization|thirteenth-century
  urban apogee]].
- **A culture of death.** The *Dance of Death*, the cadaver tomb, and a heightened, anxious piety
  (see [[late-medieval-lay-religion]]).

## Historiography

Klapisch-Zuber and Freedman frame the central debate: whether the plague was an **autonomous,
"providential" accident** that "subverted or renewed the structures of feudal society," or whether
it merely **accelerated a crisis already underway** (the Neo-Malthusian thesis of M.M. Postan: a
society that had outgrown its resources). The two readings shape how the whole century is
understood — and how much of the late-medieval transformation (the end of western serfdom, the
crisis of the Church, the agrarian depression) can be ascribed to the disease versus to
**systemic** factors (climate, the lord–peasant relation, inheritance custom). The chapter
stresses the **fragmentary, elite-biased sources** (wills, hearth-taxes, manorial rolls) that make
medieval demography "high-risk."

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Scale and ethical stance

Logan insists no other medieval catastrophe matches the plague of **1347–50**; millions died; economic historians debate impact clinically, but pain, suffering, and death are beyond debate—pens should be “shrouded in awe” at the human toll. Older estimate death toll “quarter to a third”; more recent local/regional studies place **40–50%** of Europe; if population ~100 million, then **30–40 million** dead in little over two years—greatest European natural-cause loss before or since. Logan rejects “purgative” excess-population economic framing as morally disconnected from the victims.

### Origin and transmission

Plague endemic to central Asian steppe, outbreak ~1331, pandemic to China, India, west to southern Russia ~1345, Astrakhan 1346, Crimea. Best contemporary focus: **Caffa** (Feodosiya), where Tartar besiegers allegedly catapulted putrefying plague corpses into the Genoese city; Westerners fled; disease-ridden galleys reached **Messina** early October 1347 and Genoa; plague moved through Europe until late 1350 in Scandinavia.

### Causation — *Yersinia pestis*, bubonic and pneumonic

Cause is *Yersinia pestis* in rodent blood and fleas (esp. black rat *Rattus rattus*; flea *Xenopsylla cheopis*). **Bubonic:** buboes in groin/armpit/neck, fever, headaches, often fatal within ~5 days (Boccaccio’s Florence). **Pneumonic:** lung infection, person-to-person via cough/sneeze or flea feces on bedding; blood coughing; death within three days; fear of contagion produced family abandonment.

Boccaccio (Logan quote): “The first signs both in men and women were swellings that appeared either in the groin or in the armpits… Brothers abandoned brothers… and, as difficult as it is to believe, parents abandoned their own children, leaving them uncared for, unvisited, left to their fate like strangers.”

### Geographic progress and regional death figures

Italy late 1347–spring 1348 (majority wiped out at Piacenza, Orvieto, Siena, San Gimignano; Florence estimate 100,000 exaggerated but not wildly off; Naples 63,000; Bologna 60%; Milan up to 15%). Alps to Bavaria June 1348; Austria Nov; Vienna 500+/day next spring; Germany 1349 (Hamburg half said to perish). France via Marseilles 1348; **Avignon** severely hit spring 1348 (letter: ≥half dead; 7,000 vacant houses; 11,000 buried by 14 March in one papal cemetery); Paris >half; London ~1/3 of 40–50,000. England from south coast late June/July 1348; Ireland 1349. Ghost-ship tradition for Norway 1349; Sweden warned 1350. Some regions (parts of Hungary, Flanders, lower Netherlands) largely untouched.

Friar **John Clyn** of Kilkenny left parchment for a survivor to continue his pestilence record; a later hand noted the author apparently died: “Lest this work die with the author, I am leaving space on this parchment for the work to be continued, if anyone should survive and any child of Adam escape this pestilence…”

### Recurrences

Plague recurred 1361 (less severe/widespread but still catastrophic), 1369, 1370s, 1390 (Chaucer’s Pardoner’s Tale), 1405; 1665 London plague as final gasp of the pandemic.

### Contemporary explanations; limited lasting hysteria

Contemporaries blamed God’s wrath, planetary conjunction (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars in Aquarius), and bad humours (Paris doctors). Only remedies: flight and isolation. Logan treats massive psychological trauma as a priori expectation; anecdotes exist, but evidence tends to show fairly rapid survivor adjustment. *Danse macabre* associated by inference with the Black Death’s indiscriminate death. Religious houses recovered to ~75% pre-plague numbers by century’s end; recovery should not blind us to mid-century shock.

### Clergy mortality and pastoral crisis

Large numbers of clergy and religious died roughly proportional to general population; many parish priests acted heroically (Piacenza priest buried next day with last-rites recipient). In France some priests fled, leaving care to regulars; Hôtel-Dieu sisters at Paris nursed without fear. Bishop of Bath and Wells authorized deathbed confession to any lay person even a woman if no priest available. Archbishop of York appointed an Augustinian canon to a secular parish for lack of priests. German sources: one-third of higher clergy (most able to flee) perished; lower clergy higher. Archbishops of Catania (heroic), bishop of Paris, three archbishops of Canterbury died. Religious houses hit hard: 150 Franciscans Marseilles (priory emptied); whole Austin friars priory Avignon; 66 Carmelites Avignon; Coimbra St Peter devastated; Westminster abbot +27 monks; St Albans abbot, prior +46 monks; lone disoriented nun survivor found years later in Lincolnshire. Reliable estimates: **half of English religious died**, friars somewhat more. Clement VI stayed at Avignon through much of the plague season (see [[avignon-papacy]]).

### Anti-Jewish massacres and other scapegoating (full fidelity)

Scapegoats included Muslims in parts of Spain, foreigners (English at Narbonne; foreigners and Portuguese pilgrims in Aragon), and lepers. Far worse: **uncoordinated violent attacks on Jews** charged with **poisoning wells**. Earliest wholesale exterminations spring **1348** at **Narbonne** and **Carcassonne**; September trial of a Jewish doctor who “confessed” importing Spanish poison. Jews burned at **Basel** in wooden buildings; Germany early **1349**: Stuttgart, Memmingen, Lindau, Freiburg, Dresden, Worms, Erfurt, etc.; **Speyer** Jews murdered, corpses placed in wine barrels and dispatched down the Rhine; summer 1349 **Mainz** and **Cologne**. **Clement VI** opened Avignon to fleeing Jews, urged tolerance, threatened persecutors with excommunication; **Pedro IV of Aragon** prosecuted Barcelona perpetrators; German king and Austrian duke largely ineffectual; Cologne city fathers acted. Fury ended only with the plague’s passing. Neither popes nor kings nor civic leaders were able to stop it.

### Flagellant movement 1348–49

Flagellant bands processed village to village (hundreds, hooded, cross-bearer, purple banners, *Stabat Mater*), public scourging with metal-studded thongs after church prostrations; first strongly seen in Germany relative to the Black Death, spread to Poland, Hungary, Flanders, Low Countries; ~120 arrived in England mid-1349 (London processions described). Clement VI had participated in flagellant-like ceremonies at Avignon May 1348 but issued a **condemnation bull October 1349** as plague waned and the movement lost purpose.

### Historiography note (Logan)

Logan’s church-history frame foregrounds pastoral crisis, anti-Jewish violence, flagellants, and purgatory devotion rather than the Neo-Malthusian economic debate alone (already covered above). He insists the human toll not be subordinated to “purgative” surplus-population theses.
