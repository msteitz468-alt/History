## Narrative

The siege of La Paz in 1781 was the centrepiece of the third and southernmost regional hearth of the [[tupac-amaru-rebellion-1780]]. It was directed primarily by [[tupaj-katari]] (Julián Apaza), the charismatic Aymara leader whose forces encircled the city multiple times in one of the longest and most violent sieges in Andean colonial history.

La Paz was a significant colonial city — capital of the Audiencia of Charcas and strategically positioned on the route between the [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí]] silver mines and the Pacific. Its royalist defence was commanded by Lieutenant Colonel Sebastián de Segurola, whose correspondence provides the most detailed chronicle of the siege from the royalist perspective.

By January 1781, while [[tupac-amaru-jose-gabriel|Túpac Amaru II]] was still besieging Cusco, the La Paz region was already in open insurgency. Segurola wrote on January 27 to his superior Rueda: "The rebel [Túpac Amaru II] once again is trying to reach these places and I wish to inform you sincerely that the spirit of rebellion is general and common in all the provinces and in the city, and that it is not confined only to the number of Indians but overreaches these limits to touch many who do not hold themselves to be such." This testimony underscores the cross-ethnic spread of rebel sentiment.

The rebel forces around La Paz combined Katari's Aymara mass mobilisation with the symbolic Inka authority of Andrés Túpac Amaru (nephew of the executed rebel king), who held supreme command in the La Paz region. Andrés's romance with Gregoria Apaza, Katari's sister, wove a familial link across the Quechua-Aymara axis of the insurgency. Miguel Bastidas (relative of Micaela Bastidas, Túpac Amaru II's widow) and Mariano Túpac Amaru (the rebel couple's young son) were also present as Inka leaders in La Paz, lending dynastic legitimacy to the Aymara-based siege.

The rebel mass recruited heavily from Umasuyo Qulla communities of the Titicaca highlands — a population with historical overlap with the Canas and Canchis Quechua groups that had formed Túpac Amaru II's original base, because all derived from related Aymara ethnic stocks. This gave the La Paz siege a broader ethnic base than the Cusco revolt, even as it was more thoroughly Aymara in character.

## Causal Analysis

The Catarista insurgency had an independent genesis, predating the Cusco revolt. Julián Apaza and Tomás Catari "had ignited a series of Bolivian rebellions from 1776 on — that is, well before the events in Tinta. No evidence exists for preliminary contact between the two movements." In 1780, while Túpac Amaru II besieged Cusco, Tomás Catari was already "in the midst of a local conflagration with the miners and the *corregidores* of the Potosí region." What joined the movements was the ideological resonance of Inka messianism and shared economic grievances — the *mita* burden, the *reparto*, and the dissolution of communal tribute. The force of events, not prior conspiracy, pushed the Cataris into nominal alliance with the Amarus.

## Outcome and Consequences

Katari refused to accept the armistice that Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru eventually negotiated at Sicuani in 1782, continuing to fight after the Cusco and southern revolts were suppressed. He was captured and executed in November 1781. The armistice, the arrival of Spanish reinforcements, and the execution of rebel leaders extinguished the insurgency, though royal control over the altiplano remained fragile for years.

The siege demonstrated that the Great Rebellion had transcended its Cusco origins to become a genuinely pan-Andean crisis capable of threatening major urban centres. La Paz's experience — the rebel investment, the royalist spy network intercepting correspondence, Segurola's role as both military commander and chronicler — left an exceptionally rich archival record studied by María Eugenia del Valle de Siles.

## Historiography

Primary sources for the siege include Segurola's correspondence and edicts as well as documents intercepted from rebel traffic. Del Valle de Siles (*Testimonios del cerco de La Paz*, 1980; *Historia de la rebelión de Túpac Catari*, 1990) is the leading authority. Luis Durand edited an important anthology: *La revolución de los Túpac Amaru* (Lima, 1981). Glave (Ch. 22 of [[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v3-2]]) stresses the independence of the Catarista movements and the imperfect programmatic agreements between the Catari and Amaru coalitions even at the command level.
