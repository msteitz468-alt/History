# Operation Typhoon (1941)

Final German 1941 push to seize Moscow (part of Barbarossa). Army Group Center (Bock) made deep advances but was halted by Soviet reserves, mud, and then intense winter. Zhukov's counteroffensive (Dec 5 onward) marked the first time the Wehrmacht was forced into major retreat. Symbol of the failure of the German plan to knock the USSR out in one campaign.

See: [[operation-barbarossa-1941]], [[battle-of-moscow-1941]], [[adolf-hitler]], [[zhukov-georgy]].

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Typhoon launched 2 October 1941; by 20 October German armor was within 40 miles of Moscow and Soviet ministries evacuated to Kuibyshev. Autumn rains (*rasputitsa*) mired the advance from mid-October — Blumentritt: "the next few weeks were dominated by the mud... All wheeled vehicles sink up to their axles in the slime." The closest German penetration came on 2 December 1941, when a reconnaissance battalion of the 258th Infantry Division reached Khimki, a Moscow suburb within sight of the Kremlin spires, before being driven out the next morning — "the nearest the German troops ever got to Moscow" (Shirer 1960). Extreme cold is documented via Guderian's diary: first snow 6–7 Oct, first cold wave 3 Nov, first severe frostbite 7 Nov, −8°F by 13 Nov, −31°F by 4 Dec, with tanks "almost immobilized," telescopic sights useless, machine guns failing to fire.

Zhukov's counteroffensive of 6 December 1941 (100 divisions, seven armies, two cavalry corps, winter-equipped) broke the German drive; Halder's diary records: "The myth of the invincibility of the German Army was broken." Rundstedt was dismissed on 1 December 1941 after ordering a retreat from Rostov against Hitler's "remain where you are" order — his own account: "I immediately wired back: 'It is madness to attempt to hold... I repeat that this order be rescinded or that you find someone else.' That same night the Fuehrer's reply arrived: 'I am acceding to your request. Please give up your command.'" [[brauchitsch-walther-von|Brauchitsch]] was replaced as Army Commander-in-Chief on 19 December 1941 by Hitler personally, who told Halder: "The task of the Commander in Chief of the Army is to train the Army in a National Socialist way. I know of no general who could do that, as I want it done. Consequently, I've decided to take over command of the Army myself." Hitler privately dismissed Brauchitsch to Goebbels (diary, 20 March 1942) as "A vain, cowardly wretch... and a nincompoop."

[SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer presents both sides of the "stand fast, no retreat" controversy: Blumentritt and von Tippelskirch judged Hitler's no-withdrawal order his "one great achievement" of the war, preventing dissolution into a Napoleonic-style rout since troops caught in the open in deep snow "would simply lie down and die"; but most other generals argued a timely, orderly withdrawal would have saved men and equipment for a stronger defensive line. Shirer does not adjudicate between these positions.

## Historiography

German operational records (Halder, Bock diaries; OKW war diary) dominate the source base, with Soviet archival correctives post-1991. Debates: the weather-vs-logistics-vs-Soviet-resistance weighting of the failure; whether the August Kiev diversion ([[battle-of-kiev-1941]]) or the late-September start doomed the drive; Stahel's argument that culmination preceded the mud. Shirer supplies the contemporary German-side documentary texture and the dismissal cascade (Brauchitsch, Bock, Guderian, Rundstedt). Collection coverage: strong (West Point history/atlases, Evans, CHWWII, Shirer).
