# The Building of Edo (1604–1614)

The decade-long construction of [[tokugawa-shogunate|Edo castle]] and the city around it was one of
the largest coordinated public-works projects in Japanese history, and simultaneously an instrument
of political control: the mobilization of outside lords (tozama daimyo) as forced labor drained their
resources and left them perpetually indebted to the [[tokugawa-shogunate|bakufu]]. Source:
[[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Narrative

In 1602 Edo had already suffered its first fire, prompting Ieyasu to order tile or board roofing
in place of thatch — the first recorded use of roof tiles in the city. In **July 1604** Ieyasu
issued formal orders to all daimyo to provide labor and materials for the castle and city
reconstruction.

**Stone supply.** The most demanding element was the stone facing for moats and ramparts, quarried
in Izu. For each 100,000 koku of income a daimyo was assessed 1,120 "hundred-man stones" (stones
requiring one hundred men to handle). Three thousand ships were needed to transport the stone;
Shimazu of Satsuma alone provided three hundred ships plus a large cash donation. Ships made two
round trips monthly between Izu and Edo.

**Land reclamation.** To create usable urban ground, Ieyasu ordered the leveling of Kanda Hill
(Surugadai) and the transport of its earth to fill the two-mile-square sea swamp between Fukagawa
and Hibiya. The daimyo were divided into ten companies, one man per 1,000 koku of income —
perhaps 10,000 men in total. The filled area became the commercial quarter; the least desirable
portions were designated for the amusement district.

**Nihon-bashi.** Built during this period, the bridge was 226 feet 5 inches long and 26 feet 5
inches wide. Its name ("Japan Bridge") reflected the fact that daimyo from across Japan had
contributed to its construction; it became the measured center from which all road distances in
Japan were calculated.

**The castle itself.** Work proceeded in phases:
- 1604–1606: moats, ramparts, the main castle (Hommaru), Ni-no-Maru, San-no-Maru
- 1606: great keep begun; Todo Takatora rewarded 20,000 koku for supervision
- 1607: northern daimyo requisitioned (Date, Uesugi, Mori and others); ramparts raised from 48 to
  60 feet; outer moat widened; Ote-mon (Main Gate) and Chushaku Gate built
- 1611: another phase under Date Masamune's superintendence; Nishi Maru completed
- 1613–1614: final western mobilization (Kyushu, Shikoku, Sanyodo, Sanindo lords); Hosokawa Tadaoki
  built the upper stone rampart of the Main Castle; Shimazu built the high wall around it

Simultaneously, between 1602 and 1614, additional castles were built or reconstructed at Zese,
Fushimi, Hikone, Nagahama, Sumpu (Shizuoka), and Takada. By 1614 the total fortified perimeter of
Edo was approximately five miles in circumference, fifty acres in area.

**Water supply.** Okubo Mondo Tadayuki constructed aqueducts from a dam at Mejiro Hill, channeling
the Takada river through Kobinata, Koishikawa, Yushima, and along Kanda Hill to the northwest
districts. The Akasaka reservoir supplied southeastern districts.

**Labor and regulation.** Work sites posted notices requiring dismounting, forbidding long swords,
and prohibiting smoking — Ieyasu had issued an edict against tobacco (alongside James I and Akbar
of India), though the bakufu eventually abandoned the prohibition. Hidetada personally inspected
progress morning and evening, resting in purpose-built tearooms along the route.

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause was [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]]'s 1603 appointment as shogun and Edo's elevation
to administrative capital. The deeper strategic cause was the need to exhaust tozama daimyo wealth.
As Sadler notes, "the building of the castle was a crushing burden on these lords," evidenced by
letters from retainers such as Masuda Motoyoshi, who wrote: "Of late Ogosho Sama has been
short-tempered, and I am very apprehensive." Stone prices in Edo rose with demand; Asano complained
that "a hundred-man stone costs twelve pieces of silver now." Ships were lost to storms — "a hundred
of Nabeshima's, forty-six of Kato Yoshiaki's, thirty of Kuroda's" — at further loss to the lords.

## Consequence Analysis

The construction achieved two intertwined results. First, it produced the largest and most
formidable castle complex in Japan. Second, it systematically impoverished tozama daimyo through
mandatory unpaid corvée, making armed resistance against the Tokugawa materially impossible for a
generation. The daimyo also began keeping residences in Edo, their families effectively serving as
hostages — the germ of the later sankin-kōtai system.

## Actors

- **[[tokugawa-ieyasu]]** — overall director; personally set quality standards and enforced them
- **Tokugawa Hidetada** — day-to-day superintendent; inspected works daily
- **Todo Takatora** — energetic chief supervisor; rewarded 20,000 koku fief in Bichu
- **Date Masamune** — supervised 1611 northern phase on Nishi Maru
- **Kuroda Nagamasa** — built large part of the great keep
- **Hosokawa Tadaoki, Shimazu, Nabeshima Katsushige** — 1614 phase leaders for Main Castle and walls

## Historiography

Sadler's account draws on surviving letters from daimyo councilors (e.g., Masuda Motoyoshi of Mori
Terumoto) and on castle-construction chronicles preserved in domain annals. The letters provide
unusually candid testimony of the financial and psychological burden the corvée imposed, making
this one of the better-documented early Edo construction episodes. The dual function of the project
— urban infrastructure and political instrument — is a standard interpretation in Tokugawa
scholarship, though older accounts tended to emphasize the construction achievement at the expense
of its coercive dimension.
