# Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (1798)

In response to the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, the Kentucky and Virginia legislatures passed resolutions (drafted secretly by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison) declaring the acts unconstitutional and asserting the right of states to judge federal legislation.

## Narrative

Jefferson drafted the Kentucky Resolutions (passed in 1798, with a follow-up in 1799). Madison authored the Virginia Resolutions. They argued that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states, that the federal government had only delegated powers, and that states could "interpose" to prevent the execution of unconstitutional federal laws.

The resolutions were sent to other states (most of which rejected them) and became important campaign documents for the Republicans.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Alien and Sedition Acts, which Republicans viewed as a direct assault on the First Amendment and political opposition.

**contributed_to:** Jefferson's and Madison's shared view that the Federalists were consolidating power in ways that threatened republican government.

## Consequence Analysis

The resolutions helped frame the election of 1800 as a contest over liberty and constitutional principles. After the Republican victory, the immediate crisis passed, but the compact theory they advanced had a long afterlife in American constitutional thought, including in the nullification crisis and later sectional conflicts.

## Actors

- Thomas Jefferson (primary author of the Kentucky Resolutions)
- James Madison (author of the Virginia Resolutions)
- John Adams administration (target of the protest)

## Historiography

Malone carefully distinguishes Jefferson's stronger original language from the final versions and from Madison's more moderate position. He presents the resolutions as a defensive measure to preserve the "spirit of 1776" rather than a blueprint for disunion. Later historians have debated how much they prefigured nullification and secession.
