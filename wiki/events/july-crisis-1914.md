# July Crisis 1914

The five-week diplomatic and military crisis from the assassination of Archduke **Franz Ferdinand** in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 to the outbreak of general European war in early August. It transformed a Balkan quarrel into a continental (and then global) war through the interaction of alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, and decision-making under time pressure.

## Narrative

On 28 June 1914, heir-apparent Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie were assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb student associated with Young Bosnia and supported logistically by elements of Ujedinjenje ili Smrt ("Black Hand," led by Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijovic/Apis of the Serb intelligence service). The plot used weapons supplied via Serb army officers; Princip had crossed from Serbia. The Serb government under Nikola Pasic was aware of arms traffic risks but politically constrained by the army and nationalist opinion; it did not issue a specific warning to Vienna and treated the act as an internal Habsburg matter.

Austria-Hungary, convinced of Serbian complicity and determined that inaction would mean further decline, used the assassination as the occasion for a "final and fundamental reckoning" with Serbia. Foreign Minister Leopold Berchtold and Chief of the General Staff Franz Conrad von Hötzendorff (long advocate of preventive war against Serbia) drove policy. After securing a "blank cheque" of support from Germany (5–6 July), Austria delivered a severe ultimatum to Serbia on 23 July (timed after Poincaré and Viviani left St Petersburg). Serbia's reply accepted most terms but not Austro-Hungarian participation in internal investigations. Austria broke relations within fifteen minutes, mobilized against Serbia, and declared war on 28 July (bombardment of Belgrade).

Russia, viewing Austria-Hungary as Germany's stalking-horse and fearing loss of great-power status in the Balkans (humiliated in 1909 Bosnian crisis), moved from partial to general mobilization (Tsar approved general on 30 July after initial partial on 29th). France affirmed alliance solidarity. Germany, having issued the blank cheque expecting localization, faced Russian mobilization that threatened its war plan (concentrate first against France). Germany declared Kriegsgefahr on 31 July, general mobilization 1 August, declared war on Russia 1 August and France 3 August. Britain, after cabinet debate, affirmed naval obligations to France and used Belgian neutrality (violated by German ultimatum 2 August) as the unifying justification for war on Germany on 4 August.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` assassination (immediate trigger) and long-standing Austro-Serb rivalry intensified by Bosnian annexation 1908 (which "humiliated" Russia and confirmed Austrian dependence on Germany) and Balkan Wars 1912–13 (Serbia doubled in size; Austria's preferred outcomes repeatedly frustrated).

`contributed_to:` 
- Domestic-foreign linkage in Austria-Hungary ("if any state manifested a close connection between domestic policy and foreign policy... it was... Austria-Hungary").
- German constitutional tensions and failed Sammlungspolitik producing fatalism and militarized diplomacy.
- Naval and land arms races (Tirpitz programme, 1913 German army law, Russian "grand programme") that made readiness the measure of policy.
- Alliance rigidities and staff talks that turned Balkan quarrel into two-front war.
- Mobilization timetables (German plan abandoned Russia-only option in 1913; Russian partial mobilization technically unworkable).

No power followed a consistent grand design. The German "blank cheque" was "indeed blank"; Bethmann Hollweg pursued localization until 28/29 July then shifted; Moltke's preventive-war advocacy was fatalistic rather than calculated. Conrad pushed "va banque." British entry bridged Realpolitik and Gladstonian liberalism via Belgium. `preceded_by:` Moroccan crises (hardened Entente), Liman von Sanders affair (Russo-German antagonism).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Austrian declaration of war on Serbia; Russian general mobilization; German and French mobilizations and declarations; British entry; the First World War itself (see [[first-world-war-1914]]).

`enabled:` collapse of the Concert system; immediate global extension via colonies, navies, and Ottoman entry later in 1914.

`contributed_to:` destruction of four empires; Russian Revolution; Versailles order.

## Historiography

Strachan (this range) rejects simple Fischer continuity or pure sleepwalking: structures created the conditions for fatalism and time pressure, but July decisions were incremental, often improvised, and shaped by prior crises and individuals. The crisis militarized foreign policy ("decision for peace or war was made conditional not on the objectives of policy but on the state of military readiness"). Economic interdependence did not prevent war; popular images of short, decisive war proved insufficient for deterrence. Later accounts (Clark *Sleepwalkers*) stress distributed responsibility and contingency; Fischer school emphasizes German expansionism and domestic crisis management. Strachan's synthesis treats the July Crisis as the point where long-range factors (alliances shaped by Weltpolitik setbacks, arms races, nationality problems) met immediate agency and accident (Sarajevo driver error, Princip's position).

## A Longer View of the Causes of War (Paxton)

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton and Hessler]] (ch. 2) organize the long-range causal debate into five overlapping theories that complement the Strachan/Clark historiography above without contradicting it. First, **sovereignty and national honor**: the system of sovereign states offered "no higher tribunal for arbitration or mediation when any two states collided," and Kaiser Wilhelm II himself wrote in the margin of a memorandum that "in vital questions, and those of honor, one does not consult with others." Nearly every European statesman in 1914 accepted that war to save a state from decline was legitimate — General Friedrich von Bernhardi's 1912 essay framed Germany's choice starkly as "World Power or Decline" — and recent precedent cut both ways: restraint had produced humiliation (Russia over Bosnia in 1908, Germany in the second Moroccan crisis of 1911, Austria in the Balkan Wars), while brinkmanship had "worked" for France in Morocco in 1911.

Second, the **imperialist/commercial rivalry thesis** associated with Lenin's *Imperialism: The Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917). Paxton grants that imperial and commercial competition "unquestionably helped shape the alignments of August 1914" — the 1905 and 1911 Moroccan crises hardened French nationalism and military spending, and the Anglo-German naval race forced Britain to lean on French naval cover in the Mediterranean — but rejects the thesis as sufficient: Germany and Britain remained each other's best trading partners and were still jointly building the Berlin-to-Baghdad Railway as late as June 1914, London's bankers and merchants opposed war during the crisis itself, and Britain and Germany cooperated colonially in Africa and the Far East even as Britain's rivalries with France and Russia over colonial spheres (Africa, the Near East, Iran, Afghanistan) proved far more bitter without predicting the actual 1914 alignment.

Third, the **internal-dissent or social-control thesis** associated with Arno Mayer — the idea that fear of domestic revolution pushed leaders toward external war. Domestic strife was rising sharply everywhere in 1914: unprecedented French strikes in 1906–09 and a Left plurality in the 1914 elections, the German Social Democrats as the Reichstag's largest party since 1912, Italy's bloody "Red Week" of June 1914, Russian dread of a repeat of 1905, and in Britain a simultaneous suffragette campaign, a strike wave building toward an August general strike, and Ulster unionist threats of civil disobedience over Irish home rule. Foreign Minister Sazonov told the tsar that failing to back Serbia risked "revolution and perhaps the loss of his throne," and Asquith privately noted that the Balkan crisis might usefully throw "into the background the lurid picture of civil war in Ulster." Paxton stops short of a determinist reading — "nor did any European statesman manufacture an international emergency for purely internal purposes" — but suggests some leaders took risks more readily believing that foreign success strengthens a ruler at home.

Fourth, **alliance rigidity**. What changed after 1890 was not the wording of treaties but the psychological dependence of each power on an ally's continued strength: Bismarck had kept good relations with both Russia and Austria simultaneously, but his successors tied Germany exclusively to Austria-Hungary (and a loosening Italy), so that by 1914 Germany "felt quite alone with Austria-Hungary" and bound to its fate. France could not face Germany alone without Russia and so could not afford to look hesitant when Russia mobilized; Britain, bound by no explicit treaty, could not imagine security without a strong, friendly France.

Fifth, the **war machines and mobilization timetables**. European military budgets roughly doubled between 1890 and 1914 (to nearly 5% of national income — modest by the standards of 1937's ~10% or the Cold War 1950s' 13–15% of GNP, Paxton notes), and rail-based mass mobilization required minute pre-planning that made improvisation "likely to produce chaos." This logic forced Russian general mobilization on 30 July (the general staff insisted partial mobilization against Austria alone was technically unworkable), forced German adherence to the [[schlieffen-plan]] once Russia mobilized (Moltke could not wait to learn French intentions before committing forces), and pushed the French covering force forward on 30 July; even Churchill, as First Lord of the Admiralty, quietly kept the British fleet concentrated after its July maneuvers rather than dispersing it as usual.

On the British decision specifically, Paxton adds detail on the strategic calculus behind Sir Edward Grey's diplomacy. Senior Foreign Office diplomat **Sir Eyre Crowe**'s memorandum of 25 July framed the logic starkly: if France and Russia lost without British help, "what would then be their attitude toward England? What about India and the Mediterranean?"; if Germany won, "what would be the position of a friendless England?" Paxton argues that Britain's decision was not really about the "scrap of paper" — the 1839 Belgian neutrality treaty, as Bethmann Hollweg furiously characterized it — but about the Empire's global position; Belgian neutrality became the *public* justification that rallied opinion, not the underlying reason for war. A contributing structural factor was the 1912 decision to concentrate the Royal Navy in the Channel, leaving the Mediterranean to the French fleet, which had already made Britain dependent on French naval cooperation for access to India and Middle Eastern oil (the Royal Navy's conversion from coal to oil had begun in 1911).
