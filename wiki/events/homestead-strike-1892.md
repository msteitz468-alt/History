# Homestead Strike 1892

Violent labor conflict at the Homestead steel works of Carnegie Steel Company, June–November 1892. One of the most famous and consequential strikes in American history, it crystallized the power imbalance between organized capital and labor in the Gilded Age and severely damaged Andrew Carnegie's reputation.

## Narrative

In 1892 the contract between Carnegie Steel and the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers at Homestead was due to expire. The company, under managing partner Henry Clay Frick (while Carnegie was in Scotland), demanded substantial wage reductions and refused to recognize the union's claim to represent all workers. Frick prepared for a lockout, built a fence around the works ("Fort Frick"), and arranged for 300 Pinkerton agents to be brought in by barge on the Monongahela River to protect strikebreakers and restart operations.

On July 6, 1892, as the Pinkertons attempted to land, a battle erupted with armed strikers and townspeople. At least ten men were killed (three Pinkertons, seven workers) and many wounded. The Pinkertons surrendered and were marched through a hostile crowd. The state sent in the Pennsylvania National Guard to restore order and protect non-union workers. The strike was eventually broken; the union was eliminated at Homestead and its influence in the Carnegie mills largely destroyed. Many strikers were blacklisted. The twelve-hour day became standard.

Carnegie publicly claimed he had favored a more conciliatory approach and was distressed by the violence, but private correspondence and Frick's actions showed alignment on the goal of breaking the union. The "Gospel of Wealth" author who had once written favorably of labor organizations was now indelibly linked with the use of armed force against workers.

## Causal Analysis

Immediate causes were economic: post-1890s price competition and overcapacity in steel pushed producers to cut costs, with labor the most controllable expense. Structural: the Amalgamated had won strong contracts in good times; Carnegie/Frick wanted a free hand to modernize and control the workforce. Frick's personal style (rigid, anti-union) contrasted with Carnegie's more public flexibility. Carnegie's physical absence allowed Frick to execute the hard line while Carnegie later distanced himself rhetorically.

## Consequence Analysis

Homestead marked a decisive defeat for craft unionism in the steel industry. For the next four decades, major steel firms (including U.S. Steel after 1901) remained largely non-union. It became a symbol, alongside Pullman and Ludlow, of the violent suppression of labor organizing. For Carnegie personally, it created an enduring contradiction between his philanthropic self-presentation and his business record that biographers and historians have wrestled with ever since. Nationally, it helped fuel the Populist and Progressive critiques of concentrated industrial power.

## Historiography

Contemporary accounts split along class lines. Nasaw's biography uses new correspondence to show that Carnegie and Frick were largely in agreement on the anti-union objective, even if Carnegie preferred to avoid public confrontation. The event remains central to labor history narratives of the Gilded Age and to assessments of Carnegie's legacy. Debates continue over the extent of Carnegie's foreknowledge and responsibility versus Frick's operational control.
