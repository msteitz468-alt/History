# Dissolution of the German Parties (1933)

Between May and 14 July 1933 every German political party other than the NSDAP
was banned, dissolved itself under duress, or was absorbed — completing the
one-party state. Source: Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]], Part 5).

## The SPD: Hounded Out of Existence

On 10 May the government seized the party's assets by court order on a
fabricated embezzlement pretext (Otto Wels had already shipped funds and
archive abroad). On 17 May the remaining SPD deputies under Paul Löbe — fearing
the "unpatriotic" label — voted for Hitler's disarmament-equality resolution;
the exile leadership condemned it as the negation of the proud vote of 23
March; Toni Pfülf, one of the party's leading women deputies, boycotted the
session and killed herself on 10 June. On 21 June Frick ordered the states to
ban the party under the Reichstag fire decree. The following days brought the
"Köpenick Blood-Week": after armed resistance from one house, stormtroopers
rounded up 500 Social Democrats and beat and tortured them for days, killing
91. Individual murders continued through the summer: Felix Fechenbach
(Eisner's former secretary) taken from prison on 8 August, driven into a wood
and shot ("shot while trying to escape"); Johannes Stelling, former
Minister-President of Mecklenburg-Schwerin, tortured to death, his body sewn
into a weighted sack and thrown into a river with twelve other murdered SPD
and Reichsbanner men. At the Dürrgoy camp near Breslau the brownshirt Edmund
Heines paraded the SPD district administrator Hermann Lüdemann through the
city dressed as a harlequin. The formal ban of 14 July covered a party already
destroyed. Evans's verdict (attributed): the decisive failure was 20 July
1932 — "if there had been any moment when it might have stood up for
democracy, that was it" — though he credits the leaders' desire to avoid
shedding workers' blood.

## The Centre: Sacrificed for the Concordat

Terror from mid-February (twenty papers banned; Stegerwald beaten at Krefeld;
mass dismissals of Centre civil servants) combined with negotiation: Kaas at
the Vatican proved willing to sacrifice the party for the Concordat;
[[bruning-heinrich|Brüning]], succeeding him in May, presided over a party
that had converted its elected mandates into appointive ones. The Fulda
bishops welcomed the "national awakening" (1 June); Himmler ordered all
Bavarian People's Party deputies into "protective custody" (26 June); Eugen
Bolz — the Württemberg State President Hitler had savaged in his 15 February
Stuttgart speech — was arrested and severely beaten (19 June). The Concordat
text (agreed 1 July) banned priests from political activity; the party
dissolved itself on 5 July. Evans's structural point: Bismarck's persecution
had taught the SPD permanent opposition but taught the Catholics
reintegration-at-almost-any-price; leading Centre politicians (Papen, and to a
lesser degree Brüning and Bolz) no longer carried the democratic commitment of
Erzberger's generation. Clemens von Galen's consecration as Bishop of Münster
(28 October 1933), with SA lining the episcopal procession, sealed the
"reconciliation" — see [[german-church-struggle-1933-1939]].

## The Liberals

The State Party (ex-Democrats, 5 seats via the SPD list, including Theodor
Heuss) voted for the Enabling Act "cowed by Hitler's threat of a bloodbath,"
was stripped of its seats on the pretext its deputies were Social Democrats,
and dissolved on 28 June. The People's Party — post-Stresemann, drifted far
right — dissolved on 4 July; Hitler's assurance of non-discrimination for its
members was not honored.

## The Nationalists: The Coalition Partner Devoured

The DNVP expected to be senior partner; by March the Nazis no longer needed
them. The Oberfohren affair (the party's floor leader, who suspected Nazi
arson of the Reichstag, resigned after house raids and was found dead on 7 May
— the suicide verdict widely disbelieved); Carl Schmitt's doctrine that the
Enabling Act's "present government" meant the new *kind* of government, not
the ministers, emptied Hugenberg's resignation threat; Hitler's 30 May
"hysterical outburst" threatened to let his "SA open fire and arrange a
bloodbath lasting three days long... until there's nothing left" unless the
Nationalist paramilitaries (100,000 strong) dissolved. Hindenburg did nothing.
Hugenberg resigned 26–27 June; the party signed a "Friendship Agreement" of
self-dissolution — "little more than abject surrender" (Evans). The Steel
Helmets had already been neutralized: Seldte joined the NSDAP on 26 April,
Duesterberg was dismissed, and the organization was forcibly incorporated
into the SA at the end of May.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: the one-party state (Law against the New Formation of Parties,
  14 July 1933). Goebbels, 28 June: "The road to the total state. Our
  revolution has an uncanny dynamism."
- Evans's summary datum: the destroyed KPD+SPD together represented nearly a
  third of the electorate — more than the Nazis ever won in a free election;
  they "crumbled virtually without resistance."

## Historiography

Evans's treatment stresses the interlocking of terror and legal facade, and
the differential milieu responses (SPD opposition-culture vs. Catholic
reintegration-culture) as products of Bismarck-era persecution. The Concordat
bargain remains contested terrain in the church-complicity literature (see
[[german-church-struggle-1933-1939]]).
