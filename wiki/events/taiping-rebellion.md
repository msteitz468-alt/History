# The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864)

The vast civil war that ravaged [[qing-dynasty|Qing China]] for fourteen years — a millenarian
uprising led by a man who believed himself the **younger brother of Jesus Christ** — and, with
perhaps **20–30 million dead**, one of the deadliest conflicts in human history. From Jonathan
Spence's [[spence-search-for-modern-china|*The Search for Modern China*]] (Spence also wrote a
dedicated study, *God's Chinese Son*).

## Hong Xiuquan and the God Worshippers

The movement's distinctive shape came from **Hong Xiuquan** (1814–64), a **Hakka** villager of
Guangdong who repeatedly **failed the civil-service examinations**. After a fourth failure, he
reinterpreted earlier visions — of a golden-bearded man who gave him a sword and a younger
"Elder Brother" — in light of Christian missionary tracts, concluding that he was the **Son of
God and younger brother of Jesus**, charged to slay demons. Preaching publicly and smashing
Confucian and ancestral shrines, he gathered a **Society of God Worshippers** in rugged Guangxi.
By 1850 his ~20,000 recruits drilled troops, pooled property, banned opium and "sensuality,"
abandoned the Manchu queue (growing their hair long — hence "long-haired rebels"), and
segregated the sexes.

## The Heavenly Kingdom

After defeating Qing forces, Hong proclaimed himself **Heavenly King of the Taiping Tianguo**
("Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace") in 1851. The movement swept north, swelling to hundreds of
thousands, and in **1853 captured Nanjing**, which Hong made his capital and ruled until 1864.
Taiping policy was "startlingly radical": a **common treasury**, asceticism (bans on opium,
prostitution, alcohol), Bible-based examinations, and a notable (if limited) elevation of
**women** — who held office and sat their own examinations. The fusion of heterodox
Christianity, anti-Manchu ethnic appeal, and social radicalism made it far more than a bandit
rising.

## Suppression and toll

The Qing regular forces proved incapable; the rebellion was finally crushed by **regional
armies** raised by provincial Confucian gentry — above all **Zeng Guofan's** Hunan Army —
supported by foreign-officered units (the "Ever-Victorious Army"). Nanjing fell in **1864**;
Hong died (probably by suicide). The human cost was staggering — perhaps **20 million or more**
killed — and central China was devastated.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **social dislocation** of the post-[[opium-wars|Opium War]] Qing crisis —
  population pressure, economic disruption, banditry, and ethnic tension (the marginalized
  Hakka).
- `caused_by:` **Hong Xiuquan's** charismatic heterodox-Christian message and the weakness of
  Qing authority in the south.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` catastrophic **mortality and devastation** in the Yangzi heartland.
- `produced:` the **rise of regional/provincial military power** — the gentry-led armies (Zeng
  Guofan, Li Hongzhang) that saved the dynasty also shifted power toward the provinces, a key
  step toward the warlordism and provincial autonomy that would follow the
  [[fall-of-the-qing-1911|dynasty's fall]].
- `contributed_to:` the [[self-strengthening|self-strengthening movement]] (the reformers who
  led the suppression also led the reform) and the long-run **weakening of the Qing**.

## Significance

The Taiping is the central convulsion of the Qing crisis — evidence of how deeply the
nineteenth-century dislocation had shaken Chinese society, and a foreshadowing of the
**revolutionary upheavals** (anti-Manchu, anti-Confucian, socially radical) that would
culminate in the twentieth century. Spence treats it as a key episode in China's "search."

## Historiography

Spence's account (and his dedicated study *God's Chinese Son*) draws on Taiping religious tracts and proclamations, Qing official reports, and the records and memoirs of the provincial commanders (notably Zeng Guofan) who suppressed the rebellion, alongside Western missionary and diplomatic observers who had direct contact with the Taiping capital at Nanjing. Mortality estimates for the rebellion vary enormously across sources — figures from 20 to 30 million or more appear in the literature — reflecting the near-impossibility of reconstructing population loss across a war zone the size of the Yangzi valley using incomplete Qing census data. Historiographical debate concerns how to classify the movement: as a millenarian religious uprising, a proto-nationalist ethnic revolt (Hakka marginalization), a social-revolutionary movement (given its radical property and gender policies), or some combination — with Chinese Communist historiography historically emphasizing the Taiping's social radicalism as a precursor to twentieth-century revolution, a reading Western scholarship treats more cautiously.
