## Narrative

The Abbasid Revolution was the most consequential political transformation in the history of the early Islamic caliphate: the overthrow of the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad dynasty]] by a broad coalition centered on the Hashimid family of the 'Abbasids (descendants of Muhammad's uncle al-'Abbas), led militarily by Abu Muslim from the province of Khurasan, and proclaimed caliphate at Kufah in 749–750 CE.

### Background: The Collapse of Marwani Authority

By the 740s the Umayyad caliphate under Hisham (r. 724–743) had achieved considerable administrative efficiency, but at the cost of deepening contradictions. The [[kharijites|Kharijite]] revolts were intensifying; the Shi'i Zayd b. 'Ali rose in Kufah in 740 with wide support; tribal feuding between the Qays and Yaman/Qahtan factions within the Arab military had become chronic. Hisham's successors — Walid II (743–744), Yazid III, and his brother — were increasingly ineffective. Marwan II (744–750), a capable military commander who had improved Arab drill organization in the Byzantine frontier wars, seized power but represented Qays faction sentiment rather than Muslim unity, and had already destroyed the older Syrian Marwani power base in suppressing rivals.

Against this background, Khariji movements in southern Arabia and the Jazirah both nearly seized the initiative before Marwan's disciplined troops crushed them. An important Shi'i revolt under 'Abd Allah ibn Mu'awiyah — a descendant of Abu Talib, 'Ali's father — gathered support across the Iranian highlands from "representatives from almost all elements of the Piety-minded opposition" before Marwan's forces defeated it too ([[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v1-1974]]).

### The 'Abbasid Propaganda Network

The 'Abbasids had been quietly organizing for decades. Their claim derived from a legitimacy transfer: Abu Hashim, head of the partisans of 'Ali's son Ibn al-Hanafiyyah, had allegedly bequeathed his authority and organizational network to the 'Abbasid family before his death (c. 716 CE). This gave the 'Abbasids both a Shi'i pedigree and a ready-made secret organization.

In Khurasan — where Mawali formed an "important and active element" jealous of Arab privilege, and where tribal Qahtan discontent with the central Marwani regime ran high — 'Abbasid agents operated under cover from the 730s onward. Their chief agent Khidash was executed by the Marwanis in 736 after preaching radical Ghulat-style doctrines the 'Abbasids promptly disowned. The propaganda continued. Around 745 CE, Ibrahim al-Imam (the 'Abbasid imam) dispatched his freed slave Abu Muslim to Khurasan to convert agitation into active revolt.

### Abu Muslim's Campaign (747–750)

Abu Muslim raised the black banners of mourning and revolt in Khurasan in 747. The black banners symbolized "mourning for [Husayn's] deaths — and indirectly, therefore, for all the injustices that either old-line Arabs or insulted Mawali felt they had received at the hands of the ruling Arab factions." He named no specific caliphal candidate, canvassing support in the name of "the house of Muhammad" generally, which allowed maximum coalition-building across Shi'i, Mawali, and Qahtan-tribal discontents.

Marwan II discovered Ibrahim al-Imam's connection with the revolt and had him killed (749). Abu Muslim added him to the list of Hashimid martyrs. By 748, with all other anti-Marwani movements defeated, Abu Muslim's was the only opposition still in the field and attracted "wide support."

Abu Muslim proved "a talented statesman, able to mobilize excellent generals." His Khurasanis moved west through Iran with little resistance. At the Euphrates, a major Marwani army was defeated. At Kufah — in the heart of Shi'i territory — Abu al-'Abbas, Ibrahim's brother, was proclaimed caliph with the byname al-Saffah ('the blood-shedder', 749–750). Marwan II's last army was defeated near the Tigris (750); he fled, unable to raise new forces in Syria, whose power he himself had already destroyed, and was finally caught and killed in Egypt. The 'Abbasid domination extended "as far as Egypt" almost immediately.

### The 'Abbasid Betrayal

The revolution's aftermath was as significant as its conduct. The 'Abbasids immediately abandoned their Shi'i allies:

- **Abu Salamah**, head of the Iraqi Shi'i coalition, had hoped to install an 'Alid caliph; when no 'Alid acted, he accepted al-Saffah reluctantly. He was soon killed.
- **Abu Muslim** himself, despite being the military architect of the revolution, was on bad terms with al-Mansur (r. 754–775). Al-Mansur lured him to a private audience "without his devoted soldiers" and had him murdered. The soldiers accepted the *fait accompli*.
- **Muhammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyyah** ('the pure soul'), an 'Alid claimant to the Mahdi title, rose in the Hijaz and Basra. His revolt — "so pietistic that it tried to imitate, against 'Abbasi might, techniques Muhammad had used against Arab tribesmen" — failed entirely. Even Basra's sympathizers were bloodily crushed.

Hodgson is explicit: "when the 'Abbasids had seized power they turned their backs on their more strictly Shi'i supporters, for the most part, and made relatively little of their role as imams endowed with 'ilm."

## Causal Analysis

Hodgson's analysis foregrounds three interlocking causes:

1. **Structural delegitimization of the Umayyads**: The Umayyad failure to embody Islamic egalitarianism — particularly their maintenance of Arab ethnic privilege in defiance of Islamic universalism — alienated both the Mawali and the piety-minded opposition across the empire. This was the underlying condition.

2. **Shi'i emotional and organizational infrastructure**: The martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala', the subsequent 'Alid martyrs (Zayd, his sons), and the network inherited from Abu Hashim provided both the mobilizing emotion and the organizational capacity that allowed Abu Muslim to build a winning coalition. Without [[shia-islam|Shi'i]] legitimacy, the 'Abbasid movement would have lacked both rationale and machinery.

3. **Khurasani specificity**: The geographic location mattered. Khurasan combined Mawali discontent, Qahtan tribal rivalry with the Marwani-identified Qays, and sufficient distance from Marwani military power in Syria and Iraq to allow the conspiracy to mature. Nasr b. Sayyar (Marwani governor) "could barely keep order" even before Abu Muslim arrived. Marwan II, too busy suppressing closer revolts, could not respond adequately to "so distant a danger."

Claude Cahen's study ('Points de vue sur la révolution "abbaside"', *Revue historique*, 230, 1963) is cited by Hodgson as "the most careful recent study of the 'Abbasi cause and its leadership of the Shi'i movement."

## Consequence Analysis

### Immediate Consequences

- **End of the Umayyad dynasty**: Al-Saffah's systematic massacre of the Umayyad family — including the macabre banquet at which invited Umayyad survivors were cut down, a carpet spread over their dying bodies, and the banquet continued to "the sound of their groans" — announced that the new regime would not be bound by the old Arab aristocratic norms of personal dignity.

- **Shift of capital to Iraq**: Al-Mansur founded [[baghdad]] in 762 CE, near the Sasanian capital Ctesiphon, deliberately away from Shi'i Kufah. This geographic shift consolidated the displacement of Syrian Arab power by Khurasani-Iraqi power.

- **Mawali integration**: The 'Abbasids "made no effective distinction between the old Arab families and the new Muslims, the 'Mawali'." This fulfilled one of the central demands of the opposition.

### Long-term Consequences

- **Sunni-Shi'a crystallization**: The Abbasid betrayal of Shi'i expectations forced a polarization. As Hodgson writes, this is the moment when Muslim allegiance was "polarized throughout the empire, pro or con." The Sunni (Men of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah) position — accepting the religious authority of all of Muhammad's companions — was the 'Abbasid-backed majority answer to the Shi'i rejection of the first three caliphs.

- **Absolutist caliphate**: The 'Abbasids completed the transformation of the caliphate into something approximating Irano-Sasanian absolutism: summary executions, a powerful vizier controlling finances, a spy network, and the elevation of the caliph above all social groups. The [[piety-minded-opposition|Piety-minded]] were given formal legal recognition but no political power.

- **Shari'ah institutionalization**: The 'Abbasids appointed as *qadis* men recognized by the piety-minded, binding the courts to Shari'ah *fiqh*. This gave the [[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]]-era legal schools their institutional footing within the state, a significant concession that diffused opposition.

## Actors

- **Abu Muslim**: Freed slave of Ibrahim al-Imam; military organizer of the revolt; killed by al-Mansur in 754 despite being the revolution's architect. Hodgson notes he "seems to have stressed revenge for the deaths of various members of the house of Muhammad."
- **Al-Saffah** (Abu al-'Abbas, r. 750–754): First 'Abbasid caliph; his reign defined by the massacre of the Umayyads and the brutal assertion of a new political style.
- **Al-Mansur** (Abu Ja'far, r. 754–775): Second caliph; the real builder of 'Abbasid imperial structure; founded Baghdad, built the bureaucratic and spy apparatus, patronized the development of Shari'ah courts, and systematically eliminated potential rivals including Abu Muslim and the Alid Nafs al-Zakiyyah.
- **Marwan II** (r. 744–750): Last Umayyad caliph; a capable military commander who nonetheless could not overcome the structural delegitimization of his dynasty; killed in Egypt.

## Historiography

The Abbasid revolution was long interpreted as primarily an Iranian national reaction against Arab dominance — a reading Hodgson implicitly rejects by stressing the Mawali's Islamic rather than Iranian ethnic motivation, and by foregrounding the Shi'i ideological dimension. Cahen (1963) represents the most careful recent reassessment available to Hodgson. The question of whether the Abbasid movement was primarily a social revolution (Mawali vs. Arabs), a religious revolution (Shi'i Islam vs. Umayyad irreligion), or a tribal realignment (Qahtan vs. Qays) remains an open historiographical controversy — Hodgson argues all three operated simultaneously and cannot be separated.
