# Collision at Cajamarca (16 November 1532)

On 16 November 1532, [[pizarro-francisco|Francisco Pizarro]] and a tiny force of roughly 168 Spaniards (including 62 horsemen) captured the Inca emperor Atahuallpa in the presence of his army of thousands at the highland Peruvian town of Cajamarca. It was the opening and decisive blow of the [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532|Spanish conquest of Peru]], and [[diamond-guns-germs-and-steel-1997|Jared Diamond]] uses it as the framing case for [[guns-germs-and-steel|the entire "guns, germs, and steel" thesis]] — a microcosm of the collision between Eurasian and Native American societies after 1492.

Atahuallpa had just won a civil war against his half-brother Huascar and was at the height of his power as absolute ruler of the largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas. Pizarro’s band had marched inland from the coast, exploiting the empire’s recent trauma (an epidemic, probably smallpox, that had already killed the previous emperor Huayna Capac and many others, triggering the succession crisis).

## Narrative

The Spaniards entered the town square of Cajamarca. Atahuallpa, surrounded by tens of thousands of troops, agreed to meet Pizarro’s representatives in the square the next day. When the emperor arrived with a large unarmed retinue (after a religious purification), the Spaniards sprang a trap. A Dominican friar presented the *Requerimiento* (a legal-theological justification for conquest). When Atahuallpa threw down the proffered Bible, the Spaniards opened fire with cannons and harquebuses, charged with cavalry, and attacked with steel swords. The Inca forces, unprepared for horses, steel, and gunpowder and without their emperor’s command structure, panicked. Thousands were killed in the confined space; Atahuallpa was seized.

The emperor was held for ransom (a famous room filled with gold and silver). After the ransom was paid, he was tried on charges including idolatry and the murder of Huascar, and executed in 1533. Spanish forces then advanced on Cuzco, the Inca capital, exploiting ongoing Inca divisions.

## Causal Analysis

**Proximate causes** (the immediate “guns, germs, and steel”):
- Steel weapons and armor vs. Andean clubs, slings, and quilted armor.
- Horses for mobility, shock charges, and psychological terror (llamas were not ridden or used in warfare the same way).
- Firearms and cannons (limited numbers but devastating in effect).
- Surprise and tactical discipline in an enclosed space.
- Political disarray: recent civil war + epidemic that had already decapitated the Inca leadership and weakened the population.

**Ultimate causes** (why the Spaniards had these advantages and the Incas did not):
- Eurasia’s earlier and more productive development of food production (Fertile Crescent and other centers) → larger populations, surpluses, specialization, dense settlements → evolution of crowd diseases (to which Andeans had no immunity) and complex political/military organization.
- Continental geography: Eurasia’s east-west axis facilitated diffusion of crops, livestock, technology, and ideas; the Americas’ north-south axis and barriers slowed or prevented equivalent developments (see [[continental-axes]] and [[geographic-determinism]]).
- The specific trajectory of western Eurasian societies by 1492 produced the full package of guns, germs, steel, writing, and state power.

Diamond uses Cajamarca in Chapter 3 as the vivid hook for the entire book: the outcome was not random or due to individual genius or moral superiority but the product of 13,000 years of divergent continental histories.

## Consequence Analysis

- Immediate: Fall of the Inca Empire’s core; Spanish control of the richest silver-producing regions (Potosí).
- Medium-term: Acceleration of the conquest of the Andes and the rest of the Americas; massive flow of precious metals to Europe.
- Long-term: One of the decisive steps in the creation of a global system dominated by Eurasian-derived societies; massive demographic collapse in the Andes from disease, exploitation, and war; cultural and political transformation of the region.
- Symbolic: Often cited (including by Diamond) as the emblematic moment when the “Guns, Germs, and Steel” advantages became visible on the ground.

## Actors

- **[[pizarro-francisco|Francisco Pizarro]]**: Leader of the expedition; combined audacity, ruthlessness, and exploitation of local conditions.
- **Atahuallpa**: Last independent Inca emperor; victor in the civil war but fatally miscalculated the Spanish threat.
- **[[inca-empire|Inca Empire]]**: Largest and most administratively sophisticated Native American state; built on earlier Andean traditions but vulnerable to the combination of disease, civil war, and the specific technologies the Spaniards brought.
- Broader Spanish forces and the Spanish crown (institutional and logistical backing).

## Historiography

Diamond presents Cajamarca as the outcome of deep structural (ultimate) causes rather than contingency or heroism alone. Critics of geographic determinism argue that Inca political mistakes, the role of Andean allies who joined the Spaniards, the specific personalities involved, and later Spanish administrative choices also mattered. The event is also central to debates over the “Black Legend,” virgin-soil epidemics, and the nature of European imperialism.

The capture is documented in Spanish eyewitness accounts (e.g., by Pizarro’s secretary and soldiers) and later Inca and mestizo chroniclers (Garcilaso de la Vega, Guamán Poma). Archaeological and epidemiological work continues to refine understanding of the pre-contact epidemic that preceded the Spaniards.
