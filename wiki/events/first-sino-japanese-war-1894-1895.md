# The First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)

The war between [[meiji-period|Meiji Japan]] and [[qing-dynasty|Qing China]] over the control
of Korea — Japan's first foreign war as a modern state and the moment it announced itself as
an imperial **great power**, decisively overturning the centuries-old [[ming-dynasty|Sinocentric]]
order of East Asia. From the [[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History
of Japan Vol. 5]] (Akira Iriye, ch. 12).

## Narrative

The proximate cause was the **struggle for Korea**: through the 1880s Japan and China had
jockeyed for influence in the peninsula, where Japanese exports (cotton yarn and manufactures)
were growing fast and where reformers and conservatives looked respectively to Tokyo and
Beijing. The **Tonghak peasant rebellion (1894)** drew in both powers' troops; when China
refused Japan's demand to jointly "reform" Korea, Japan seized the Korean court and war
followed. The modernized Japanese army and navy won rapid victories on land and at sea
(the Yellow Sea; the capture of P'yŏngyang, the Liaodong forts, and the Qing fleet's base at
Weihaiwei). The war ended in the **Treaty of Shimonoseki (April 1895)**, negotiated for Japan
by [[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]] with the Qing statesman Li Hongzhang.

## The Treaty of Shimonoseki and the Triple Intervention

By the treaty China recognized **Korea as fully independent** (ending Chinese suzerainty),
ceded **Taiwan, the Pescadores, and the Liaodong peninsula** to Japan, paid a colossal
**indemnity of 200 million taels** (~¥300 million, several times Japan's annual budget),
opened four treaty ports, and granted Japan most-favored-nation status and manufacturing
rights in China. Within days the **Triple Intervention** — Russia, France, and Germany —
forced Japan to **return Liaodong** in exchange for a larger indemnity, a humiliation that
fueled Japanese resentment of Russia (which then took Liaodong's Port Arthur for itself) and
the slogan *gashin shōtan* ("endure hardship for revenge"), pointing toward the
[[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]].

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the contest for **Korea** and the collapse of the old tributary order — Japan
  insisting on Korean "independence" from China precisely to open it to Japanese influence.
- `caused_by:` Meiji Japan's **drive to great-power status** — Iriye argues imperialism was
  the behavior of a **modern state** (politically and militarily "equipped to do so," and
  backed by aroused national opinion), *not* primarily a product of mature capitalism, which
  in Japan was "just getting under way."
- `contributed_to:` the convergence of domestic forces — even the Asianist and popular-rights
  opposition's heightened patriotism "solidified the basis of the Japanese state as it staged
  its first imperialist ventures."

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Japan's emergence as an **imperial power** with its first colony (**Taiwan**)
  and a seat "at the imperialist banquet table"; the indemnity funded heavy industry (the
  **Yawata** iron-and-steel works).
- `produced:` the **end of Chinese suzerainty over Korea**, opening the path to Japanese
  domination and annexation (1910).
- `contributed_to:` the **scramble for concessions** in a China now revealed as weak, and
  (via the Triple Intervention) the Russo-Japanese rivalry that led to the
  [[russo-japanese-war-1904|war of 1904–5]].
- `produced:` (Iriye) the deeper **militarization of Japanese society** and the entrenchment
  of imperialism "at the center of the politics, economy, and culture" of the state.

## Historiography

Iriye's chapter argues against a narrowly economic ("Leninist") reading of Japanese
imperialism: industrial-finance capital was only "one ingredient," and it was **the state**,
backed by mass national sentiment, that drove expansion. The war strengthened the very trends
— centralization, industrialization, militarization — that had produced it, while also
breeding new internal tensions over the "burden as well as the glory of empire."
