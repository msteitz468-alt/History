# Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773)

On the night of 16 December 1773, disguised patriots (some as "Mohawk Indians," others with soot or women's clothing) boarded three ships at Griffin's Wharf in Boston and dumped 90,000 pounds of East India Company tea into the harbor. The action was carried out with discipline: no looting, private property other than the targeted tea was respected, and the tea that washed ashore was destroyed to prevent salvage.

## Narrative

The Tea Act was not a new tax on the colonists. It was a measure to rescue the financially distressed East India Company: a massive government loan, permission to sell tea directly to American retailers, and elimination of the English import duty on tea re-exported to America. The result would have made tea *cheaper* for colonial consumers. The Sons of Liberty, led locally by Samuel Adams's networks, nonetheless destroyed the cargo. George Robert Twelves Hewes, a poor shoemaker among the participants, enforced discipline against would-be looters.

Contemporaries called it "the destruction of the tea." The event is remembered today as the Boston Tea Party.

In a letter to Benjamin Franklin, Samuel Adams denounced the act as “introductive to Monopolies,” which he viewed as “dangerous to Publick Liberty,” particularly when “under the direction and Influence of Government.” The “destruction of the tea,” as it was known in the early nineteenth century, was a direct-action attack on private property to resist government-abetted corporate power. Careful discipline ensured the tea was not looted and resold; when tea floated ashore, it was sunk.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Tea Act's combination of monopoly power and tax relief for a "too big to fail" corporation, imposed by a Parliament in which colonists had no representation.

**contributed_to:** Colonial perception that the British government was using fiscal tools to favor connected private interests over colonial rights and local self-government.

**preceded_by:** The Stamp Act crisis, Townshend duties, and the partial repeal that left the tea duty in place as a symbol of parliamentary authority.

## Consequence Analysis

British retaliation (the Coercive/Intolerable Acts) closed the port of Boston, altered the Massachusetts charter, and expanded military presence, radicalizing moderates and producing the First Continental Congress. The event supplied a powerful symbol of resistance that later generations would reinterpret.

## Actors

- Sons of Liberty
- Samuel Adams (organizer)
- East India Company (corporate beneficiary)
- Governor Thomas Hutchinson (refused to allow the ships to leave without paying duties)

## Historiography

Popular memory and much textbook history present the Boston Tea Party as a protest against taxation. Williamson (2025) demonstrates from contemporary sources (including a letter from Samuel Adams to Franklin) that the objection was to the monopolistic corporate bailout and the principle of unrepresented power, not to the tax burden itself. The disciplined, non-looting character of the action is often downplayed in favor of a more anarchic "tax revolt" image. The event remains central to debates over the relationship between taxation, representation, and corporate power in the American political tradition.
