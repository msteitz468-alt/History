# Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773)

On the night of **16 December 1773**, disguised patriots (some as “Mohawk Indians,” others with soot or women’s clothing) boarded three ships at **Griffin’s Wharf** in Boston and dumped East India Company tea into the harbor. Contemporaries called it “the destruction of the tea”; later memory made it the Boston Tea Party.

## Narrative

### Tea Act and consignees (spring–autumn 1773)

[[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Schiff]] (with earlier wiki layers from Williamson): Parliament passed the **Tea Act** in spring 1773 with little debate—East India Company export of tea directly without additional English duty, agents eliminating middle merchants, unloading warehouse surplus, underselling Dutch smugglers, and slipping residual **Townshend** sovereignty into the cup. Tea was already politically toxic as the sole remaining dutied Townshend item. Of colonial agents, none more offensive in Boston than the six appointed: two of [[hutchinson-thomas|Hutchinson]]’s sons, two relatives, two close friends. Philadelphia and New York intended to prevent landing and hoped Boston would join.

In a letter to Benjamin Franklin, [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]] denounced the act as “introductive to Monopolies,” dangerous to “Publick Liberty” when “under the direction and Influence of Government.” In November 1773 he wrote that his next letter would not be on a trifling subject; within weeks 342 chests were in the harbor—an associate later asked if his letters were “designedly oracular.”

### Dartmouth, Old South, and the twenty-day clock

The **Dartmouth** arrived ~28 November carrying **114 chests**. Once past Castle William, the owner had **20 days** to unload or pay duty—grace expired **midnight 17 December**. If unpaid, customs would impound and auction; landing, in Boston logic, ruined American liberties. North End men at the **Green Dragon** demanded agent resignations at the Liberty Tree (agents no-show 3 November); Molineux confronted the Clarkes’ warehouse. Thousands used **Old South**—Boston’s largest structure—as civic center; Hutchinson saw selectmen, clerks, and neighboring towns as if an “entirely new regime” had emerged from the shadows.

**16 December, 10 a.m.:** ~**6,000** people (some from 25 miles) crammed Old South. Customs and navy denied Francis **Rotch** clearance; Hutchinson refused a pass. After Adams’s announcement that the meeting had “done all they could for the salvation of their country,” and a stalling speech by Dr. Young, disguised men emptied **342 chests** into the harbor in roughly **two hours** under moonlight within warship range. Order was praised: “Perhaps never was more work of the like kind done in so little time, and with so little confusion.”

### Execution details

Three groups with captain/boatswain; hold keys and candles requested; tidewaiters put ashore; other cargo gently replaced; **only tea** destroyed. A man pocketing leaves was tackled through a gauntlet; crews swept decks and replaced padlocks. Value ~**£10,000** sterling (Schiff: eight Hutchinsonian mansions’ worth). Perpetrators soot-faced, blankets, red wool caps—“as one man”; variously styled Mohawks/Narragansett; disguise transformed riot into raid. Rev. Samuel Cooper: “a remarkable instance of order and justice among savages.” George Robert Twelves Hewes and others enforced non-looting discipline.

Adams later claimed ~**40 men** did the destruction; he headed the **24 December** secrecy pledge list. London inquiry: Rotch named Adams first among prime movers; no one placed him at the wharf. Schiff’s judgment: prime mover by **stage management**, not a preconcerted verbal cue on the floor; fingerprints absent by design. Adams dispatched [[revere-paul|Paul Revere]] with the news; understated letter to Arthur Lee—“spirit of the people… surprised.” Blame fixed on Hutchinson and agents; destruction framed as “rationally destroying property.”

### Intercolonial and second dump

New York and Philadelphia ordered tea back after Boston’s news; South Carolina’s was unloaded but never sold. Early **March 1774**, a brigantine with **28 non–East India** chests met the same treatment at Griffin’s Wharf “with great regularity and dispatch.”

### Imperial reply

**11 February 1774:** Wedderburn and Thurlow first assured the king the violence was high treason, then reversed—hearsay, no secure prosecutions; the king exploded. **Boston Port Act** passed end of March—close the harbor until East India reimbursement; Gage as governor and commander; government seat to Salem. London *Evening Post* charge: riot and trespass; repayment figures circulated at **£30,000**; Gage rejected merchant reimbursement offers because the merchants had not destroyed the tea. By early July a Philadelphia Crown officer lamented that 1773 vandalism was “entirely lost” in 1774 punishment. Community to starve without formal crime alleged in Boston’s hearing.

John Adams, who three years earlier had argued law discourages riots, exulted: “There is a dignity, a majesty, a sublimity, in this last effort of the patriots that I greatly admire.”

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Tea Act’s combination of monopoly power and residual duty, imposed by a Parliament in which colonists had no representation; refusal of Hutchinson/agents to return tea without duty.

**contributed_to:** Colonial perception that fiscal tools favored connected private interests; [[committees-of-correspondence]] as ready alarm and coordination instrument (21 Oct letter; Nov 6 to every Massachusetts town).

**preceded_by:** Stamp Act crisis, Townshend duties, partial repeal leaving the tea duty, occupation, Massacre commemorations, Hutchinson letters disclosure (June 1773).

## Consequence Analysis

British retaliation (Coercive/Intolerable Acts) closed Boston’s port, altered the Massachusetts charter, and expanded military presence, radicalizing moderates and producing the **First Continental Congress**. Schiff: if committees **wired** the colony, 16 December **threw the switch**—every Massachusetts town “on tiptoe.” Violence united British parties against America; Franklin’s Privy Council humiliation (29 Jan 1774) was an early casualty of the news.

## Historiography

Popular memory and much textbook history present the Boston Tea Party as a protest against taxation. Williamson (2025) demonstrates from contemporary sources (including Samuel Adams to Franklin) that the objection was to monopolistic corporate bailout and unrepresented power, not tax burden as such. The disciplined, non-looting character is often downplayed in favor of an anarchic “tax revolt” image.

Schiff adds: (1) precise operational chronology (Dartmouth clock, Old South numbers, 342 chests, ~£10,000, three-group procedure); (2) Adams as stage-manager whose absence from the wharf was intentional stagecraft, not innocence; (3) memory politics—destruction of East India property “surfaced only a half-century afterward, repackaged as a costumed caper,” while Boston minimized messy street protests and later sanitized rebellion without Adams. A 1789 depiction was already fanciful (disguised “Indians” become actual Native Americans). The event remains central to debates over taxation, representation, and corporate power.
