# Night of the Long Knives (1934)

## Narrative

The 'Night of the Long Knives' (30 June–2 July 1934) was a series of state-ordered murders carried out on Hitler's authority to crush the leadership of the SA, eliminate other perceived threats (including conservatives around Papen and former Chancellor Schleicher), and secure Hitler's unchallenged control. It removed the most serious internal challenge to the regime and to Hitler's position at that stage.

The immediate trigger was the perceived threat from SA Chief of Staff Ernst Röhm, whose ambitions for a 'second revolution' and incorporation of the SA into the Reichswehr alarmed the army, business interests, and conservatives. Hindenburg and the Reichswehr leadership made clear that action against the SA was expected. Hitler had hesitated, valuing Röhm's past services, but the loss of face and pressure from Göring and Himmler tipped the balance.

On 30 June, Hitler flew to Bad Wiessee, confronted Röhm and SA leaders at the hotel, and ordered immediate arrests. Six SA men were shot that day in Stadelheim on Hitler's direct orders (crosses marked on a list; told 'You have been condemned to death by the Führer! Heil Hitler!'). Röhm was initially spared. In Berlin, Göring activated murder squads with the password 'Kolibri'. Victims included Herbert von Bose, Edgar Jung, Gregor Strasser, General Schleicher and his wife, Major-General Bredow, Ritter von Kahr, and others (including mistaken victims). Erich Klausener (Catholic Action) was shot on Heydrich's orders. In Munich and Silesia, 'local initiative' added to the toll; blood-lust developed its own momentum. Estimates of total killed range from 85 documented to 150–200.

Röhm was shot on 1 July at Stadelheim by SS men under Theodor Eicke (Dachau commandant) and Michael Lippert after refusing suicide (pistol left with him was untouched). Hitler's announcement: 'The former Chief of Staff Röhm was given the opportunity to draw the consequences of his treacherous behaviour. He did not do so and was thereupon shot.'

On 2 July Hitler announced the end of the 'cleansing action'. Göring ordered police to burn files. On 3 July a cabinet meeting (Blomberg praising Hitler's 'resolute and courageous action'; Justice Minister Gürtner accepting legality) passed the Law for the Emergency Defence of the State retroactively legalizing the measures as 'emergency defence of the state'. On 13 July Hitler addressed the Reichstag in a two-hour speech, openly accepting full responsibility: he was 'the supreme judge of the German people', had given the order to shoot the guilty, and taken responsibility even where guilt was not fully proven. The speech was received with tumultuous cheering inside and broad public approval outside.

Public reaction inside Germany was overwhelmingly positive. The SA's bullying and unruliness had become widely resented; Hitler was seen as the restorer of order and protector of the 'little man'. 'If only the Führer knew' excused him from negative aspects. Reports noted 'unreserved recognition' and even deification. The Churches were silent. The army celebrated the destruction of its rival. Carl Schmitt published 'The Führer Protects the Law'.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Röhm/SA power pretensions vs. Reichswehr monopoly on arms; conservative pressure channelled through Hindenburg/Blomberg; Hitler's calculation that the army was indispensable for rearmament and that the SA had outlived its utility for street power.

`contributed_to:` Göring-Himmler alliance and ambition; improvised nature allowing score-settling (Strasser, Kahr, etc.).

`preceded_by:` SA growth and radicalism post-1933; tensions with army throughout early 1934.

Hitler was reluctant on Röhm personally but acted ruthlessly once decided. The action was partly improvised; 'the blood-lust had developed its own momentum.'

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Elimination of SA as independent power; SS freed from SA control and made directly responsible to Hitler (20 July 1934); army complicity that bound it to Hitler; retroactive legalization of murder by the state; public legitimation of Hitler's role as 'supreme judge'.

`enabled:` Total power consolidation (Hindenburg death weeks later); army oath to Hitler personally; emasculation of SA into loyal auxiliary.

`concurrent_with:` Ongoing Gleichschaltung and consolidation.

The purge demonstrated to all opponents that Hitler would stop at nothing. Observers abroad largely treated it as an internal 'gangland bloodbath' and continued to deal with Hitler as a statesman.

## Actors

- Primary: Hitler (orders, responsibility, Reichstag justification); Röhm (target); Göring and Himmler (execution in Berlin/Bavaria, pressure on Hitler).
- Secondary: SS squads (Eicke/Lippert); Reichswehr (Blomberg/Reichenau — beneficiaries and complicit); Papen circle (victims and survivors); Strasser, Schleicher, Kahr (old scores).

## Historiography

Kershaw details the improvisation, the mixture of central direction and local initiative, the public acclaim despite (or because of) the gangster methods, and the critical role of army complicity. The episode is presented as the decisive removal of the last serious threat to Hitler's position before Hindenburg's death. Emphasis on how it illustrated Hitler's willingness to use utmost brutality and how it was applauded as 'statesmanlike duty'. Central to analysis of how violence was normalized and how the regime moved from revolutionary movement to personalized dictatorship. Quotes from cabinet minutes, Reichstag speech, Sopade reports, Rosenberg diary, post-war testimony. Notes foreign misreading of the domestic gangster as separate from the 'responsible statesman' in diplomacy.
