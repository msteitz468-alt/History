# 1964-65 New York World's Fair

Robert Moses-directed international exposition at Flushing Meadows, Queens. Moses viewed the Fair primarily as a means to complete his long-planned chain of urban parks on the former garbage dump site, designating the parks the "ultimate residuary legatee" of any surplus and guaranteeing completion by summer 1967.

## Narrative

On the single square mile of the Fair grounds (over which Moses had sole jurisdiction), well over a billion dollars was spent by governments and private interests. The Fair Corporation itself built almost nothing of the pavilions, leaving exhibitors to bear costs while high rents flowed to the park legacy. Moses vetoed mass transit extensions to the site, preferring it not be too accessible to low-income (especially minority) populations.

The Fair was plagued by low attendance (Opening Day 49,642; many days under 100,000; break-even required 220,000 daily), accounting tricks (crediting advance sales to year one), monopolies (garbage collection, insurance), inflated PR and union fees, and international setbacks (BIE refused sanction; Britain, France, Italy and most of the Commonwealth withdrew). Moses attacked the press personally in a magazine article, leading to exposés.

Deficits mounted; key aides (Spargo fired for "negative thinking"; comptroller Witt died after confrontation; finance chair Moore resigned). Noteholders ultimately received about $3M on a $30M investment (~33 cents on the dollar). The Fair Corporation spent $22M+ on permanent park improvements.

## Causal Analysis

Moses' prioritization of parks over the Fair as spectacle, combined with his authority-style control, secrecy, and intolerance of dissent or bad news, produced over-optimistic projections, suppressed criticism, and structural problems (no competitive bidding for key services, refusal of transit, international isolation).

## Consequence Analysis

The Fair left a significant (if incomplete) park legacy at Flushing Meadows but at enormous public and private cost, with scandals that damaged Moses' reputation and contributed to his eventual fall. It exemplified the late-career "lust for power" and the human/institutional costs of his methods.

## Historiography

The dominant account is Robert Caro's *The Power Broker* (1974), a Pulitzer-winning
biography built on extensive interviews (with Moses aides, critics, and Moses himself
late in life), contemporary press coverage, and Fair Corporation financial records; Caro
is explicitly hostile to Moses' late-career methods, and his framing of the Fair as a
vanity/parks project rather than a civic celebration has become the standard
interpretation but has drawn some pushback from Moses defenders (notably in the 2007
Columbia exhibition and related reassessments) who stress the era's genuine achievements
and note Caro's narrative selects for damning anecdotes. Financial reconstruction relies
on Fair Corporation accounting, which was itself contested at the time (accusations of
manipulated figures), so precise loss figures vary slightly across sources. No serious
scholarly dispute exists over the broad outcome (financial failure, low attendance,
scandal) even where interpretations of Moses' motives differ.
