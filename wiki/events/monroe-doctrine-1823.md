# Monroe Doctrine (1823)

Foreign policy statement announced by President James Monroe in his December 1823 annual message to Congress, declaring that the Western Hemisphere was no longer open to further European colonization and that the United States would regard any European attempt to interfere with the newly independent nations of Latin America, or to extend European political systems into the Americas, as an act hostile to U.S. interests. In exchange, the doctrine pledged American non-interference in existing European colonies and in purely European affairs.

Drafted principally by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams, who persuaded Monroe to issue an independent U.S. statement rather than a joint declaration with Britain (which had proposed cooperating to deter Spanish reconquest of its former colonies but sought to also bind the U.S. against annexing new territory itself), the doctrine reflected both genuine sympathy for the newly independent Latin American republics and American strategic interest in excluding European powers from further hemispheric expansion. At the time of its announcement the doctrine had little independent enforcement power — American security in the hemisphere depended in practice on the British Royal Navy's parallel interest in blocking a Holy Alliance intervention — but it became a foundational reference point for U.S. foreign policy, invoked and reinterpreted for over a century, from its later use to justify direct U.S. intervention in Latin America and the Caribbean (the 1904 Roosevelt Corollary) to Cold War-era justifications for opposing Soviet influence in the hemisphere.

## Causal Analysis

The doctrine was **caused by** fears — encouraged though not directly acted upon — that the European Holy Alliance might assist Spain in reconquering its lost American colonies, and was **enabled by** British naval interest in preventing exactly such an intervention for its own commercial reasons.

## Consequence Analysis

The doctrine **produced** a durable rhetorical and later practical framework for asserting U.S. hegemony over the Western Hemisphere, and **enabled** later, far more interventionist policies (the Roosevelt Corollary, repeated 20th-century interventions in Latin America and the Caribbean) that extended well beyond its original defensive, anti-colonial intent.

## Historiography

Historians distinguish the doctrine's limited original 1823 scope — primarily anti-colonial and reactive — from its much more expansive later invocations, especially the interventionist Roosevelt Corollary, cautioning against reading twentieth-century U.S. hegemonic practice directly back into Monroe's original statement.
