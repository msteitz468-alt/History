# Battle of Baideng (200 BCE)

## Narrative

The Battle of Baideng (Wade-Giles: *P'ing-ch'eng*) was the decisive military confrontation that established the terms of Han–Xiongnu relations for the next two generations. Liu Bang — Han Gaozu (Kao-tsu), the founder of the Han dynasty — led an army in pursuit of a Xiongnu raiding force deep into the frontier zone, was encircled by Modu Chanyu (Modun) at Baideng Mountain near Pingcheng in the Yan-men commandery (east of modern Datong, Shanxi), and was forced to extricate himself through negotiation rather than military force. The defeat was remembered in the historiographical tradition as the foundational humiliation of the early Han — "the outrage of P'ing-ch'eng" — and became the touchstone for all subsequent debates about Han frontier policy.

The battle was treated by Di Cosmo as background knowledge shared by Han statesmen: "the humiliating defeat suffered by Han Kao-tsu at P'ing-ch'eng," cited in the debates of 135–134 BCE (*Han shu* 52, 2398–2403), was the standard argument against offensive action. In those debates Han An-kuo deployed the battle as evidence that appeasement "had 'benefited five generations'" by averting a repetition of the Baideng catastrophe. Wang Hui countered that the conditions had changed: the defeat had occurred because "the empire was not in a position to continue to fight after the many years of civil war at the end of the Ch'in," and the peace Liu Bang secured "was simply not there anymore."

The location — P'ing-ch'eng prefecture, part of the Yan-men commandery — is identified by Hans Bielenstein as "situated east of present Ta-t'ung" (*Bulletin of the Museum of Far Eastern Antiquities* 39, part II [1967]: 86, n. 3).

## Causal Analysis

Three causal factors drove the battle:

**Han military weakness at the dynastic founding.** Liu Bang's armies had spent years in civil war against Qin remnants and rival warlords. By 200 BCE the Han was not a consolidated empire but a fragile coalition still subduing semi-independent kings (*chu-hou-wang*). This preoccupation left insufficient resources for a disciplined northern frontier strategy. As Wang Hui put it in 134 BCE, Liu Bang "had fought continuously for over ten years, and therefore he could not avenge the outrage of P'ing-ch'eng. Without force there is no ability" (*Han shu* 52, 2400).

**Xiongnu cavalry superiority in their own terrain.** The Xiongnu under Modu had assembled an empire with strategic mobility that Chinese armies could not match in the open steppe. Han An-kuo articulated the asymmetry repeatedly: "the Hsiung-nu depend upon the hooves of their own horses... they move around like a multitude of birds and are difficult to capture and control" (*Han shu* 52, 2398). Logistical constraints further limited Chinese pursuit capacity: Han armies could not sustain expeditions in nomadic territory for more than approximately one hundred days.

**Strategic overreach.** The pursuit of a raiding force deep into frontier territory was characteristic of the Han's early failure to understand the Xiongnu's deliberate use of mobility as a tactical asset. The defeat was not an accident of the battle itself but the structural consequence of fighting the Xiongnu on their own terms.

## Consequence Analysis

The most consequential outcome was not the battle itself but the diplomatic settlement it necessitated. Within two years (198 BCE) Liu Bang formalized the [[heqin-policy|heqin]] ("peace-and-kinship") policy with the Xiongnu, inaugurating a tributary relationship that would govern Han–Xiongnu relations for two generations. The heqin framework included:

- Annual tribute of silk, grain, and other goods delivered to the Xiongnu chanyu
- Marriage alliances, with Chinese princesses (or women styled as such) given as consorts to the chanyu
- Recognition of the chanyu as the nominal equal of the Han emperor — the political symbolism in which the two rulers were portrayed as "Earth" and "Heaven," or as "brothers"
- Border markets opened as controlled exchange points

The policy persisted through five reigns — from Liu Bang through Emperors Hui, Wen, Jing, and into the early years of Wu — precisely because it was understood as the lesser of two evils. The Baideng defeat had demonstrated that full military engagement carried unacceptable risk. As Han An-kuo argued in 135 BCE, attacking "would not be better than ho-ch'in."

The deeper consequence was structural: the defeat forced the early Han emperors to prioritize internal consolidation over external expansion, managing the centrifugal pressures of the semi-independent regional kings (*chu-hou-wang*) rather than projecting force northward. The Xiongnu exploited this period of Han internal fragility to extract tribute and maintain raiding pressure simultaneously — the dynamic that eventually made the heqin unsustainable.

## Actors

- **[[liu-bang|Liu Bang (Han Gaozu)]]** — Han founding emperor; pursued the Xiongnu force and was encircled; forced to accept terms.
- **[[modu-chanyu|Modu Chanyu (Modun)]]** — Xiongnu supreme leader (chanyu) who encircled Liu Bang; the Baideng encirclement was a demonstration of his empire's military reach.
- **[[han-dynasty|The Han dynasty]]** — institutionally reorganized its frontier strategy in the aftermath, adopting heqin as the operating doctrine.
- **[[xiongnu|The Xiongnu]]** — the empire whose military superiority on the steppe the battle definitively demonstrated.

## Historiography

The battle is not narrated in detail in Di Cosmo's chapter because "the plain narrative of the confrontation between Han and Hsiung-nu during the reign of Han Wu-ti is well known and does not need to be repeated here." Di Cosmo relies most heavily on the *Shih chi* (chapter 110) of [[sima-qian|Sima Qian]], with supplementary use of the *Han shu* (chapter 94) of Pan Ku. The primary debates in the *Han shu* (chapter 52, 2398–2403) preserve the fullest contemporary discussion of the battle's significance.

The historiographical controversy surrounding Baideng concerns not the battle itself but its interpretation: was the heqin that followed a pragmatic stopgap or an expression of genuine Han tributary status? Di Cosmo's structural incompatibility thesis argues this dichotomy is less important than the question of why the heqin consistently failed to prevent border violence — a failure rooted in the asymmetric authority structures of the two polities rather than in the diplomatic intentions of either side. See [[heqin-policy]] for full treatment.

The *Han shu* account is described by Di Cosmo as "the major source for Han history" but "less valid in this study because it is not the first account to detail the history of Han–Hsiung-nu relations" — priority belonging to Sima Qian's *Shih chi*. The lingering controversy about the authenticity of *Shih chi* chapter 123 (possibly derived from *Han shu* chapters 61 and 96) does not affect the Baideng narrative, which is chiefly in chapter 110 of the *Shih chi* and presents no significant textual problems.
