# Battle of Boroughbridge (1322)

The battle in which the rebel forces of Thomas of Lancaster were decisively defeated by the royalist army of Andrew Harclay at the bridge and ford over the River Ouse near Boroughbridge, Yorkshire. It marked the effective end of the Despenser War baronial rebellion and provided a positive demonstration of infantry tactics that influenced subsequent English practice.

## Narrative

Andrew Harclay, with a force drawn largely from the northern counties (Cumberland, Westmorland, or York), positioned his troops to block the approaches to the bridge and ford. The bulk of his men were infantry armed with spears or pikes; even the knights fought dismounted. He placed pikemen and dismounted knights at the northern end of the bridge and, opposite the ford, formed other pikemen into a schiltrom "according to the manner of the Scots."

Thomas of Lancaster and Humphrey de Bohun, Earl of Hereford, approached with a cavalry-heavy force including many knights. Hereford led an assault on the bridge. The charge was met by pikes thrust from all sides; Hereford was killed on the bridge along with his standard-bearer and other knights. The attackers were driven back.

Lancaster's division attempted to cross the ford but was slowed and disordered by archery and the density of pikes. After Hereford's death the rebels' zeal cooled and they retreated. Lancaster sought a truce but was overwhelmed. The battle was won by Harclay's infantry formations.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the Despenser War and the baronial rebellion against Edward II and the Despenser favourites.
- `contributed_to:` Lancaster's opposition and the decision to march north.
- The use of dismounted infantry and schiltrom tactics (explicitly compared to Scottish practice at Bannockburn) allowed a smaller force to defeat a more "knightly" cavalry army when geography (bridge and ford) narrowed the avenues of attack.

## Consequence Analysis

- Ended the Lancaster rebellion; Thomas of Lancaster was captured and executed shortly afterward.
- Provided a clear, successful English example of dismounted infantry defeating cavalry charges, reinforcing the lessons (positive in this case) from Bannockburn.
- "Never again would an English army fight as it had at Bannockburn. From this time until the end of the Middle Ages the chief tactic of the English was to dismount most of the cavalry and to order them in a solid infantry formation... This once powerful and proud cavalry-based army had become little more than... 'mounted infantry.'" (DeVries range 05)
- Influenced the English shift to the dismounted + archery system later used in the Hundred Years' War.

## Actors

- Andrew Harclay: royalist commander who used terrain and infantry formations to victory; later created Earl of Carlisle.
- Thomas of Lancaster: rebel leader, executed after the defeat.
- Humphrey de Bohun, 4th Earl of Hereford: killed leading the bridge assault.

## Historiography

Contemporary accounts (Chronicon de Lanercost, Vita Edwardi II, Brut, Bridlington) differ on details such as whether Hereford's men were mounted or on foot at the bridge and the precise role of archers. DeVries privileges the more detailed narratives and emphasises the infantry schiltrom at the ford and the solid line at the bridge as the decisive factors. The battle is seen both as the end of a major baronial revolt and as a key positive example accelerating English adoption of dismounted tactics.
