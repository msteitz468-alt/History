## Narrative

The half-century from 1532 to 1580 transformed the Andes from the domain of the [[inca-empire]] into the Viceroyalty of Peru, the most powerful European political structure in the Americas. Karen Spalding (Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas, Vol. III Part 1, Ch. 12) insists this transformation cannot be told solely from the European victors' perspective: "conquests can provide only a chronology for the winners, for like many defining 'events,' they become clear only with the passage of time, and only as long as they are seen from a single perspective."

### The Invasion and Killing of Atahualpa (1531–1533)

[[pizarro-francisco]] left Panama in January 1531 with approximately 180–200 men and thirty horses, authorized by the crown under a *capitulación* granting him governor-general status over all lands he conquered. The Inka state he entered was already fractured by civil war following the sudden death (likely from smallpox, c. 1525) of Huayna Capac: Atahualpa (Quito-based) and Huascar (Cusco-based) were fighting for succession. At [[collision-at-cajamarca-1532]], the Spaniards ambushed Atahualpa, killing thousands of his largely unarmed escort and taking the Sapa Inka prisoner. Atahualpa offered the famous ransom — "a room filled with gold and silver as high as he could reach" — which was collected and melted down to over 1.5 million pesos (some 13,420 pounds of gold and 26,000 pounds of silver). Despite fulfilling the ransom, Atahualpa was condemned to death in spring 1533 on rumors of organizing resistance. The treasure of Cajamarca "sparked a rush of men to Peru in the hope of making their fortunes."

### Two Perspectives on the Same Events

Spalding distinguishes sharply between the European and Andean readings of 1532–1536. From the Spanish perspective, Atahualpa's capture and death *was* the conquest. From an Andean perspective, "there is little evidence that the Andean combatants saw what happened to Atawallpa as anything more than another part of the long civil war that rent the Inka state from the 1520s." Pizarro was acutely aware that his survival depended on Inka factional politics: "if that were not so, there would be great confusion, for each [lord] would revolt along with those who obeyed him, and it would be very difficult to bring them to friendship with the Spaniards and service to His Majesty" (Pizarro's secretary Pedro Sancho, *Relación* [1534]).

Manco Inca, who intercepted the Spanish/Andean forces outside [[cuzco]], initiated negotiations as a Cusco lord who wanted to expel the Quito faction — he "offered an alliance with the Spaniards 'in order to expel from the land all those from Quito, for they were his enemies'" (Sancho, *Relación*, 61). Manco was crowned in [[cuzco]] in a ceremony where Spaniards occupied the position of "valued allies," though each side fundamentally misunderstood the meaning of the other's rituals.

### The Plunder Economy and the Encomienda (1533–c. 1560)

From 1533 Pizarro distributed [[encomienda]] grants — authority over Andean polities assigned to individual Spaniards in proportion to their service. The first encomiendas in Peru "were grants of authority over thousands of people, assigned in terms of a kuraka (indigenous ruler) and all of the people subject to him." The institution adapted the Reconquest model, codified earlier in the Caribbean; it "refined the practice [of plunder] to a more institutionalized and regular form … in which the right to appropriate the goods and labor of the native population was restricted to one man alone: the encomendero."

The [[ayllu]]-based Andean productive system was initially left structurally intact and redirected to European ends. [[curaca|Kurakas]] (hereditary local lords) mediated between encomenderos and communities: "It was the task of the kuraka to make that claim for the encomendero, translating the latter's demands into labor time invested in producing the goods Europeans demanded." Spanish observers noted this resemblance to Inka patterns: "[T]hese encomenderos each set themselves up as an Inka, and appropriated by virtue of the encomiendas all of the rights, tributes, and services granted by that land to the Inka" (Fernando de Santillán, quoted by Spalding).

The early Potosí silver economy exemplified this hybrid: kurakas of the southern highlands sent rotating labor quotas (*mitayos*) to mine silver using traditional Andean techniques — wind-heated *huayra* furnaces fueled by llama dung — while also supplying the mine city with foodstuffs via llama trains. By the 1560s the trade in cloth and foodstuffs at Potosí amounted to about 300,000 pesos, "most of which was in the hands of the members of Andean society."

### Manco Inca's Revolt and the End of the Inka System (1536–1539)

In spring 1536 Manco Inca besieged [[cuzco]] with an estimated 100,000–200,000 warriors, cutting it off for more than four months, and simultaneously sent forces toward Lima. The siege ultimately failed when Manco's forces "no longer maintained by the vast system of state storehouses, began to drift off to prepare their fields for planting." In 1539 Manco withdrew to Vilcabamba in the eastern Andes, where he led a small peripheral polity until his assassination (1544). "The vast territory once ruled from Cusco became, as it had been before the expansion of Cusco, a collection of *behetrias*, or self-governing polities of varying size."

Throughout the subsequent Spanish civil wars (Pizarro vs. Almagro and their successors), Andean polities fought on both sides; "the majority of the fighting forces on all sides were Andean, not Spanish." Andean losses to war, forced labor, famine, and epidemic disease were catastrophic. The coastal population fell to approximately 4% of its pre-conquest size between 1525 and 1575; the highland population lost two-thirds to three-fourths within forty years of Spanish entry.

### Reform Attempts and Political Crisis (1540s–1560s)

Pedro de la Gasca ("the Pacifier") restored royal authority after the Gonzalo Pizarro revolt (1544–1548), imposed a tribute system in 1549, and made modest reductions in encomendero demands. But Gasca "paid a high price for peace" and multiplied encomiendas by dividing ethnic groups among several encomenderos — further fragmenting Andean authority. An alliance of Dominican and Franciscan friars, sympathetic *audiencia* members, and Andean kurakas petitioned the crown against the encomienda system through the 1550s. In 1560 kurakas offered 800,000 pesos through the intermediary of Domingo de Santo Tomás if the crown would reject perpetual encomienda grants. This coalition ultimately failed as Philip II, facing a bankrupt treasury and expensive European wars, shifted toward fiscal extraction over native protection.

The kuraka Don Diego Caqui of Tacna illustrates elite Andean participation in the colonial economy: by 1588 he owned four vineyards, a winery, a llama train to Potosí, two frigates, and a sloop — yet this accumulation was ultimately threatened by the Toledan reforms.

### The Toledan Reforms and Destructuration (1569–1580)

Viceroy Francisco de Toledo (1569–1581) reorganized the colonial system through three linked instruments:
1. **Reducciones**: forced resettlement of dispersed Andean populations into concentrated nucleated towns, enabling more efficient tribute collection and evangelization.
2. **The Potosí mita**: institutionalized rotating forced labor at the silver mines, now organized on a massive, coercive scale far exceeding the pre-Hispanic [[mita]].
3. **Corregidores de indios**: Spanish rural governors appointed over Andean districts, nominally to protect natives but rapidly becoming a new instrument of exploitation.

Spalding argues that Toledo is often credited with creative political organization, but "the destructuration of Andean societies was largely accomplished by the time he reached the region." Within a decade of Toledo's departure, new Indian settlements were losing population and corregidores were notorious for their exploitation.

Toledo also ordered the execution of the last Inka ruler, Tupac Amaru I, in 1572 — closing any remaining legitimacy for the Vilcabamba resistance.

### Andean Agency and Resistance

Spalding emphasizes that Andean peoples were not passive victims. Strategies included:
- **Migration** (*forasteros*) to escape tribute and labor demands.
- **Elite petitioning**: kurakas meeting at San Pedro Mama near Lima in 1562 gave power of attorney to Domingo de Santo Tomás to represent their interests in Madrid.
- **Economic participation**: kurakas monopolized food and cloth supplies to Potosí, rapidly adopted European products (grapes, cattle) for the market.
- **Religious resistance**: The **Taki Unquy** movement (c. 1564, region of Huamanga) called on Andeans to reject Christianity, avoid Spanish churches, and return to Andean regional deities — priests predicted that "Titicaca," "Tiahuanaco," and other *huacas* were about to defeat the Christian God and create a new world.
- **Gender resistance**: Female kurakas who held real authority in the conquest period saw that authority systematically eliminated within half a century; "the elimination of women from access to positions of authority was clearly a priority for the Spaniards."

## Causal Analysis

The transformation was enabled by a convergence of structural factors rather than Spanish military superiority alone:

- `caused_by: [[collision-at-cajamarca-1532]]` — the ambush and capture of Atahualpa removed the single figure around whom Inka legitimacy centred, triggering factional realignment that the Spaniards exploited.
- `caused_by: [[spanish-conquest-of-peru-1532]]` — the conquest campaigns dismantled Inka administrative infrastructure and expropriated state storehouses that underwrote Andean reciprocity.
- `contributed_to: [[inca-empire]] civil war` — the Atahualpa–Huascar succession war had already fractured Andean military and political unity before Pizarro's arrival; "the war for succession opened the entire network of alliances for a process of renegotiation."
- `contributed_to: [[encomienda]]` — the Reconquest-derived encomienda institution provided a ready legal framework for labor extraction without initially disrupting Andean productive organization.
- `contributed_to: demographic collapse` — epidemic disease (smallpox c. 1525, 1546, 1558–59; typhus or plague c. 1590) killed between two-thirds and nine-tenths of coastal and highland populations, collapsing the labor base on which both Andean reciprocity and Spanish extraction depended.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[mita]]` (Toledan) — the pre-Hispanic labor rotation was converted into a coercive colonial institution supplying Potosí with tens of thousands of forced laborers annually.
- `produced: [[global-silver-trade]]` — Potosí silver, extracted by Andean labor and processed with Andean techniques, became the engine of early modern global commerce.
- `enabled: [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]` — the destruction of Inka sacred infrastructure and suppression of regional cults (including violent campaigns against the Taki Unquy) created the conditions for Catholic evangelization.
- `produced: co-optation of [[curaca]] office` — hereditary Andean lords were transformed into colonial intermediaries, trapped between Spanish demands and community obligations, their traditional reciprocal authority hollowed out.
- `produced: reducciones` — forced resettlement disrupted [[vertical-archipelago]] resource-access patterns and severed communities from dispersed resource colonies.

## Actors

- **[[pizarro-francisco]]**: Governor-general of Peru; distributed first encomiendas; founded Lima (1535); killed by Almagrist faction (1541).
- **Atahualpa (Sapa Inka)**: Captured at [[collision-at-cajamarca-1532]]; executed 1533 despite paying ransom; his death removed central Inka political legitimacy.
- **Manco Inca (Manqu Inka)**: Crowned in Cusco as Inka with Spanish support (1533); besieged Cusco (1536); withdrew to Vilcabamba (1539); led peripheral resistance until assassination.
- **[[curaca|Kurakas]]**: Hereditary lords who mediated tribute and labor extraction; participated actively in colonial markets; some led political coalitions against the encomienda.
- **Pedro de la Gasca**: Royal pacifier (1547–1550); imposed formal tribute system; redistributed encomiendas; made limited reductions in native exploitation.
- **Francisco de Toledo** (Viceroy, 1569–1581): Imposed reducciones, reorganized Potosí mita, established corregidor system; executed Tupac Amaru I.
- **Domingo de Santo Tomás**: Dominican friar; compiled first Quechua grammar and dictionary (1560); represented kuraka interests before the crown; bishop of Charcas.

## Historiography

Spalding identifies a major historiographical shift since the 1980s away from depicting Andeans as passive victims or Spaniards as all-powerful:

> "There is no longer any need to choose between the two standard — and equally unsatisfactory — versions of this period: either portraying the Indians as passive victims or the Spaniards as all-powerful."

**Source challenges**: All post-1532 records are generated by Spaniards or by Andeans writing in Spanish/through Spanish institutional channels. "The standard bias of the written word, which is a bias toward the view of an elite, is multiplied by the fact that all our data about Andean people undergoes a process of translation and interpretation through a different culture." Before 1532 only archaeological records exist.

**Demography debate**: Estimates for the pre-conquest Andean population range from ~9 million to 32 million. Noble David Cook's *Demographic Collapse* (1981) is the standard work; Carlos Sempat Assadourian has questioned some data. Maria Rostworowski has argued that population may have already declined under Inka rule before Spanish arrival.

**Agency debate**: Steve Stern (*Peru's Indian Peoples and the Challenge of Spanish Conquest*, 1982/1993) pioneered the agency perspective for Huamanga; Spalding's *Huarochiri* (1984) applied it to the Lima region. Carlos Sempat Assadourian's *Transiciones hacia el sistema colonial andino* (1994) provides the most detailed analysis of kuraka–Church–encomienda political dynamics.

**Toledo's legacy**: Peruvian historiography has traditionally credited Toledo as "the great organizer," but Spalding argues the destructuration was largely accomplished before him, and his laws were poorly enforced. The "golden age" of Toledo is described as a later colonial myth used to argue for re-imposition of centralized control.

**Taki Unquy**: Spalding notes the movement is documented only in self-serving reports by the priest Cristobal de Albornoz petitioning for promotion; Gabriela Ramos has cast doubt on Albornoz's depiction of his own role. The movement's real scale and ideology remain debated.
