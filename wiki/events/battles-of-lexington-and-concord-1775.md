# The Battles of Lexington and Concord (19 April 1775)

> **⚔️ Staff-college tactical analysis:** see **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/battles-of-lexington-and-concord|Lexington & Concord — Tactical Analysis]]** in the warfare hub for the full order of battle, the phased conduct of the action, and the principles-of-war assessment.

The first shots of the **[[american-revolution|American Revolution]]**. On the night of 18–19
April 1775, General **Thomas Gage** — military governor of Massachusetts under the Coercive Acts —
dispatched roughly **900 light infantry and grenadiers** toward Concord, expecting to return to Boston by 8 a.m. But intelligence of the
expedition had already leaked; **[[revere-paul|Paul Revere]] and William Dawes** rode to raise the militia.

## Narrative

### Orders, targets, and the alarm ([[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Schiff]])

Lord **Dartmouth**’s late-January order (second copy to Gage **16 April**) treated American unrest as the work of “a rude rabble, without plan, without concert, and without conduct” and directed imprisonment of malefactors even at risk of hostilities—strike before opposition solidified. Adams’s name stood first among principal actors and abettors; whether to try in London or hang in Boston remained open; arrest only if prosecution could be secured. By spring, Gage knew arresting popular leaders would trigger the hostilities he meant to avoid (humanity and woeful outnumbering); he told Dartmouth that arresting Adams would be the last letter London ever received—“knocked on the head.”

Gage’s **written** orders stressed Concord munitions: dismantle carriages, upend powder, burn tents, scatter balls—**no arrests** on paper (London had not yet emphasized stores the same way). Multiple officers later said the expedition’s business was stores *and* “the bodies of Messrs. Hancock and Adams.” Concord, aware since late March, had carted off cannons and flour and buried musket balls. Evening of **18 April**: ~eight British officers near Lexington with pistols under overcoats; local militia put a 10–12-man guard on the **Clarke parsonage**, where [[adams-samuel|Samuel Adams]] and [[hancock-john|John Hancock]] lodged.

Revere, after near-encounter with British officers, rushed from Dr. Joseph Warren (last remaining patriot leader in occupied Boston); boat to Charlestown then ~twelve miles west; Warren had already dispatched an earlier rider by a longer route. Both sped toward Adams and Hancock—“secret and speedy intelligence”: British regiments on the move; the two leaders their quarry. **Dawes** arrived ~half an hour later. Midway Lexington–Concord, Revere, Dawes, and companions rode into an ambush; officers knew Revere, clapped a pistol to his head; his horse was taken; he returned on foot ~**2:30 a.m.** Adams labored to impress on Hancock that their place was not the battlefield—“That is not our business; we belong to the cabinet.” They fled by coach toward Woburn hours before the engagement (later a swamp dive when regulars approached); Adams exulted “O! What a glorious morning is this!” (Hancock mistook weather for politics). Neither Adams nor Hancock heard a shot at Lexington that morning.

### The fighting

At Lexington the regulars met a small militia company; a shot of unknown origin opened a brief,
one-sided fight. After the unexpected engagement, regulars marched the six miles to Concord (~**9:00 a.m.**), destroyed what cannon/powder/balls they found, and began the long retreat under continuous fire from militia swarming cover. By dark return to Charlestown, roughly a **third** of those who had set out were dead or wounded—an eighteen-mile retreat “charted by smoke.” West Point / standard military tallies: British losses exceeded **270, with 65 dead**; American losses “just under 100.” The countryside rose, and a provincial army gathered around Boston. Gage initiated the “unnatural rebellion” he had hoped to avoid—without capturing the large cache carted off days earlier.

### Immediate political management

Adams insisted Lexington (Concord “not yet in the picture”) would be “famed in the history of this country”; all depended on proving the regulars fired first. Before armed escort rescued him from Worcester he arranged accounts of the encounter to London by **four channels**—faction report beat Gage’s official dispatch by nearly two weeks. Roads thick with overloaded carts; town records underground; “a thousand uncertain and different reports.” Essential to certify the assault unprovoked—sister colonies insisted Massachusetts act only on the defensive. Adams termed the opening “this important glorious crisis,” still for some weeks a civil war.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Gage's attempt to **disarm the rebellion** by a coup de main against its supplies and
  leaders, under the militarized regime imposed by the **Coercive Acts (1774)**.
- `contributed_to:` the colonial **alarm-and-muster system** and prior intelligence, which turned a
  raid into an ambush.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` open war and the **[[siege-of-boston-1775|siege of Boston]]**.
- `produced:` the impetus for Congress to create the **[[continental-army|Continental Army]]** (June
  1775) and appoint [[washington-george|Washington]].

## Historiography

The identity of whoever fired the "shot heard round the world" at Lexington is a classic unresolved question — contemporary depositions taken by both sides (the Massachusetts Provincial Congress and British officers) already disagreed, and no definitive attribution has ever been established. Paul Revere's own later account and the depositions collected within days by Patriot authorities are invaluable but not neutral, having been assembled explicitly to build a political case that the British fired first; British officers' reports offer a countervailing but similarly interested perspective. Historians continue to debate how spontaneous versus organized the militia response was, given the sophistication of the alarm system that assembled thousands of men within hours. The battle's place as the acknowledged "start" of the war is a matter of retrospective political framing as much as a clean military fact, since low-level friction had been building for months.

[[schiff-revolutionary-samuel-adams-2022|Schiff]] layers the political targets and propaganda race: Gage’s paper orders vs officers’ later claims about seizing Adams and Hancock; Adams’s maxim that “Facts were facts but could always stand a little polish”; multi-channel London narrative management; and the king’s initial dismissal that well-drilled regulars could not have retreated before “ragtag American farmers.” Revere waited twenty-three years to reveal the full arrest story—mythic tetrameter versus operational intelligence.
