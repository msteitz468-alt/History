# Remilitarization of the Rhineland (1936)

## Narrative

On 7 March 1936, German troops marched into the demilitarized Rhineland, unilaterally ending the military restrictions imposed by the Treaty of Versailles (1919) and endorsed by the Locarno Pact (1925). The move was a calculated gamble by Hitler that destroyed the last major constraints of the post-war settlement on Germany's western borders and marked a decisive step in the 'diplomatic revolution' that overturned the European order.

Under Versailles, Germany was prohibited from erecting fortifications, stationing troops, or undertaking military preparations on the left bank of the Rhine and within a 50 km strip on the right bank. Locarno had made this status a cornerstone of western security. Any unilateral change threatened the basis of that security arrangement.

The remilitarization had long been on the agenda of German nationalists and was viewed by the army as essential for rearmament plans established in December 1933 and for western defence. The Foreign Ministry had assumed it would eventually be achieved through negotiation. Hitler had spoken of it confidentially since 1934 and again in summer 1935. By late 1935 the French anticipated a fait accompli.

The opportunity crystallized in early 1936 from the Abyssinian crisis. Mussolini's war against Ethiopia provoked League condemnation and sanctions, shattering the Stresa Front (Britain, France, Italy). Mussolini signalled in January 1936 that he had no objection to Austria becoming a German satellite and publicly indicated that Stresa was 'finally dead'. In conversations with Ambassador Hassell he made clear that Italy would offer no support to France and Britain if Germany responded to the Franco-Soviet pact by acting in the Rhineland. The Abyssinian crisis also widened the rift between Britain and France (especially after the leaked Hoare-Laval Plan). French ministers and military leadership (grossly exaggerating German strength) opposed military retaliation; reaction would be political only. Soundings indicated Britain would also refrain from military action. The chances of success for a swift coup were assessed as high.

Domestic considerations reinforced the timing. The winter of 1935–36 had brought a severe food crisis, raw-material shortages threatening rearmament, and reports of sagging party morale and poor economic conditions especially in the Rhineland (where Catholic-Church tensions were also acute). Neurath noted to Hassell (18 February) that 'for Hitler in the first instance domestic motives were decisive'. Hitler himself spoke of appealing 'both abroad, as well as to his own nation'. A dramatic national triumph would dispel the 'food crisis' and 'Church struggle' problems overnight, reinvigorate the masses and activists, and strengthen the regime at home. Elections and a plebiscite were immediately scheduled for 29 March.

Hitler pondered the matter at the Winter Olympics in Garmisch-Partenkirchen (early February). Rumours circulated in Berlin. He invited objections, particularly from the Foreign Office and military. On or around late February/early March he decided to act. The timing was set to coincide with Heroes' Memorial Day (8 March) for maximum propaganda effect. The move bore Hitler's hallmark throughout: the seizing of the moment, the element of surprise and drama, the reliance on bluff.

German troops (limited in number to avoid provocation) entered the zone. There was no French or British military response. Protests were issued (French and Italian sharper than British), but the fait accompli stood. At home, delirious crowds and euphoric acclaim greeted the news. Hitler had once again demonstrated that 'boldness over caution' succeeded. The outcome was, in his eyes and those of contemporaries, his greatest triumph to date. It further enhanced his popularity, his self-belief ('I go with the certainty of a sleepwalker...'), and the Führer myth.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Hitler's personal decision on timing and execution (key gamble after prior successes leaving League 1933 and conscription 1935).

`contributed_to:` Abyssinian crisis (Mussolini's signals + Anglo-French rift + Stresa collapse); French political/military reluctance; British inclination to accommodate German demands; shared nationalist aims of rearmament and Versailles revision among military, diplomats, and party.

`preceded_by:` Conscription announcement (16 March 1935); Poland non-aggression pact (Jan 1934); internal army-party reconciliation (3 Jan 1935 speech).

Hitler supplied the decisive timing and theatrical boldness. The broad revisionist aims were widely shared; 'little else was Hitler’s own work.' Domestic pressures (food shortages, morale) made a coup attractive as a 'new national slogan to fire the masses again.'

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Immediate destruction of Locarno/Versailles constraints on the Rhineland; massive boost to Hitler's domestic standing and myth; elections/plebiscite confirming support; accelerated rearmament path and western defence planning.

`enabled:` Further loosening of European state system ('house of cards'); enhanced readiness of élites to accept Hitler's supremacy; precedent for subsequent coups (Anschluss, Munich).

`concurrent_with:` Ongoing rearmament strains and Four Year Plan preparations.

The success reinforced the 'working towards the Führer' dynamic: subordinates saw that anticipating and supporting bold moves aligned with the Führer's will paid off handsomely.

## Actors

- Primary: Hitler (decision, timing, public justification); Blomberg, Neurath, Fritsch (military/diplomatic execution and acquiescence); Mussolini (enabling signals).
- Secondary: French and British governments (weak responses); Wehrmacht (operational implementation); Rhineland population and Gauleiter (rapturous reception).

## Historiography

Kershaw presents the Rhineland coup as the culminating demonstration (to 1936) of how Hitler's personalized rule fused with structural opportunities. Emphasis on his 'gambling instinct, use of bluff, and sharp antennae for the weak spots of his opponents.' He took the key decisions alone; military leaders were often astonished and initially opposed on timing/risk grounds but came round. Domestic motives (explicitly noted by Neurath and Hitler) are given weight alongside foreign opportunity. The episode is central to the 'working towards the Führer' chapter: success accredited to Hitler intensified the cult and radicalization dynamic. Cross-reference to intentionalist/structuralist debates and to later sources on how the 1936 triumphs paved the road to 1938–39.

**Collection coverage note (from range):** Excellent primary-grounded narrative in Kershaw *Hubris* (detailed on decision mechanics, quotes from Hassell, Goebbels, Rosenberg, Dietrich, contemporary diplomats). Strong on interaction of domestic and foreign drivers.

Concurring details (ingested 2026-07-02): only 3,000 troops moved deep into
the zone, padded with police units to seem more numerous, against a France
that believed German strength ten times the reality; the cabinet was not
told until the last minute. Domestic drivers (food shortages, the Church
conflict) needed a coup. Popular reaction was pride shot through with fear
of war (Sopade agents reported only Nazis celebrating; the 98.9% plebiscite
of 29 March was rigged). The consequence Evans stresses: France lost the
physical ability to enforce Versailles by occupying the Ruhr, tipping
Eastern Europe's small states toward accommodation with Berlin; Hitler's
"sleepwalker" remark (14 March 1936) marks the crystallizing invincibility
