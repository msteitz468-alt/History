# Siege of Petersburg (1864–1865)

A nearly ten-month siege of the rail hub of Petersburg, Virginia, fought
between Grant's Union forces and Lee's Army of Northern Virginia from June
1864 to April 1865. It effectively ended the war of maneuver in the Eastern
Theater and substituted extended trench warfare, foreshadowing the
entrenched fighting of the First World War.

## Narrative

After the bloody indecision of the Overland Campaign, Grant crossed the
James River and attempted to seize Petersburg by surprise before Lee's army
could arrive; a series of missed opportunities allowed Confederate defenders
to establish a fortified line. Extended siege operations followed, including
the failed Union assault at the Battle of the Crater (July 1864) and
repeated efforts to sever the Weldon and South Side Railroads supplying both
Petersburg and Richmond. Sheridan's cavalry victory at Five Forks (1 April
1865) finally broke the Confederate right flank, forcing Lee to abandon both
Petersburg and Richmond on 2–3 April.

## Causal Analysis

The siege was a direct outgrowth of Grant's shift to a war of attrition once
the Overland Campaign failed to destroy Lee's army outright; Confederate
manpower and rail-supply shortages made an extended static defense the only
viable option, but that defense could not indefinitely withstand Union
numerical and logistical superiority.

## Consequence Analysis

The fall of Petersburg and Richmond precipitated Lee's retreat toward
Appomattox and his surrender within a week, effectively ending large-scale
organized Confederate resistance in Virginia.

## Historiography

The campaign is extensively documented through official records and
participant memoirs on both sides; historiographical debate centers less on
facts than on the campaign's significance as a preview of industrial-age
siege warfare, and on whether Grant's costly Overland approach was
strategically necessary given that Petersburg's fall was ultimately achieved
through attrition and maneuver rather than direct assault.
