# Collapse of the Soviet Union, 1990–1991

The final dissolution of the USSR, including the August 1991 coup, declarations of independence by republics, and Gorbachev's resignation.

Detailed analysis and claims from CHCW Vol. III (Pravda chapter).

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] supply a detailed economic
and political chronology of the endgame. Economically: net Soviet income fell **17% in
1991**, a steeper single-year contraction than any year of the US Great Depression.
Politically, the Baltic states led the way: independence declarations followed in
March–May 1990; on **August 23, 1989** (the 50th anniversary of the Nazi-Soviet Pact)
roughly **2 million Balts** formed a human chain across Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania in
protest. Soviet crackdowns followed in **Georgia (April 1989)** and **Baku/Azerbaijan
(January 1990)**, and — as already noted on [[gorbachev-mikhail]] — in **Lithuania and
Latvia (January 1991)**, killing 13. The failed **Union Treaty of April 1991** secured
agreement from only 9 of the 15 republics; the Baltics, Moldova, Armenia, and Georgia
held back. The **Ukrainian independence referendum of December 1, 1991** (90% in favor,
including the Russian-speaking east) is identified as the "fatal blow" that made the
union's continuation impossible — consistent with the account on [[yeltsin-boris]] of
the Belovezha Accords eight days later.

## Historiography

Explanations divide between structure and agency: long-run economic
sclerosis and the burden of empire (Kotkin's "Armageddon averted" framing)
versus Gorbachev's contingent choices — glasnost destroying the party's
legitimacy faster than perestroika could build a replacement, and his refusal
to use force holding the door open for the republics. Nationality-driven
accounts stress the Baltic and Ukrainian secessions as the proximate solvent.
[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]] presents the collapse as unintended
by every major actor, including Yeltsin until late 1991.

## McCoy's Reading

[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|McCoy (2024)]] opens his account of the collapse
by arguing that mass popular protest — the Baltic "singing revolution" and 1989 human
chain, the East European "autumn revolutions," and the civil resistance that defeated
the [[august-coup-1991|August 1991 coup]] — was as decisive as elite decisions
("Gorbachev was no more the architect of that historic change than Bush himself"). He
also advances the contested claim, recorded on
[[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis|his controversy page]], that the
[[central-intelligence-agency|CIA]]'s Afghan operation helped precipitate the collapse,
and that post-1991 NATO expansion — which George Kennan called "the most fateful error
of American policy in the entire post-cold-war era" — catalyzed later Russian
revanchism rather than merely responding to it.
