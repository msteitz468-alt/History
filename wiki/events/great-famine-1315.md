# Great Famine of 1315–1322

## Narrative

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] presents the **great famine** that began in **1315** as the first of two fourteenth-century scourges of massive mortality (the second being the [[black-death|Black Death]]). Often forgotten relative to the plague, it was devastating in its own right. Severest impact ran to **1317**, in some regions to **1322**. Cold winters and wet summers cut food crops across **northern Europe** roughly north of an Alps–Lyons–La Rochelle line, including the British Isles (except northern Scotland), the Baltic to southern Scandinavia and Poland. **Germany, northern France, and Flanders** were worst hit.

French wheat yields are estimated down ~50%; London wheat prices nearly **500%** higher; Holland fish similarly inflated. Livestock disease devastated herds (e.g. Ramsey estates cattle collapses). Mortality estimates run as high as **15–20%** in affected places; **Ypres** lost 10% of its population from May to October 1316 alone; **Tournai** 1316 mortality was 250% above usual; new cemeteries appeared across Flanders, Brabant, Germany, and Slovakia.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` multi-year climatic failure (cold winters, wet summers) destroying cereal harvests and compounding livestock epizootics.
- `contributed_to:` market price spikes that priced food beyond the reach of the poor even where grain still moved commercially.
- `preceded_by:` high late-thirteenth-century population pressure on marginal land (Logan’s broader calamitous-century framing).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` tens of thousands of deaths in Flanders and northern France alone; demographic and economic shock across northern Europe.
- `produced:` an “almost universal crisis for the northern European church” (Jordan, quoted by Logan): religious leaders died (three abbesses of Reinsburg in 1316; many Belgian house heads 1316–19); houses sold lands or went into debt—German evidence of drastic survival measures.
- `enabled:` a weakened demographic and nutritional baseline when plague arrived a generation later (`followed_by: [[black-death]]`).

## Actors

Populations and institutions rather than named protagonists: urban poor, peasantries, monastic and episcopal houses forced into land sales and debt.

## Historiography

Logan pairs famine with plague as the dual mortality crisis of the fourteenth century, while noting that Avignon is the more familiar “church history” story of the same decades. William Chester Jordan’s *Great Famine* underpins the institutional church-crisis claim. Quantitative mortality and price series remain regionally uneven; local studies (Ypres, Tournai, manorial herds) are the hard anchors.
