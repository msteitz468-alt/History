## Narrative

By approximately 550 CE, or perhaps slightly earlier, [[teotihuacan]]'s population had begun a measurable decline. The Metepec ceramic phase, the city's last, correlates with this period of contraction. Parts of the city — especially its periphery — were progressively abandoned. The population probably decreased by at least a third and possibly by more than half (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 9).

Material indicators of decline accumulated during this terminal phase. Locally-made hemispherical bowls imitating Thin Orange Ware appeared, suggesting that imported goods from southern Puebla were becoming harder to obtain. In the La Ventilla district, garbage accumulated in streets, ultimately blocking drains; gates were erected blocking some streets; over two meters of midden accumulated in a street outside the Yayahuala compound. As Cowgill notes, "civic services were breaking down in Teotihuacan's last years and people were more wary of their neighbors" (Ch. 9). Signs of rapid abandonment were also identified in La Ventilla — artisans' tools and partly finished objects were left in place rather than carried away, suggesting precipitate departure.

Then major structures in the civic-ceremonial core and some elite residences were systematically burned. **Dating is an attributed tension across sources, not a single settled year:** this page’s slug retains ~**550 CE** as one scholarly anchor (Beramendi-Orosco et al. archaeomagnetic readings and related ceramic arguments); Cowgill’s 2015 monograph places burning probably around **600–650 CE**; Cowgill’s CHNPA chapter (2000) says **perhaps around A.D. 650, but possibly a century later**, with recent radiocarbon allowing a century or more earlier; Adams’s CHNPA introduction states that elite direction failed by **A.D. 650** and transitional centers began to fill the power vacuum outside the Basin. Absolute dates for central Mexico may be off by a century or more (Cowgill 2000).

A 6 cm layer of ash and charcoal was found atop the eastern outer platform of the [[ciudadela-teotihuacan]], where a 60 cm stone male statuette was smashed and its fragments widely scattered. Cowgill personally observed composite censers with diagnostically Metepec flush-rim flanges smashed in place atop the latest concrete floor at the rear of the [[feathered-serpent-pyramid]], placing the burning at or near the end of the Metepec phase rather than its beginning (Cowgill 2015).

## Causal Analysis

The causes of collapse are genuinely contested and probably multiple. They can be grouped into three broad categories.

**Internal sociopolitical and economic factors.** Growing disparities in wealth among the city's residents are documented archaeologically: Ian Robertson's neighborhood studies found markers of wealth inequality increasing from the Miccaotli through Xolalpan phases. Martha Sempowski's grave-offering studies show that in some compounds offerings were markedly impoverished relative to earlier periods. Manzanilla's work at the Teopancazco compound suggests that late in Teotihuacan's history "intermediate elites" — persons with status and wealth independent of the state — may have increasingly siphoned off surpluses formerly accruing to the state, weakening its capacity to function. Cowgill also raises the possibility of irreversible bureaucratic proliferation: after the widespread construction of residential compounds, "materially tangible innovation almost ceased" for several centuries (Ch. 9). Millon proposed growing ideological rigidity as a contributing factor.

**Environmental factors.** Evidence is conflicting. Some paleoclimatic data suggest a drier interval around this period in western Mexico, but Emily McClung's work in the Teotihuacan Valley finds relatively high humidity persisting without major deforestation or other environmental degradation, with the clearest drought signals appearing several centuries later at the time of Tula's collapse.

**External pressure.** The Coyotlatelco ceramic complex that follows Teotihuacan styles differs markedly and discontinuously from Teotihuacan ceramics. Mastache and Cobean see Coyotlatelco antecedents in areas several hundred kilometers to the west, including the Bajío; Healan and Hernandez find especially close resemblances in ceramics from around Ucareo. Linguistic, biological, archaeological, and ethnohistoric evidence (Beekman and Christensen 2003) points toward a significant migration of Náhuatl speakers into Central Mexico around this time. Cowgill's current best assessment: "internal social, political, and economic troubles, perhaps especially growing power of intermediate elites, weakened the Teotihuacan state to an extent that made it vulnerable and an attractive target for invasion by people from West Mexico" (Ch. 9).

**Who burned it?** No single agent is identified. Millon stresses violence and selectivity — most outer residences abandoned without burning — implying **insiders and pent-up anger**. Cowgill notes **outsiders** could also identify state temples and palaces and might thoroughly destroy ritual settings to disable Teotihuacan as a political force. Either way, vulnerability required **prior systemic decline**: “What ended was not just a dynasty, it was the belief system that had supported the state” (Cowgill 2000). Internal revolt and external conquest are not mutually exclusive if breakdown preceded intervention.

**Tula and northwest migrants.** [[tula-hidalgo|Tula]] itself could not have destroyed Teotihuacan (not yet significant). Ancestors of Tula’s builders may have moved in after Teotihuacan was already too weak to prevent them, adding problems and hastening final collapse without yet having a powerful center of their own (Cowgill 2000). Ceramic and linguistic evidence for substantial northwest migration as Teotihuacan declined is consonant but not ironclad (Coyotlatelco affinities; Nahua entry timing).

## Consequence Analysis

The Teotihuacan state ended. The city was probably briefly near-abandoned (poorly understood Oxtoticpac complex) and then reoccupied, but subsequent occupation concentrated outside the former civic-ceremonial core — the makers of Coyotlatelco ceramics show "limited occupation of the former civic-ceremonial core … suggesting that they wanted to dissociate themselves symbolically from the Teotihuacan state" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 9). Multiple high-density Coyotlatelco areas within the city suggest decentralized political organization with no successor to Teotihuacan's central authority.

**Material rupture (Cowgill 2000):** talud-tablero temples and the apartment-compound residential type disappear; cylindrical vases, Thin Orange, candeleros, composite censers, and San Martín Orange vanish; cooking vessels reshape. City soon large again under Xometla/Coyotlatelco — ~12 km², ~40,000 people; greatest spatial shift since Patlachique; abrupt change suggests considerable ethnically different in-migration, possibly Nahua speakers. Mazapan-phase Teotihuacan remained large (average population unlikely much below ~30,000).

The Epiclassic interval (roughly 550/650–850/950 CE) saw the growth of several competing centers: [[xochicalco]] in Morelos, [[cacaxtla]] in Puebla-Tlaxcala, Cantona east of the highlands, and [[el-tajin|El Tajín]] in northern Veracruz; [[cholula]] may have spanned the transition more than any other central Mexican center but remains enigmatic. The Basin's single-dominant-center pattern gave way to a more dispersed, competitive settlement system. Charlton (CHNPA Ch. 11) treats this epi-Teotihuacan city-state landscape as structurally parallel to the post-[[fall-of-tula-1150|Tula]] fragmentation that later resolved into the Aztec macrostate.

**Relation to Maya collapse.** Cowgill (2000) argues Teotihuacan’s decline in the 500s–600s **cannot have been a central factor** in the much later lowland [[maya-classic-collapse-800ce|Maya collapse]] if influence was more ideational than imperial, though Maya internecine warfare may have intensified once Mesoamerica was no longer overshadowed by Teotihuacan. This sits as an attributed position beside network-disruption readings in other sources.

San Martín Orange Ware — the specialized utilitarian ceramic that had flourished in the Tlajinga district — disappeared: "the fact that San Martín Orange did not survive the collapse of the Teotihuacan state is probably not because of any ideological aversion to it but because market conditions would no longer support this degree of specialization" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 7). Candeleros and composite censers also vanished, replaced by different ceramic ritual forms.

## Actors

Primary actors are unknown: no named rulers or generals are attested in the Teotihuacan archaeological record. The state itself — [[teotihuacan]] — is the primary actor in decline. "Intermediate elites" within the city (Manzanilla's formulation) are implicated as internal stressors. Probable West Mexican migrants/invaders are inferred from ceramic and linguistic evidence but remain archaeologically anonymous.

## Historiography

The chronology of collapse remains disputed and must be reported as **attributed alternatives**, not merged into a false consensus:

| Attribution | Approximate date / claim |
|---|---|
| Event slug / Beramendi-Orosco et al. (2009) archaeomagnetic | Burning ~**550 CE**; Metepec as post-burning occupation (contested) |
| Cowgill 2015 (*Ancient Teotihuacan*) | Burning probably ~**600–650 CE**; Metepec-diagnostic censers in burn layer argue against early-Metepec destruction |
| Cowgill 2000 (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 6) | **Perhaps ~A.D. 650**, possibly a century later; recent 14C may push earlier by a century+; dates may be off by a century or more |
| Adams (CHNPA intro) | Elite direction failed by **A.D. 650**; transitional centers fill power vacuum outside Basin |

Cowgill 2015 argues against a pure 550-burning/Metepec-as-aftermath model on ceramic grounds — Metepec-diagnostic censers appear smashed in the burning layer at the FSP. Better definition of the Metepec ceramic complex is still needed.

The cause of the burning is similarly debated. Sanders (2006) and others emphasize internal socioeconomic collapse, with Coyotlatelco derivative from Teotihuacan antecedents. Mastache and Cobean, and more recently Healan and Hernandez, argue Coyotlatelco antecedents lie to the west. Millon emphasizes insider selectivity of burning; Cowgill leaves agents open (insiders, outsiders, or combination) while insisting systemic decline preceded vulnerability. Survivors likely scattered and lost cultural identity (possible [[cholula]] continuities in later tradition). Whether any surviving Teotihuacanos retained visible ethnic identity is debated: Cowgill is skeptical that descendants kept archaeologically visible markers beyond a generation or two.
