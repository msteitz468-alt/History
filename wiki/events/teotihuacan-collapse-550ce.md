## Narrative

By approximately 550 CE, or perhaps slightly earlier, [[teotihuacan]]'s population had begun a measurable decline. The Metepec ceramic phase, the city's last, correlates with this period of contraction. Parts of the city — especially its periphery — were progressively abandoned. The population probably decreased by at least a third and possibly by more than half (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 9).

Material indicators of decline accumulated during this terminal phase. Locally-made hemispherical bowls imitating Thin Orange Ware appeared, suggesting that imported goods from southern Puebla were becoming harder to obtain. In the La Ventilla district, garbage accumulated in streets, ultimately blocking drains; gates were erected blocking some streets; over two meters of midden accumulated in a street outside the Yayahuala compound. As Cowgill notes, "civic services were breaking down in Teotihuacan's last years and people were more wary of their neighbors" (Ch. 9). Signs of rapid abandonment were also identified in La Ventilla — artisans' tools and partly finished objects were left in place rather than carried away, suggesting precipitate departure.

Then, probably around 600–650 CE (though some evidence points to 550 CE), major structures in the civic-ceremonial core and some elite residences were systematically burned. A 6 cm layer of ash and charcoal was found atop the eastern outer platform of the [[ciudadela-teotihuacan]], where a 60 cm stone male statuette was smashed and its fragments widely scattered. Cowgill personally observed composite censers with diagnostically Metepec flush-rim flanges smashed in place atop the latest concrete floor at the rear of the [[feathered-serpent-pyramid]], placing the burning at or near the end of the Metepec phase rather than its beginning.

## Causal Analysis

The causes of collapse are genuinely contested and probably multiple. They can be grouped into three broad categories.

**Internal sociopolitical and economic factors.** Growing disparities in wealth among the city's residents are documented archaeologically: Ian Robertson's neighborhood studies found markers of wealth inequality increasing from the Miccaotli through Xolalpan phases. Martha Sempowski's grave-offering studies show that in some compounds offerings were markedly impoverished relative to earlier periods. Manzanilla's work at the Teopancazco compound suggests that late in Teotihuacan's history "intermediate elites" — persons with status and wealth independent of the state — may have increasingly siphoned off surpluses formerly accruing to the state, weakening its capacity to function. Cowgill also raises the possibility of irreversible bureaucratic proliferation: after the widespread construction of residential compounds, "materially tangible innovation almost ceased" for several centuries (Ch. 9). Millon proposed growing ideological rigidity as a contributing factor.

**Environmental factors.** Evidence is conflicting. Some paleoclimatic data suggest a drier interval around this period in western Mexico, but Emily McClung's work in the Teotihuacan Valley finds relatively high humidity persisting without major deforestation or other environmental degradation, with the clearest drought signals appearing several centuries later at the time of Tula's collapse.

**External pressure.** The Coyotlatelco ceramic complex that follows Teotihuacan styles differs markedly and discontinuously from Teotihuacan ceramics. Mastache and Cobean see Coyotlatelco antecedents in areas several hundred kilometers to the west, including the Bajío; Healan and Hernandez find especially close resemblances in ceramics from around Ucareo. Linguistic, biological, archaeological, and ethnohistoric evidence (Beekman and Christensen 2003) points toward a significant migration of Náhuatl speakers into Central Mexico around this time. Cowgill's current best assessment: "internal social, political, and economic troubles, perhaps especially growing power of intermediate elites, weakened the Teotihuacan state to an extent that made it vulnerable and an attractive target for invasion by people from West Mexico" (Ch. 9).

**Who burned it?** The text does not identify a single responsible agent. Internal revolt and external conquest are both plausible; the two are not mutually exclusive if internal breakdown preceded military intervention. The systematic destruction of civic-ceremonial structures rather than residential areas suggests deliberate targeting of state apparatus.

## Consequence Analysis

The Teotihuacan state ended. The city was probably briefly abandoned and then reoccupied, but subsequent occupation concentrated in residential areas outside the former civic-ceremonial core — the makers of Coyotlatelco ceramics show "limited occupation of the former civic-ceremonial core … suggesting that they wanted to dissociate themselves symbolically from the Teotihuacan state" (Ch. 9). Multiple high-density Coyotlatelco areas within the city suggest decentralized political organization with no successor to Teotihuacan's central authority.

The Epiclassic interval (roughly 550/650–850 CE) saw the growth of several competing centers: Xochicalco in Morelos, Cacaxtla-Xochitécatl in Puebla, Cantona east of the highlands, and El Tajín in northern Veracruz. The Basin of Mexico's earlier pattern of a single dominant center gave way to a more dispersed, competitive settlement system.

Teotihuacan's decline was roughly contemporary with the florescence of the southern [[maya-civilization|Maya Lowlands]] Classic (600–850 CE), and the two phenomena may be related through the disruption of long-established exchange networks.

San Martín Orange Ware — the specialized utilitarian ceramic that had flourished in the Tlajinga district — disappeared: "the fact that San Martín Orange did not survive the collapse of the Teotihuacan state is probably not because of any ideological aversion to it but because market conditions would no longer support this degree of specialization" (Ch. 7). Candeleros and composite censers also vanished, replaced by different ceramic ritual forms.

## Actors

Primary actors are unknown: no named rulers or generals are attested in the Teotihuacan archaeological record. The state itself — [[teotihuacan]] — is the primary actor in decline. "Intermediate elites" within the city (Manzanilla's formulation) are implicated as internal stressors. Probable West Mexican migrants/invaders are inferred from ceramic and linguistic evidence but remain archaeologically anonymous.

## Historiography

The chronology of collapse remains disputed. Beramendi-Orosco et al. (2009), relying on archaeomagnetic dates, place the burning around 550 CE and treat the Metepec phase as post-burning occupation. Cowgill argues against this on ceramic grounds — Metepec-diagnostic censers appear smashed in the burning layer at the FSP, placing destruction at or near the end of Metepec rather than its beginning. Better definition of the Metepec ceramic complex is needed to resolve this.

The cause of the burning is similarly debated. Sanders (2006) and others emphasize internal socioeconomic collapse, with the Coyotlatelco complex derivative from Teotihuacan antecedents. Mastache and Cobean, and more recently Healan and Hernandez, argue that Coyotlatelco antecedents lie to the west, suggesting an influx of outsiders. Cowgill regards these as complementary rather than exclusive. The question of whether any surviving Teotihuacanos retained visible ethnic identity is also debated: Cowgill is skeptical, arguing that within a generation or two descendants probably shed archaeologically visible Teotihuacan markers.
