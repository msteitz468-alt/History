# Behistun Inscription (Darius I's account of his accession and the suppression of the rebellions)

## Narrative
In 522 BCE, after the death of Cambyses II in Egypt, a Magian named Gaumata seized the throne by claiming to be Bardiya (Smerdis), the son of Cyrus the Great. Darius, son of Hystaspes and an Achaemenid, with the support of six Persian nobles and the favor of Ahuramazda, killed Gaumata at the fortress of Sikayauvatiš in Media and claimed the kingship.

Darius then faced rebellions in nine provinces (Elam, Babylon, Media, Sagartia, Margiana, and others). He fought 19 battles and captured 9 "liar-kings" in the space of one year. The inscription lists each rebellion and Darius's victory over it.

The monument at Behistun was carved to commemorate these events and to proclaim Darius's version of the truth: all his opponents were followers of "the Lie" (drauga); he himself upheld order (arta) with the help of Ahuramazda.

## Causal Analysis
The immediate cause was the power vacuum after Cambyses' death and the apparent ease with which a pretender could claim legitimacy in the absence of the king. Darius presents the revolts as the work of "the Lie," requiring violent restoration of truth and order. The inscription is royal propaganda. Modern historians debate whether Gaumata was a genuine usurper or whether Darius invented or exaggerated the story to legitimize his coup (cf. Herodotus: two Magi brothers, name Smerdis; Ctesias: Spendadates; Justin/Trogus: different sequence). The Behistun text is corrective primary evidence, stating it as surprise that no Persian, Median, or Achaemenid opposed the usurper during Cambyses' absence. The 19th-c. edition contrasts it repeatedly with Greek accounts while preserving the Magophonia feast and Darius as religious reformer. (See [[behistun-inscription]] source for vocabulary entries on Gaumata, arika/heretics, ayadana restoration, and comparisons.)

## Consequence Analysis
The successful suppression of the rebellions allowed Darius to reorganize and consolidate the empire, laying the foundations for its administrative system (satrapies, Royal Road, etc.). The Behistun inscription became a model for later Achaemenid royal statements and the key primary source for these events. A copy was disseminated as far as Elephantine in Egypt.

Provinces (from vocabulary): Athura (Assyria), Gadara/Gandhara (Indus), Asagarta (Media-dependent; Chitratakhma revolt claiming Cyaxares descent), Thatagush (Sattagydia, Hindu Kush), Khwarizm (Oxus mouth), etc. Arian etymologies imply early colonization (e.g., native names in Susiana revolt). Scythians: Sakukha (Skunkha) with pointed cap on the relief (Tigrakhuda western Saka); paradraya (European Scythians beyond the sea in Hellespont campaign). Ideology: drauga ("the Lie") vs. arta; ayadana restored; arika (heretics/Magi). Gobryas (Gaubruwa, son of Mardonius) one of the Seven conspirators; later Susiana expedition. Atrines led Susiana revolt immediately after accession (Arian name). Arakha (Armenian) in Babylon as Nabochodrossor pretender.

## Historiography
The Behistun text is royal propaganda and must be read against Greek accounts. It is, however, contemporary and in the king's own language and voice. The 19th-century decipherment of the Old Persian version (by Rawlinson and others) made the inscription the foundation for the recovery of Achaemenid history.
