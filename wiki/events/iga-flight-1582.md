## Narrative

In the fifth month of 1582, Tokugawa Ieyasu was visiting Sakai and Kyoto as a guest of [[oda-nobunaga]], accompanied by only a small retinue of senior retainers including Sakai Tadatsugu, Ishikawa Kazumasa, Ii Naomasa, Sakakibara Yasumasa, and Okubo Tadachika. He received sudden news — delivered by Chaya Shirojiro on horseback — that Akechi Mitsuhide had revolted, stormed the Honnōji where Nobunaga was staying, set fire to it, and forced his death.

Ieyasu's first response was to propose marching toward the capital to die with Nobunaga or avenge him. Honda Tadakatsu persuaded him that a more effective loyalty would be to escape, raise troops in Mikawa, and then strike at Akechi. The group turned away from the capital toward the Kizugawa, with Honda forcing crossing with a commandeered boat and destroying it behind them to prevent pursuit.

The journey through Iga province was treacherous: the previous year Nobunaga had ordered the "thorough extermination" of the Iga samurai. Fortunately for Ieyasu, some of those survivors had fled into Tokugawa territory and been treated kindly there. When they heard Ieyasu was passing through, several hundred rallied to escort him. Hattori Hanzo, one of Ieyasu's men who was a native of Iga, guided the party through the mountains.

At the small coastal port of Shirako, the merchant Kadoya Shichirojiro happened to have a large ship. He took the party aboard and a fair wind carried them to the port of Ohama in Mikawa. Sadler notes: "This experience was considered the greatest hazard of Ieyasu's life." The journey from Sakai to Okazaki took six days in total. Kadoya was afterward rewarded with a monopoly on the carrying trade in those regions.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[assassination-of-oda-nobunaga-1582]]` — The Honnōji Incident created the sudden crisis. Ieyasu had no troops in the area and was caught at maximum vulnerability, far from his home provinces.
- `contributed_to:` The prior gentleness of Ieyasu's treatment of Iga refugees who had fled Nobunaga's purge was the decisive enabling factor in crossing the province safely.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[ieyasu-seizure-of-kai-and-shinano-1582]]` — Ieyasu returned to Okazaki and within a week marched to avenge Nobunaga, but news of Hideyoshi's victory at Yamazaki reached him first; he redirected this energy toward seizing the now-leaderless province of Kai.
- `enabled: [[komaki-nagakute-campaign-1584]]` — Surviving the flight allowed Ieyasu to build the power base in five provinces from which he could contest Hideyoshi's supremacy.

## Actors

- [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] — principal figure; organized and led the escape
- Honda Tadakatsu — persuaded Ieyasu against suicide; provided armed protection throughout
- Hattori Hanzo — Iga native who guided the party through the province
- Chaya Shirojiro — Kyoto merchant who brought the first news and assisted throughout; a lifelong friend of Ieyasu
- Akechi Mitsuhide — whose revolt made the flight necessary

## Historiography

Sadler's account draws on several Japanese chronicles, including the Mikawa Go-Fudoki, and a popular "Kodan" story embellishing the flight with a dramatic ship-search episode (a spear thrust into the cargo hold that Ieyasu deflected by wiping the blade). Sadler treats this as folklore. The broad outline of the flight — the route through Iga, Hattori Hanzo's role, the Kadoya ship — is consistent across sources. The flight demonstrates a structural feature of Sengoku politics: the value of prior generosity to defeated enemies as a reserve of goodwill that could be called upon in moments of crisis.
