# Fall of Babylon to Cyrus (539 BCE)

The **fall of Babylon** to **[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus the Great]]** in October **539 BCE**
ended the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]] — the last native Mesopotamian
state — and delivered the entire ancient Near East to **[[achaemenid-empire|Persia]]**.
It is the closing event of [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 27,
Wiseman), the hinge at which the volume hands the region on to CAH Vol. IV and the
Persian age. As a genuine discontinuity it is filed as a **transition page**.

## Narrative

By 539 **[[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]** already ruled **Media, Anshan, Parsua, and Elam**,
and had conquered **Lydia** (Croesus, Sardis). Babylon under **[[nabonidus|Nabonidus]]**
was internally divided — the king long estranged from the Marduk priesthood by his
cult of Sin and his decade at Tema, his son **Belshazzar** having held the regency.
As the war clouds gathered, the gods of the outlying cities (Marad, Kish,
Khursagkalamma) were carried into Babylon for safety — "always the first defensive
step taken in anticipation of an attack from the north-east."

Cyrus marched down the **Diyala** and **stormed Opis** on the Tigris, defeating the
Babylonian defenders with heavy casualties and driving them behind the **"Median
Wall."** On **14 Tashritu, Sippar fell without a battle** and Nabonidus fled. Two
days later (**13 October 539**) the Persian general **Ugbaru (Gobryas), governor of
Gutium, entered Babylon without a battle**; his troops, shields but no weapons drawn,
guarded Esagila and the holy places so that the temple services continued
uninterrupted. **Belshazzar was slain** at the city's capture; **Nabonidus surrendered**
soon after and was spared, settled (per Berossus) in **Carmania**, where he died. On
**30 October Cyrus himself entered Babylon**, proclaiming peace, confirming the city's
*kidinnūtu* privileges, and ordering the **return of exiled gods** to their cities.

The **swiftness and bloodlessness** of the fall, reflected in both Daniel and
Herodotus, points to **collaborators and dissidents** inside the city. Herodotus's
tradition that the Persians entered through the **dried-up river bed** (the Euphrates
deflected near Opis) may preserve a memory of a commando approach beneath the walls.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **rise of [[achaemenid-empire|Persia]]** under [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]
and his prior conquest of Media, Lydia, and Elam, which encircled Babylonia.
contributed_to: by **[[nabonidus|Nabonidus's]]** religious reforms and long absence,
which "divided the political and economic leaders" and produced the pro-Persian party
inside Babylon — a faction visible in the appearance of a Persian-named official at
Eanna on the eve of the fall.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **end of the [[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian Empire]]** and the
incorporation of Mesopotamia, Syria, and the Levant into the **[[achaemenid-empire|
Persian Empire]]** — "from the Upper to the Lower seas" now Persian. Cyrus appointed
**Gubaru** as satrap and was joined by his son **Cambyses**.
enabled: the **return of the Jewish exiles** to Jerusalem, proclaimed (per the
Biblical tradition and consonant with the **Cyrus Cylinder's** policy of restoring
deported gods and peoples) after the seventy-year Exile begun at
[[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|587]].
Wiseman stresses **continuity at the social level**: "the change to Achaemenid rule
seems to have had little effect on the traditional way of life" of Babylonia.

## The "Darius the Mede" problem

Wiseman discusses the Book of Daniel's **"Darius the Mede,"** placed between Nabonidus
and Cyrus — variously explained as a confusion with Darius I, an identification with
the governor **Gubaru**, or (his preferred "reasonable solution") a cryptic
description of **Cyrus himself**, who also styled himself "King of the Medes." No
documents are dated by any such intervening king.

## Historiography

The event is exceptionally well sourced: the **Nabonidus Chronicle**, the **Cyrus
Cylinder** (Cyrus's own propaganda, crediting Marduk with delivering an impious
Nabonidus into his hands), the **Dynastic Prophecy**, dated contracts, and the later
narratives of **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]], Xenophon, Berossus, and Daniel**.
All the major sources are **partisan** — Cyrus presents himself as Marduk's liberator,
the Jews as Yahweh's instrument, the later Babylonian texts as victims — so the
question of whether Babylon greeted Cyrus "as a conqueror or as a deliverer," Wiseman
says, "it is hard to tell." This is the model case of a conquest whose legitimating
narrative was written by the victor.
