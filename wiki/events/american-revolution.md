# The American Revolution (1765–1783)

The **American Revolution** was the revolt of Britain's thirteen North American colonies that
produced the **independence of the United States** (1776/1783) and the first large modern
**republic** founded on Enlightenment principles of popular sovereignty and individual rights.
It was the opening act of the [[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]]. From
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v8|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. VIII]].

## Narrative

After the **[[seven-years-war-1756|Seven Years War]]** (1763) left Britain victorious but
deeply indebted, Parliament tried to tax the colonies (the Stamp Act 1765, the Townshend and
Tea duties), provoking resistance under the slogan **"no taxation without representation."**
Escalating confrontation (the Boston Massacre, the Boston Tea Party 1773, the Coercive Acts)
led to war at **Lexington and Concord (1775)** and the **Declaration of Independence (4 July
1776)**, drafted by Jefferson on Lockean and Enlightenment principles. The Continental Army
under **Washington** held on; the victory at **Saratoga (1777)** brought **France** into the
war (1778), followed by Spain and the Dutch, turning a colonial revolt into a global conflict.
The Franco-American victory at **Yorktown (1781)** forced British negotiation; the **Treaty of
Paris (1783)** recognized US independence. The **Constitution (1787)** then created a federal
republic.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` British attempts to **tax the colonies** to pay Seven Years War debts, colliding
  with colonial traditions of **self-government** and the principle of consent.
- `contributed_to:` **[[enlightenment|Enlightenment]]** ideas of natural rights, liberty, and
  republicanism (Locke, Montesquieu), and a vigorous colonial public sphere.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **United States** — the first modern federal democratic republic, with a
  written constitution and a bill of rights.
- `produced:` a **model and inspiration** for the [[french-revolution-1789|French Revolution]]
  and the wider [[atlantic-revolutions|Atlantic revolutions]] (its "political and psychological
  impact in Europe," NCMH VIII).
- `produced:` the near-bankruptcy of **France** (whose intervention helped trigger its own
  1789 crisis) and a British reorientation toward Asia and a "second empire."

## Military Dimension

The military history of the war — the conduct of its campaigns, treated in detail from
*[[west-point-history-american-revolution-2017|The West Point History of the American
Revolution]]* — is anchored in the dedicated **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/american-revolutionary-war|warfare-hub campaign analysis]]**. In outline:
the war opened at [[battles-of-lexington-and-concord-1775|Lexington and Concord]] and the
[[siege-of-boston-1775|siege of Boston]]; [[washington-george|Washington]]'s
[[continental-army|Continental Army]] survived the disasters of 1776
([[battle-of-long-island-1776|Long Island]]) and struck back at [[battle-of-trenton-1776|
Trenton]] and [[battle-of-princeton-1777|Princeton]]. The British took Philadelphia
([[battle-of-brandywine-1777|Brandywine]]), but [[battles-of-saratoga-1777|Saratoga]] (1777)
brought the decisive [[franco-american-alliance-1778|French alliance]]. After the army's
professionalization at [[valley-forge-1777|Valley Forge]] and the proving fight at
[[battle-of-monmouth-1778|Monmouth]], the war shifted south: British triumphs at
[[siege-of-charleston-1780|Charleston]] and [[battle-of-camden-1780|Camden]] gave way to
[[greene-nathanael|Greene]]'s war-winning attrition ([[battle-of-cowpens-1781|Cowpens]],
[[battle-of-guilford-courthouse-1781|Guilford Courthouse]]), ending in the combined-arms,
coalition victory at [[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]] and the [[treaty-of-paris-1783|
Treaty of Paris]].

The *West Point History* stresses that the **French were indispensable**: "The Continental
Army did not win the war in America; it was on the winning side." Its achievement was to
**survive** until foreign intervention — above all French sea power — could tip the balance.

## Historiography

Debated as to how "revolutionary" it was — a conservative defense of existing colonial
liberties, or a genuinely radical founding (Bailyn's ideological origins; Wood's
"radicalism"). Its limits are stressed too: slavery survived, and "all men are created equal"
excluded the enslaved, women, and Native peoples — tensions that shaped later American history.

**Hartz's consensus reading (1955).** [[hartz-louis|Louis Hartz]] ([[hartz-liberal-tradition-1955]])
reframed 1776 as a revolution *without* a social revolution: America had no ancien régime to
destroy — "the established feudal structure it did not have to destroy" was, from the European
vantage, the Revolution's most notable feature. Hence its sober, non-messianic character (no
crusade to remake the world; Dissenting clergy supplied a religion that "was already
revolutionary"), its anti-power rather than power-seizing impulse (limited government and
judicial review instead of the European liberal flirtation with enlightened despotism), and the
quick, nearly consensual "mopping up" of feudal relics (primogeniture, entail, establishment) —
Virginia's aristocrats of 1785 "went under with scarcely a blow." Hartz read Federalist
"leveller" alarms as ideological phantoms (Hamilton "had to conjure up a set of Civil Wars"
Hobbes at least had lived through), and argued the Constitution's conflict-management machinery
survived only because its Hobbesian premises were wrong for a fundamentally unified Lockean
society — "their conclusions were 'right' only because their premises were wrong." This directly
rebuts the Beardian "social revolution" reading and underpins [[consensus-history]] and the
[[lockean-consensus]].
