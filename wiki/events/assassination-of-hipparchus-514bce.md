# The Assassination of Hipparchus (514 BCE)

At the **Panathenaea of 514 BCE**, **Harmodius and Aristogeiton** — lovers from the genos
of the **Gephyraei** — struck down **[[pisistratidae|Hipparchus]]**, brother of the tyrant
Hippias. Though it did *not* end the [[greek-tyranny|tyranny]], the deed became the founding
legend of Athenian freedom, commemorated in the statues of the **Tyrannicides** and the
**Harmodius drinking-song**. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 4, D. M. Lewis) and
the ancient sources disagree sharply about why it happened.

## Narrative

Thucydides' version: **Hipparchus made advances to Harmodius**, who rebuffed him and told
his lover Aristogeiton; Hipparchus then publicly **insulted Harmodius' sister** (declaring
her unfit to be a basket-bearer in the procession). The conspirators chose the Panathenaea,
when citizens would be armed for the procession. Believing themselves betrayed to Hippias
(who was marshalling the procession in the Ceramicus), Harmodius and Aristogeiton killed
Hipparchus by the **Leocoreum**. **Harmodius was cut down at once** by the guards;
**Aristogeiton died under torture**. **Hippias kept control**, disarmed the citizens by a
ruse, and ruled on.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: **disputed.** **Thucydides** insisted (against the popular view) that the
origin was "an erotic occurrence" — a private quarrel of love and honour that only later
took on political colour; **Aristotle** broadly followed him but differs in many details
(making Thessalus, not Hipparchus, the insulter). The **general Athenian belief**, which
Herodotus does not deny, was that the **motive was political** and the aim was to end the
tyranny. Use unknown: Lewis treats the true motive as unrecoverable, noting "there
must have been different versions from the beginning."

## Consequence Analysis

produced: a **markedly harsher tyranny** — Hippias, more fearful (and, says Aristotle,
shaken by Aristogeiton's revelations under torture), turned to executions, exiles, new
fortifications (Munychia), and the Lampsacene marriage-alliance that looked to Darius. The
deed thus **hastened** rather than ended the regime.
enabled: the later **cult of the Tyrannicides** — the statues by Antenor, the
**"Harmodius song"** proclaiming that they had "made Athens *isonomos*" — which retrojected
the founding of [[isonomia|political equality]] onto 514. Thucydides was "enraged" by this
claim; he and the sober tradition stress that it was the **Spartans**, not the assassins,
who actually freed Athens (510).

## Historiography

A classic crux of source-criticism. **Thucydides** (I.20, VI.53–59) deploys the episode
to demonstrate the carelessness of popular tradition; **Herodotus**, **Aristotle's Ath.
Pol.**, and **Plato's Hipparchus** preserve incompatible versions. Lewis judges that "to
assess the variants is hardly profitable," the distortions being amplified by the later
patriotic cult and by a law forbidding insults to the Tyrannicides' memory.
