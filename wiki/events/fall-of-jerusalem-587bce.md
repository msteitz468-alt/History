# Fall of Jerusalem and the Babylonian Exile (587 BCE)

The **fall of Jerusalem** to [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar II]] in **587/586 BCE**
ended the [[kingdom-of-judah|kingdom of Judah]], destroyed **Solomon's Temple**, and
began the **Babylonian Exile** — one of the most consequential events in the history
of Israelite religion and of the Western religious tradition. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|
CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] treats it from the Babylonian side (Ch. 27, Wiseman) and the
Judaean side (Ch. 30, Mitchell, ingested in Cycle 5); this page anchors the event.

## Narrative

Judah had been a Babylonian vassal since [[battle-of-carchemish-605bce|Carchemish
(605)]], but oscillated toward Egypt. After **Jehoiakim's** revolt, Nebuchadnezzar
captured Jerusalem on **15/16 March 597**, deporting King **Jehoiachin**, the elite,
and the temple vessels, and installing **Zedekiah** (Mattaniah) as a sworn vassal.
When **Zedekiah** in turn became "the focus of opposition to Babylon," summoning the
neighbouring states to Jerusalem and appealing to Pharaoh **Apries**, Nebuchadnezzar
responded.

The Babylonians began **large-scale siege operations** on **10 Tebet of Zedekiah's
ninth year** (II Kings 25:1; Jer. 39:1), throwing up circumvallation walls. An
Egyptian relief force caused only "a temporary diversion." The siege signals between
**Lachish and Azekah** are echoed in the **Lachish ostraca**. The Babylonians breached
the northern wall on **9 Tammuz** and **sacked the city and temple the following
month (25 August 587** by Nisan-year reckoning; **586** by the Tishri-year). Wiseman
notes "no sure evidence that the final outcome resulted from starvation" — the month's
interval suggests protracted negotiations. **Zedekiah** fled through the Royal Gardens,
was caught, and at **Riblah Nebuchadnezzar killed his sons before his eyes, then
blinded him**.

Archaeology confirms the destruction: the **City of David (stratum 10)** shows total
destruction and fierce conflagration; **Lachish, Ramat Rahel, Gezer, Arad, En-gedi**
and other towns were destroyed, though the territory of **Benjamin** to the north was
largely spared. Fewer captives were taken than in 597; the poorest were left to work
the royal estates. **Gedaliah** was made Babylonian governor at **Mizpah**, but his
**assassination** triggered a final deportation: in **582** Nabuzaradan carried off a
further **745 Judaeans** (Jer. 52:30), ending Judah's independence.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: **Zedekiah's revolt** and his appeal to Egypt, in breach of his loyalty
oath to [[nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]]; the prophet **Jeremiah's**
pro-Babylonian counsel was overruled.
contributed_to: the structural impossibility of Judah's position — a small state
"destined to be dominated by foreign powers, with but a few years' respite, for the
next fourteen centuries" (Wiseman), caught between Babylon and Egypt since
[[battle-of-carchemish-605bce|Carchemish]].

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **[[babylonian-exile|Babylonian Exile]]** — the deportation of the Judaean
elite to Babylonia (settled, per Dandamaev, "in specially designated regions near Nippur,"
not enslaved, retaining their elders and ethnic identity — see [[neo-babylonian-civilization]]);
the destruction of the **First Temple**; the end of the Davidic monarchy. In Palestine
Nebuchadnezzar left **Gedaliah** as governor at Mizpah; his **assassination** triggered a
final deportation (582) and the flight of survivors, with Jeremiah, to Egypt (Ch. 31).
enabled: the transformation of Israelite religion in exile — the editing of the
**Deuteronomistic History**, the exilic prophecy (Ezekiel, Second Isaiah), and a
faith reoriented around **text, law, and covenant** rather than temple and land. The
exiles' descendants **returned** under [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus's]] decree after
[[fall-of-babylon-539bce|539]] — the crucial difference from the [[fall-of-samaria-722bce|
northern deportation of 722]], whose exiles did not return.
This is the paradigmatic instance of the [[assyrian-mass-deportation|Assyrian
deportation method]] inherited and applied by the Neo-Babylonians.

## Historiography

The event is dated by the convergence of the **Babylonian Chronicle** (silent on the
587 siege itself, which it post-dates), dated Babylonian texts, the **Hebrew Bible**
(II Kings, Jeremiah, the rations tablets naming "Ya'ukin king of Judah"), the
**Lachish ostraca**, and **destruction-layer archaeology**. The principal unresolved
question is the **year — 587 vs. 586** — turning on whether a Nisan or Tishri regnal
calendar is used; Wiseman follows 587, Malamat and others 586. The fuller Judaean
narrative, the religious meaning of the Exile, and the prophetic literature are
treated in Cycle 5 (Chs 29–31, Mitchell).
