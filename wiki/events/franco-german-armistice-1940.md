# Franco-German Armistice, 22 June 1940

The armistice signed at Rethondes near Compiègne — in the same railway
carriage as November 1918, effective 25 June — ending French belligerence and
creating the legal framework of [[vichy-france]].

## Narrative

Pétain, premier from 17 June, announced within hours that "it is necessary to
stop the fighting"; the terms divided France into occupied and unoccupied
zones, kept ~2 million POWs in Germany, imposed occupation costs (set in
August at 400 million francs/day — ultimately ~58% of state income 1940–44),
required the fleet's demobilization (not surrender), and — Article 3 — bound
French officials to "collaborate faithfully" with the occupier: the textual
origin of "collaboration" (see [[collaboration-and-collaborationism]]).
Article 19, obliging France to surrender German refugees on demand, abridged
the right of asylum with consequences that ran to [[the-holocaust]]. France
was "the only defeated nation allowed to treat state-to-state for an
armistice" — a leniency Hitler chose deliberately, telling Mussolini on
17 June that he feared the French government would otherwise flee to London
and fight on.

## Causal analysis

`caused_by:` [[fall-of-france-1940]]. But Paxton's Prologue insists the
armistice was a **choice among real alternatives**, never formally voted on:
the Breton redoubt collapsed, but the North African option was "far more
promising" — Noguès's forces "burning" to fight, the fleet intact at
Mers-el-Kébir, the *Massilia* actually sailing with 30 parliamentarians on
21 June. "There were valid alternatives to an armistice, therefore, and they
were Hitler's nightmare." In retrospect Paxton judges fighting on "almost
certainly the correct policy" — psychologically unthinkable in 1940 (fear of
revolution, WWI trauma, the demographic "hollow years"). `produced:`
[[vichy-france]] and, by giving the new regime shelter and urgency,
`enabled:` the [[national-revolution-vichy|National Revolution]] and the
armistice-trap logic that drew Vichy into German repression (hostages, labor,
deportations). `followed_by:` Mers-el-Kébir (3 July 1940, over 1,200 French
sailors killed by the Royal Navy), which poisoned [[de-gaulle-charles]]'s
early recruitment.

## Historiography

The postwar defense treated the armistice as inevitable and providential; the
expected quick peace (officer pensions were arranged for only three months)
never came, and every Vichy policy flowed from that miscalculation. Paxton's
reframing — "This is not another book about why France fell. It is about what
Frenchmen decided to do next" — made the armistice decision, rather than the
military collapse, the founding question of Vichy studies.
