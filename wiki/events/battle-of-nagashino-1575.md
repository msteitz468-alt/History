## Narrative

### Background: The Siege of Nagashino

In the fifth month of 1575 Takeda Katsuyori, son of [[takeda-shingen]], besieged Nagashino Castle in Mikawa with an army of approximately thirteen thousand. The castle sat at the confluence of the Takigawa and Onogawa rivers and the upper Toyokawa, guarding the gateway from Kai and Shinano into the western provinces. Its garrison of about five hundred was commanded by Okudaira Sadamasa, twenty-four years old, and supported by two Matsudaira relatives.

The siege had been prompted by the treachery of Oga Yashiro, a trusted Ieyasu financial official who had plotted to open Okazaki to Katsuyori. When the plot was discovered — Oga's wife had known of it and tried to dissuade him — Nobuyasu informed his father; Oga was subjected to prolonged public torture and died. Katsuyori, already marching when he learned the plot had been exposed, felt he could not retreat without disgrace and proceeded to invest Nagashino instead.

The Takeda forces attempted to mine the castle, launched raft assaults on the rivers, and brought up towers to overlop the ramparts — all repelled at considerable cost. After a general assault on the fourteenth failed with heavy losses, Katsuyori settled in for a blockade, stretching cables across the rivers with bells to detect swimmers. The garrison had food for only four or five days.

### Torii Suneemon

The messenger Torii Suneemon, thirty-four years old, volunteered to swim out through the cables and carry word to Ieyasu. On the night of the fourteenth he cut the cables with a dagger and swam downstream, lit a beacon at dawn to signal his safe passage, and reached Okazaki to report. He told Ieyasu the garrison had ammunition and high spirits but only two or three days of food. He was introduced to Nobunaga, who promised immediate departure.

Suneemon returned to signal the garrison that relief was coming, but the besiegers had spread sand on the riverbanks and rigged alarm bells; he was caught on the sixteenth. Brought before Katsuyori, he agreed to tell the garrison there was no hope of relief and they should surrender. Taken close to the castle, he shouted instead that Ieyasu and Nobunaga were on their way and relief was three days off. He was crucified. "Among the records of the Japanese samurai his fame stands high" (Sadler). One Takeda retainer was so struck by his example that he had a picture of Suneemon on the cross painted as a banner.

### Strategic Preliminaries

Nobunaga left Gifu on the thirteenth and was at Okazaki the next day. His councils debated; one argued that defeat would only strengthen Tokugawa-Takeda alignment. Ieyasu may have implied this possibility directly to Nobunaga. The relief army totaled roughly 38,000 (Nobunaga's 30,000 and Ieyasu's 8,000), facing Katsuyori's approximately 15,000.

Sakai Tadatsugu approached Nobunaga publicly proposing a flanking attack on the besieging forces — Nobunaga berated him and dismissed him — then privately approved the plan and gave him five hundred matchlockmen as reinforcement. Sakai set out with about three thousand men total, worked around in a heavy rainstorm, divided into three detachments, and struck the Takeda siege works at Nakayama and Tobigasu simultaneously, causing a panic and killing their commander, Takeda Nobuzane.

Katsuyori's council was divided. His veteran generals — Baba Nobuharu, Yamagata Masakage, Naito Masatoyo, the Sanada brothers — advised withdrawal. Atobe Oinosuke argued that twenty-seven generations of Takeda chiefs had never turned their backs on a foe. Katsuyori chose to fight. Baba offered the compromise of assaulting the castle first at limited cost, arguing Nobunaga had only five hundred matchlockmen; if they could take the castle they could hold it. "When did you hear of Nobunaga retreating when he had once started out to fight?" was the response (Sadler), and that settled it.

### The Battle

Nobunaga had prepared carefully. He selected three thousand specially trained matchlockmen from his entire force of ten thousand and placed them under Sasa Narimasa, Maeda Toshiie, and three other experienced captains. He issued strict orders: hold fire until the enemy was close to the stockades, then fire in alternate volleys of a thousand men. He had also equipped his troops with stakes and rope for field fortifications.

The battle took place on the rough, uneven ground of Shidara-ga-hara, from the foothills of Mount Gambo to the Toyokawa River. Nobunaga placed his force behind a stockade facing a small rapid stream, with gaps at every forty or sixty yards for counterattack. On the left he placed Sakuma Nobumori outside the stockade to draw the enemy forward; on the right Okubo Tadayo with three hundred matchlockmen was positioned in front for a flanking attack.

At five o'clock on the morning of the twenty-first day Katsuyori drew up right, left, and center of three thousand each, with three thousand in reserve. His remaining forces continued the castle investment.

The Takeda right, under Yamagata and others, attacked Okubo's Tokugawa men outside the stockade; steep riverbanks and heavy fire prevented advance, and the two flanks locked together in indecisive hand-to-hand fighting. The Takeda center attacked but was driven back everywhere by fire from behind the stockade. On the Oda left wing Sakuma's feigned retreat drew the Takeda right under Baba onto a small eminence; they then launched an assault on the stockade, were repulsed, and were then taken in flank and rear by Shibata Katsuie and Hashiba Hideyoshi, losing heavily and killing many of their leaders.

Katsuyori threw in his reserves for an all-out assault; it made no headway. When Sasa Narimasa signaled the crisis, Nobunaga ordered his army forward through the stockade gaps, while the Tokugawa men swung around the flanks. The Takeda broke and fled. By about three o'clock in the afternoon the battle was over. Of fifteen thousand Takeda, only about three thousand escaped; the allies took approximately ten thousand heads. All the major veteran commanders — Baba, Yamagata, Naito, the Sanada brothers — were killed. The Oda-Tokugawa forces themselves lost six thousand.

Okudaira Sadamasa and his garrison then sallied from the castle against the besieging forces, who were simultaneously attacked from the other side by Sakai's detachment; those not killed fled.

### Aftermath

After the battle Ieyasu rewarded Okudaira Sadamasa generously, giving him considerable estates, right of audience, the Yoshimitsu sword that Nobunaga had given Ieyasu after Anegawa, and (at Nobunaga's request) his own eldest daughter in marriage. Nobunaga gave Sadamasa a syllable of his own name, making him Nobumasa.

Nobunaga also requested Ieyasu to kill his uncle Mizuno Nobumoto of Kariya, who was alleged to have sold rice to a besieged Takeda castle — Sadler notes this may have been a slander by Sakuma. Ieyasu complied.

## Causal Analysis

Immediately: Katsuyori's refusal to heed his veteran generals and withdraw in the face of overwhelming odds. The underlying cause was the gap between his inheritance of Shingen's fearlessness and his failure to inherit Shingen's shrewdness and patience. Sadler's structural comment: "Katsuyori lacked those qualities that were most conspicuous in Ieyasu, shrewdness and adaptability and self-control or patience... only his fearlessness, which unqualified did him no good."

Technically: Nobunaga's tactical innovation of alternating volleys from behind a stockade neutralized the Takeda cavalry charge, the arm in which they were most formidable. "It was the machine gun and wire entanglement of those days" (Sadler, quoting Lieutenant-General Oshigami, 1913).

## Consequence Analysis

The battle effectively broke Takeda military power. The deaths of their veteran commanders — men who had served under Shingen — left Katsuyori without the counsel and unit cohesion that had made the clan formidable. The final collapse came in 1582 when Nobunaga, Ieyasu, and Hojo coordinated against Katsuyori simultaneously; his retainers and castle-commanders surrendered or defected so rapidly that he was left with perhaps forty men, finally dying by seppuku at a place called Tago along with his young wife, his son, and a handful of loyal followers.

Ieyasu's contrast with Nobunaga at the inspection of Katsuyori's head is historically notable: where Nobunaga crowed, Ieyasu rose from his camp-stool, had the head placed on a ceremonial stand, addressed it respectfully, attributed the disaster to "youthful impetuosity in not heeding the advice of your veteran councilors," and ordered proper burial with an endowed shrine. "The Oda retainers contrasted Ieyasu's delicacy with their master's boorishness, and but eighty days after this Nobunaga himself fell in the tragedy of the Honnōji" (Sadler).

## Historiography

Nagashino was long celebrated in Japanese military history as the battle that demonstrated the decisive power of firearm volley tactics, replacing cavalry charges as the decisive arm. Sadler (1937) accepts this interpretation fully, citing both the chroniclers and a 1913 Japanese military analysis. More recent scholarship (post-Sadler) has complicated this narrative, arguing that the tactical discontinuity has been overstated and that the stockade-and-volley combination was not as novel or as complete a replacement for traditional fighting as the legend suggests. The Tokugawa Art Museum's battle screen (noted by Sadler) showing horses falling under fire was influential in establishing the iconic image of the battle. The collection's coverage is limited to Sadler; specialist battle-history scholarship is not represented.
