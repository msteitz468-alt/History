# Guatemala Coup of 1954 (Operation PBSUCCESS)

## Narrative
The CIA's Operation PBSUCCESS (approved by Eisenhower in December 1953, $3 million budget, superseding the cancelled 1952 Operation PBFortune) overthrew elected Guatemalan President [[arbenz-jacobo|Jacobo Árbenz]] between May and June 1954. The operation unfolded in three phases: (1) March–June psychological warfare and diplomacy, including the pirate radio station "La Voz de Liberación" run by David Atlee Phillips, leaflet drops, a Catholic pastoral letter, and a naval blockade from May 24; (2) June 18–20, Colonel Carlos Castillo Armas led a roughly 480-man "invasion" force that stalled at the border — the CIA's own official history later called it a "tragicomedy"; (3) June 22–30, CIA-supplied P-47 fighters (funded via businessman William Pawley withdrawing $150,000 in cash) bombed rail lines and a British freighter, demoralizing Guatemalan army officers. Árbenz resigned June 27, 1954 and took refuge in the Mexican embassy. US Ambassador John Peurifoy then pressured rival factions in San Salvador talks to install Castillo Armas as president in July 1954.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` Árbenz's 1952 agrarian reform law, which expropriated roughly 400,000 uncultivated acres from United Fruit Company (part of 1.4 million acres redistributed to about 100,000 campesino families by 1954), compensated at the company's own undervalued declared tax value.
- `contributed_to:` United Fruit Company's lobbying of the Eisenhower administration through personal ties — UN Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge (a family investor), State Department official John Cabot (brother of a former United Fruit CEO), and the Dulles brothers (both former United Fruit attorneys at Sullivan & Cromwell).
- `enabled:` E. Howard Hunt's initiation of the anti-Árbenz push as CIA Mexico City station chief, and the propaganda groundwork laid by the pirate radio and leaflet campaign.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` Castillo Armas's installation as president; Árbenz spent 72 days in the Mexican embassy, was forced to strip to his underpants at the airport by the Guatemalan military as a humiliation, and died years later in a Mexico City bathtub, "possibly by suicide" (McCoy), after fifteen years of CIA surveillance and denial of the right to speak, work, or publish.
- `produced:` United Fruit Company later faced a US Department of Justice antitrust suit (1954+) forcing the sale of its Guatemalan railroads and lands.
- `contributed_to:` (per McCoy's interpretive thesis, not settled consensus — see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]) Guatemala's 1960–1996 civil war, which killed roughly 200,000 people (McCoy's figure). McCoy presents this as a downstream consequence rather than a directly caused outcome of the coup alone.
- `followed_by:` Castillo Armas was murdered by his own bodyguard in July 1957.

## Actors
- **[[arbenz-jacobo|Jacobo Árbenz]]** — reformist president, overthrown.
- **[[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]]** — planned and executed PBSUCCESS; E. Howard Hunt ran the psychological-warfare campaign he himself called "a terror campaign," and later led the Nixon administration's Watergate break-in team (1972), describing himself at sentencing as "alone, nearly friendless, ridiculed, disgraced, destroyed as a man."
- United Fruit Company — lobbied for the coup and controlled roughly 80% of US banana imports.

## Historiography
McCoy's account draws on the CIA's own official history and the memoirs of Phillips and Hunt. He frames Guatemala (alongside [[iran-coup-1953]]) as establishing a "sound model for future operations" in the CIA's own words — a precedent McCoy argues was later applied, disastrously, to the very different conditions of Cuba (see [[bay-of-pigs-1961]]). The claim that the coup was a primary or necessary cause of the later civil war (rather than one contributing factor among domestic political and social conditions) is McCoy's interpretive thesis; see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]. Source: Alfred W. McCoy, *Cold War on Five Continents* (2024).
