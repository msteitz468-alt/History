# The Battle of Buxar (1764)

The **Battle of Buxar** (22 October 1764) was the decisive engagement that confirmed the
[[english-east-india-company|English East India Company]] as the master of eastern India. Where
[[battle-of-plassey-1757|Plassey (1757)]] had made the Company a kingmaker in Bengal through
intrigue, Buxar was a **straight military victory** over the combined armies of three Indian
powers — and its diplomatic settlement turned the Company from a trading concern into a
**territorial sovereign**. From the [[cambridge-history-of-india-v5|Cambridge History of India,
Vol. V]].

## Narrative

After Plassey the Company installed and deposed nawabs of Bengal at will. Its puppet **Mir Kasim**
proved unexpectedly able and tried to restore the nawabate's independence — building a modern army,
moving his capital to Monghyr, and (fatally) abolishing the **internal customs duties** the
Company's servants had been evading through the misuse of the *dastak* (duty-free trade pass), thus
levelling the field between British and Indian merchants. The quarrel became war. Defeated and
expelled, Mir Kasim allied with **Shuja-ud-Daula**, the Nawab of **Awadh (Oudh)**, and the titular
Mughal emperor **Shah Alam II**. Their combined host met the Company army under **Major Hector
Munro** at **Buxar** on the Ganges and was decisively beaten.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the [[battle-of-plassey-1757|post-Plassey]] Company domination of Bengal and the
  plunder of its revenues by Company servants.
- `contributed_to:` Mir Kasim's effort to reassert the nawabate's sovereignty and end the
  **dastak** abuse — which made conflict with the Company unavoidable.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Treaty of Allahabad (1765)**, by which Shah Alam II granted the Company the
  **diwani** — the right to collect the revenue and administer the civil finances — of **Bengal,
  Bihar, and Orissa**, India's richest provinces, in exchange for an annual tribute.
- `produced:` the Company's transformation into a **territorial power**: it now held the wealth and
  the legal-fiscal authority of a major Indian state behind a fiction of Mughal delegation (the
  "dual government" Clive devised), funding further conquest from Indian revenue.
- `enabled:` the whole subsequent edifice of **[[british-raj|British India]]** — Buxar, more than
  Plassey, is the true foundation of British rule in India.

## Actors

The [[english-east-india-company|East India Company]] (Munro) against the alliance of **Mir
Kasim**, **Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh**, and the **Mughal emperor Shah Alam II** — the defeat of the
emperor himself dramatising how far Mughal authority had collapsed. See [[mughal-empire]].

## Historiography

The [[cambridge-history-of-india-v5|CHI Vol. V]] (1929) tells Buxar as administrative-military
narrative from the British side. Modern scholarship stresses what the imperial account underplays:
the **fiscal predation** the diwani unleashed (the drain of Bengal's revenue, a factor in the
catastrophic **Bengal famine of 1770**), and the way "trade" and "conquest" were inseparable. The
event is the hinge at which the Company's commercial and territorial histories merge.
