# Belitung Shipwreck

The **Belitung** shipwreck (also **Batu Hitam**, “Black Rock”) is a ninth-century vessel discovered in the **Gelasa Strait** between Bangka and Belitung Island, Indonesia, in **1998**. It carried “the richest shipload of ancient Chinese products ever found.” The ship probably sank between **830 and 840 CE**, had been **built in the Persian Gulf**, and was on the normal shipping lane from China to Java when it hit a reef near shore. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] Early Classic maritime discussion.

## Narrative

Construction and repair point to an Indian Ocean sewn-plank tradition operating through the Straits world. The hull had been dismantled in the Straits of Melaka to insert new caulking of **Melaleuca** between planks; planks were lashed with **Hibiscus tiliaceus**—Seasian materials and labour in a Gulf-built hull.

Cargo (high-level inventory from Miksic & Goh):

- tens of thousands of Chinese ceramics;
- gold artifacts of imperial quality;
- silver ingots;
- star anise; lead;
- **more than 50,000** ceramics from the **Changsha** kiln complex—the “first indication of mass production on this scale in the world”;
- Seasian items (Indonesian gold coin, resin, jackfruit-wood box, mortar and pestle) suggesting **Seasian crew** participation.

Loading may have been at **Yangzhou**; many jars are of Guangdong type. Destination theories:

1. **Oman** — would put the wreck hundreds of kilometres off course; Miksic and Goh judge this “highly unlikely.”
2. **Java** in the [[sailendra|Sailendra]] / [[mataram-kingdom|Mataram]] era—Java sent **six embassies** to China in **813–839**; a ship bearing diplomatic gifts in response would have carried objects of immense value.
3. Java then onward to Oman as a multi-leg voyage.

The Phanom Surin wreck in the [[dvaravati|Dvaravati]] coastal zone (Tang jars, possible western Indian/Near Eastern “torpedo jar,” ninth century) **resembles** Belitung and shows the same horizon of western Indian Ocean hulls in Seasian waters.

## Causal Analysis

- **part_of** Early Classic [[indian-ocean-trade|Indian Ocean]]–South China Sea trade linking Tang export production to Seasian and western Asian demand.
- **concurrent_with** Sailendra-era central Java, Srivijayan Straits politics, and Tang commercial expansion.
- **caused_by** (immediate wreck): navigational hazard—reef strike on the China–Java lane—not political destruction.
- **enabled_by** (voyage existing at all): institutionalized long-distance ceramic trade, monsoon sailing, and multi-ethnic crews and repair networks in the Straits.

Do not conflate the wreck event with the causes of the trade system: the trade **preceded** and **produced** the voyage; the reef produced the archaeological closed find.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced**: a closed chronological and cargo assemblage for ~830–840 CE.
- **enabled**: refined understanding of Changsha mass export, imperial-quality gold in commercial channels, and hybrid ship technology (Gulf build + Seasian repair).
- **contributed_to**: arguments that Seasian ports and crews were integral to China–West Asia routes, not mere passive waypoints.
- **analogous_to** / **concurrent_with**: other Early–Middle Classic wrecks (Intan, etc.) that together map cargo profiles—Belitung remains exceptional for Chinese ceramic volume and quality.

For the wiki’s periodization, Belitung sits squarely in the [[seasian-classic-periodization|Early Classic]] (600–900), concurrent with Heling/Shepo–Tang diplomacy and Sailendra monumentalism.

## Actors

- Persian Gulf shipbuilding tradition and Indian Ocean navigators.
- Tang Chinese kiln and metal exporters (Changsha, Guangdong jars, Yangzhou loading hypothesis).
- Seasian crew and Straits repair specialists (Melaleuca caulking, Hibiscus lashings; Seasian personal items aboard).
- Possible Javanese court recipients in the Sailendra/Mataram diplomatic world.
- Modern excavators and conservators (1998 discovery)—the “event” as archaeological revelation as well as ninth-century wreck.

## Historiography

Debates focus on:

1. **Shipbuilding tradition** (Arab/Persian Gulf sewn-plank vs other Indian Ocean types).
2. **Intended destination** (Oman vs Java vs multi-leg).
3. **Diplomatic vs purely commercial** cargo (imperial-quality gold strengthens the gift/embassy reading but does not prove it).
4. Ethics and methods of commercial salvage vs scientific excavation (broader than Miksic & Goh’s historical use of the cargo).

Miksic and Goh use Belitung as a cornerstone of Early Classic maritime archaeology rather than as a standalone museum catalogue. Comparative hull and cargo work with Phanom Surin and later wrecks continues to refine the picture. Source base for this page: Miksic & Goh 2017.
