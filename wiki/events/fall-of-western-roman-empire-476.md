# Fall of the Western Roman Empire

> **Transition page** (CLAUDE.md standing list): the conventional end of antiquity in
> the West, 476 CE.

## Narrative

The **fall of the Western Roman Empire** is the fragmentation, across the fifth century
CE, of Roman political authority in the Latin-speaking West into a patchwork of Germanic
successor kingdoms. Its conventional terminal date is **476 CE**, when the Germanic
commander **Odoacer** deposed the boy-emperor **Romulus Augustulus** and, rather than
appoint a new western emperor, returned the imperial regalia to **[[byzantine-empire|Constantinople]]**
— ruling Italy as a king nominally under the Eastern emperor.

The process is better dated from **376 CE**, when Gothic groups fleeing the
**[[hunnic-migrations|Huns]]** were admitted across the Danube, then rebelled and
destroyed a Roman army at **Adrianople (378 CE)**. Over the next century the Western
government, based at **Ravenna**, lost control of its provinces: the **Visigoths**
sacked Rome (410) and settled in Gaul and Spain; the **Vandals** took North Africa
(429–439) and sacked Rome again (455); the **Franks**, **Burgundians**, and others
carved out kingdoms; Britain was abandoned. By 476 the Western emperor ruled little
beyond Italy, and the office simply lapsed.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[hunnic-migrations]]` — the Hunnic eruption from the steppe displaced
  Germanic peoples en masse into the empire, the proximate trigger.
- `contributed_to:` fiscal and military overstretch; reliance on federated "barbarian"
  troops settled inside the frontiers; the administrative **division** of the empire,
  which left the poorer West without the resources of the East.
- The deeper weighting of causes — internal decay versus external pressure versus
  transformation — is itself the standing **[[fall-of-rome-causes|controversy]]**. CWH IV
  (Pazdernik) frames the period not as catastrophic "fall" but as **transformation**,
  read from a surviving Constantinople.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Germanic successor kingdoms** (Ostrogothic Italy, Visigothic Spain,
  Frankish Gaul) that became medieval Europe.
- `enabled:` the rise of the **Latin Church** and Papacy as the surviving Roman
  institution, and eventually the **[[carolingian-empire|Carolingian]]** revival of a
  Western empire (800 CE).
- The **[[byzantine-empire|Eastern Roman Empire]] survived intact** — so the event is
  the fall of *half* of Rome, not of Rome as such.
- Marks the conventional transition from [[late-antiquity|Late Antiquity]] to the
  [[early-middle-ages|Early Middle Ages]].

## Historiography

The single most debated transition in Western history (see [[fall-of-rome-causes]]).
**Gibbon** made it the archetype of decline and fall; the "**barbarian invasions**"
school stressed external conquest; **Pirenne** argued Roman Mediterranean unity survived
until the Arab conquests broke it; the modern **Late Antiquity / "transformation"**
school (Peter Brown, Walter Goffart) recasts 476 as a gradual, even peaceful,
accommodation in which "barbarians" were absorbed rather than victorious. The very
choice of 476 as a date is a historiographical artifact — contemporaries did not regard
it as the end of Rome.
