# Operation Sea Lion (July–October 1940)

The planned German invasion of Britain, prepared in directive form through the summer of 1940 but never launched. Distinct from — and the intended culmination of — the air campaign at [[battle-of-britain-1940]].

## Narrative

Jodl's memo "The Continuation of the War against England" (30 June 1940) proposed a three-step "siege": air and sea war on shipping, "terror attacks against the centers of population," and then a landing as a "deathblow" (*Todesstoss*) to an already-paralyzed England — not full military conquest. Hitler's Directive No. 16 (16 July 1940) ordered invasion preparation "if necessary"; Directive No. 17 (1 August) made Luftwaffe air superiority the precondition, Hitler reserving to himself the decision on terror bombing as reprisal.

A fundamental Army–Navy split shaped planning throughout: the Army (Brauchitsch) wanted a broad landing front from Ramsgate to Lyme Bay with 260,000 men ashore by day three; the Navy (Raeder) said it lacked the shipping and escort capacity for this and pushed for a narrow front between Dover and Eastbourne, warning in a 29 July memo that a postponement to "May 1941 or thereafter" might be necessary. Halder's exasperation is recorded verbatim to Admiral Schniewind on 7 August: "I might just as well put the troops that have landed straight through a sausage machine!" At the Obersalzberg conference of 31 July 1940, Hitler decided: "An attempt must therefore be made to prepare the operation for September 15, 1940 … The decision as to whether the operation is to take place in September or is to be delayed until May 1941, will be made after the Air Force has made concentrated attacks on southern England for one week." A 3 September OKW directive (signed Keitel) set a sailing date of 20 September and landing on the 21st, with go/no-go authority reserved until 24 hours before zero hour.

Occupation planning was drastic and unique among Nazi conquests: Brauchitsch's 9 September 1940 directive ordered internment and deportation to the Continent of all able-bodied British males aged 17–45; a "Special Search List, G.B." (*Sonderfahndungsliste*) of roughly 2,300 names for immediate Gestapo/SD arrest included Winston Churchill, Bertrand Russell, Virginia Woolf, H.G. Wells, and Sigmund Freud (already dead, listed in error). Six Einsatzkommando were organized under Heydrich in August 1940 for London, Bristol, Birmingham, Liverpool, Manchester, and Edinburgh/Glasgow. Britain's own preparations were correspondingly severe: Churchill later stated that a real invasion attempt "would have been on both sides grim and great. There would have been neither mercy nor quarter," and Britain had reportedly decided to use mustard gas against German beachheads as a last resort.

British intelligence badly misjudged German intentions all summer, expecting the main blow on the east coast — producing the false invasion alert "Cromwell" on 7 September 1940 after the first massive daylight raid on London was misread as an invasion prelude. The German Naval War Diary entry of 17 September 1940 records the Navy's own assessment: "The enemy Air Force is still by no means defeated... The Fuehrer therefore decides to postpone 'Sea Lion' indefinitely." Formal cancellation-as-camouflage followed on 12 October 1940, ordering that "the British must continue to believe that we are preparing an attack on a broad front."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` [[battle-of-britain-1940]]'s failure to secure the air superiority Directive No. 17 required — the Luftwaffe never achieved the "overcome the British Air Force with all means" precondition. `contributed_to:` the operation's own postponement was the Kriegsmarine's crippled state after the [[norwegian-campaign-1940|Norwegian campaign]] (losses of 10 of 20 destroyers, 3 of 8 cruisers), which left too few escorts for even the Navy's narrow-front plan. `caused_by:` the political failure of Hitler's 19 July 1940 "peace offer," rejected within the hour by the BBC.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` no invasion; the Battle of Britain was fought to its air-campaign conclusion instead, becoming — per [[battle-of-britain-1940]] — Germany's first strategic defeat of the war. `enabled:` Hitler's attention to shift toward the East: the Berghof conference deciding the eventual invasion of the USSR (31 July 1940) occurred the same day Hitler set the tentative September Sea Lion date, foreshadowing [[operation-barbarossa-1941]]. `contrasts_with:` the earlier German campaigns' pattern of directive-to-execution speed — Sea Lion is the one major 1940 operation that was planned in exhaustive documentary detail and never executed.

## Actors

[[adolf-hitler]] held final authority throughout and never formally cancelled, only "postponed indefinitely," the operation. [[churchill-winston]]'s government prepared for invasion on the assumption it was real. Raeder and Brauchitsch's institutional rivalry (Navy caution vs. Army ambition) shaped the plan's repeated narrowing.

## Historiography

[SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer argues explicitly against postwar German generals' claims (Rundstedt, Blumentritt) that Sea Lion was mere "bluff" or a "game," citing the "mountainous secret German military files" as proof Hitler seriously intended invasion, and cautioning that Nuremberg-era general testimony should be read skeptically because the generals had "many axes to grind" in discrediting Hitler's military leadership after the fact — Rundstedt's own postwar claim ("I have a feeling that the Fuehrer never really wanted to invade England") is presented by Shirer as a representative example of this self-exculpatory pattern. [EYEWITNESS] Shirer personally toured the Channel coast between Antwerp and Boulogne in mid-August 1940 and, seeing Luftwaffe activity but a striking lack of invasion barges, concluded at the time "the Germans were bluffing" — he later learned the invasion fleet was not actually assembled until 1 September, so his contemporary impression, while directionally right about German capability at that moment, was formed before assembly even began. This tension between his own real-time judgment and the documentary record he later uncovered is itself part of Shirer's point about the difficulty of assessing Nazi intentions from appearances alone. This remains the standard framing: Sea Lion was a genuine, detailed operational plan whose precondition (air superiority) was never met, rather than a diplomatic bluff staged for domestic or Soviet consumption.
