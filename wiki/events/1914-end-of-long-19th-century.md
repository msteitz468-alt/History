# 1914 and the End of the Long 19th Century

> **Transition page** (CLAUDE.md standing list: "1914 and the end of the Long 19th
> Century"). Marks the discontinuity between the [[long-19th-century|Long 19th
> Century]] and the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]].

## Narrative

The outbreak of the First World War in **1914** ended the "long nineteenth century"
(Hobsbawm's 1789–1914) and the **century of relative European great-power peace
between Waterloo (1815) and Sarajevo (1914)**. In [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]]'s
framing, the industrial, organizational, and national capacities accumulated across
the long nineteenth century — the [[fossil-fuel-energy-transition|energy]] and
[[industrialization|industrial]] revolutions, the [[nationalism|national state]], the
arms industries — were turned, after 1914, to destruction on an unprecedented scale.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the alliance system and great-power rivalry; the militarized,
  industrialized state.
- `contributed_to:` [[nationalism]] (especially in the lands of the multinational
  empires); [[imperialism|imperial competition]] among the late-comer powers
  (Germany, Japan, Italy) seeking resources and markets.
- The editors stress that nineteenth-century European wars "never became global in the
  same sense" as the eighteenth-century or twentieth-century ones — 1914 broke that
  exception catastrophically.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the [[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] (1914–1945); the collapse of the
  Habsburg, Ottoman, Romanov, and Hohenzollern empires.
- `enabled:` the **Russian Revolution (1917)** and the rise of
  [[communism-and-fascism|communism and fascism]]; the homogenizing national states in
  whose lands the greatest concentration of twentieth-century [[genocide]] occurred
  (see [[nationalism]], Levene).
- Accelerated the discrediting of Europe's "civilizing mission" and the rise of
  anti-colonial [[decolonization|anti-imperialism]].

## Historiography

The causes of 1914 remain one of history's most contested questions. Fritz Fischer's 1961 thesis
assigning primary responsibility to German expansionist war aims provoked decades of controversy,
countered by arguments emphasizing systemic factors — the alliance system, mobilization
timetables, and a militarized culture shared by all great powers (echoed in Christopher Clark's
*The Sleepwalkers*, which stresses contingency and shared culpability over German
premeditation). Primary sources are abundant but compromised: the diplomatic "colour books" each
power rushed into print in 1914–15 were selectively edited to assign blame elsewhere, and postwar
memoirs by statesmen and generals are heavily self-justifying. National historiographies diverged
sharply along the fault lines of interwar reparations politics and, later, Cold War alignments.
Recent scholarship has shifted from a narrow search for the "guilty party" toward transnational
and structural analysis — treating 1914 as the product of an unstable multipolar system rather
than a single actor's decision. The collection's treatment (via CWH VII and Joll) reflects this
structural turn while still surveying the older war-guilt debates.
