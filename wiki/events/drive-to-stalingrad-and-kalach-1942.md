# Drive to Stalingrad and Kalach Kessel (July–early September 1942)

**Source grounding:** All claims, verbatim quotes, details, and analysis drawn exclusively from Robert M. Citino, *Death of the Wehrmacht: The German Campaigns of 1942* (2007), Chapter 7, "Coming to a Halt: The Caucasus and Stalingrad," sections "The Drive on Stalingrad: Clearing the Don," "To Stalingrad," and "In the City" (up to the start of the September 13 assaults).

## Narrative

Compared to the complex Caucasus operation, the drive on Stalingrad was conceptually simple: a straight shot east by 6th Army (Paulus). It began as a secondary effort, undermanned and undersupplied. Fuel shortages halted Paulus almost immediately. By July 26, forward units had not been refueled for two days. 6th Army sat immobilized for nearly ten days while Soviets strengthened the Don bend and Stalingrad defenses. A Soviet counterattack by 1st and 4th Tank Armies (with air support) created a crisis for the immobilized XIV Panzer Corps; the Germans beat it back only by expending every drop of gas.

Directive 45 (and the August 1 priority reversal) made Stalingrad the new Schwerpunkt. Hoth’s 4th Panzer Army was transferred to Army Group B. With 6th Army advancing from the west inside the Don bend (XIV Panzer north skirting the Don; XXIV Panzer and LI Corps south) and Hoth approaching from south/southwest, a concentric effort of sorts developed—though Hoth remained low priority for fuel.

On August 7, refueled 6th Army resumed. XIV and XXIV Panzer Corps drove into the flanks and rear of the Soviet bridgehead (62nd and 64th Armies, ~40 miles wide by 20 deep). Luftwaffe (Fliegerkorps VIII) destroyed all Don bridges behind them. The two armored wedges met early August 8 southwest of Kalach. Large parts of 62nd and 1st Tank Armies were trapped in a tight Kessel less than four miles wide. Concentric ground attack plus massive air support reduced the pocket over two days despite breakout attempts. "The carnage was horrible."

By August 11 the Kalach pocket was liquidated: 35,000 prisoners, 270 tanks, 560 guns (plus prior Don bend fighting: total 57,000 prisoners and nearly 1,000 tanks). It was the first real encirclement of the 1942 campaign and raised hopes at Hitler’s headquarters.

Paulus then crossed the Don. XIV Panzer Corps crossed at Vertyachiy (August 21) and drove the 35-mile land bridge to the Volga. On August 23, 16th Panzer Division (Hube) reached the northern suburbs of Stalingrad and the Volga bank. It held a precarious narrow corridor; Soviet counterattacks quickly isolated it, requiring heavy Luftwaffe intervention for relief. That same night the Luftwaffe mounted its heaviest strike since Barbarossa—nearly 600 bombers (many flying multiple sorties), using incendiaries. Fires visible 40 miles away; "high enough" civilian deaths.

Hoth, approaching from south along the Ssalsk-Stalingrad railway, was delayed by 57th Army and fuel/ammo shortages. He reached ~30 miles south, then used operational maneuver (wide northwest flanking of a bottleneck at Tinguta, then northeast) to link with 6th Army on September 2. Another massive bombing raid hit Stalingrad on September 3.

A continuous German front now existed directly before Stalingrad. Remnants of 62nd (north/center) and 64th (south) Armies held the city. Zhukov’s counterstroke (September 5: 24th, 1st Guards, 66th Armies) into the northern flank failed due to poor coordination and march-to-attack planning but distracted 6th Army and bought Chuikov (62nd Army) time.

Stalingrad presented a 30-mile-long urbanized strip along the Volga’s right bank—a fortified bridgehead, not a conventional city that could be besieged. Behind it lay the river, massive Soviet artillery, and an industrializing hinterland. The Wehrmacht had "conquered its way into an impasse." City fighting would force Stellungskrieg and Festungskrieg, exactly what the convoluted 1942 plan had aimed to avoid. Nevertheless, on or around September 5 the guns roared, Panzers advanced, and Stukas screamed: the assault on Stalingrad began.

## Causal Analysis

- **Priority flip and logistics**: Initial Caucasus emphasis starved 6th Army of fuel/transport. Later Stalingrad priority still left Hoth third in line. "Hoth’s army was third of three on the priority list for fuel."
- **Soviet flight then buildup**: Soviets avoided early Kessels by flight (as in Blue opening phases) but then concentrated in the Don bend and at Stalingrad. Bridgeheads left on the north flank (Serafimovich, Kletskaya, Kremenskaya) would later prove costly.
- **Operational maneuver successes and limits**: Kalach was classic concentric (two Panzer corps wheeling). Hoth’s flanking of Tinguta showed remaining skill. But arrival staggered; no large outer encirclement of Soviet forces outside the city.
- **Geography of the city**: Long linear urban area on a major river made encirclement or siege impossible. "The Germans could never put it under siege."
- `contributed_to:` decision to enter the city despite known costs, per German tradition ("the only decision consonant with its history and traditions, dating back to Frederick the Great, Blücher, and Moltke").

Participants agreed Stalingrad could likely have been taken in late July with far less cost had it been prioritized earlier.

## Consequence Analysis

- Kalach provided a morale boost and demonstrated remaining Kesselschlacht capability, but numbers were far below 1941 totals.
- Arrival on the Volga (Hube’s dash) was impressive but created an exposed salient/corridor requiring immediate defense.
- Massive air raids announced presence but did not break defender morale (Chuikov’s 62nd Army and civilians held).
- Entry into urban fighting marked the shift from Bewegungskrieg to Rattenkrieg: "the meter replaced the kilometer as a measure of distance, and the city plan replaced the General Staff’s map" (Doerr).
- `produced:` conditions for the prolonged city battle; `part_of:` the broader 1942 "vast bulge" and twin halts (with Caucasus and El Alamein); `enabled:` eventual Soviet counteroffensive setup on the flanks (not detailed in this range).

## Actors

- **Paulus**: Hamstrung by fuel early; executed the Don clearing and Volga dash methodically. Recognized the urban impasse but followed tradition into the city.
- **Hube**: Led the dramatic 16th Panzer dash to the Volga; held precarious pocket until relieved.
- **Hoth**: Delayed but used maneuver to link up.
- **Chuikov**: Held the narrowing Volga bank strip with minimal resources; ferries/barges as lifeline.

## Historiography

Citino stresses irony: Stalingrad was originally subsidiary ("no more than a name on the map" per Kleist post-war). The campaign’s design aimed to avoid city fighting, yet geography and the failure to trap forces outside forced it. The Kalach success is noted but contextualized as too late and too small to be decisive. Emphasis on the systemic problem: even successful concentric maneuvers left the Wehrmacht "sitting out on the end of a long and vulnerable limb" with no good options. The transition to meter-by-meter infantry fighting ("Rattenkrieg") is presented as the antithesis of the operational art the Wehrmacht was trained and equipped for.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Hitler's Directive No. 45 (23 July 1942) split the summer offensive into simultaneous drives on Stalingrad and the Caucasus oil fields against Halder's explicit warning the Army lacked strength for both; Halder's diary that evening: "The continual underestimation of enemy possibilities takes on grotesque forms and is becoming dangerous... Pathological reaction to momentary impressions and a complete lack of capacity to assess the situation." Hitler had earlier told Paulus, before the offensive: "If I do not get the oil of Maikop and Grozny, then I must end this war." The 350-mile Don flank between Stalingrad and Voronezh was held by weak satellite armies (Hungarian Second, Italian Eighth, Rumanian Third and Fourth) deliberately kept separate because of Rumanian-Hungarian hostility; the Rumanian Third held 105 miles with only 69 battalions, a risk Hitler dismissed because he believed the Russians "finished." Kleist told Liddell Hart postwar that the Fourth Panzer Army "could have taken Stalingrad without a fight at the end of July, but was diverted south to help me in crossing the Don. I did not need its aid... When it turned north again a fortnight later the Russians had gathered just sufficient forces at Stalingrad to check it."

Halder was dismissed on 24 September 1942 after warning Hitler — based on accurate Army intelligence — that Stalin could still muster 1–1.25 million fresh troops north of Stalingrad, and that Soviet tank output was roughly 1,200/month; Hitler "flew at the man who was reading with clenched fists and foam in the corners of his mouth," telling him at their farewell: "You and I have been suffering from nerves. Half of my nervous exhaustion is due to you... We need National Socialist ardor now, not professional ability." Halder's diary ends on this date — a documented gap in the primary-source record Shirer relies on for the rest of the campaign. See [[halder-franz]].
