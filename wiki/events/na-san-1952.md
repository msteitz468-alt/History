# Battle of Na San (November–December 1952)

The Battle of Na San was a significant French defensive success in late 1952. It tested (and appeared to validate) the French “base aéro-terrestre” or hedgehog concept of fortified positions supplied and supported by airpower against Viet Minh main-force attacks.

## Narrative

After the RC4 disaster and other setbacks, the French established a strong air-ground base at Na San in the northwest. Giap, confident after recent gains and with improved Chinese support, launched a series of assaults beginning 23 November 1952. French artillery, air strikes, and prepared defenses inflicted very heavy casualties. The Viet Minh eventually broke off the attacks.

French forces counted around 1,000 enemy bodies; later estimates suggested Viet Minh losses could have reached 6,000 total (possibly half a division). The French declared the hedgehog tactic vindicated.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Giap’s desire to destroy a major French position and demonstrate that the Viet Minh could take fortified bases; French decision to stand and fight on ground of their choosing with air superiority.

**contributed_to:** French air resupply and fire support; prepared positions and strong artillery; Viet Minh difficulties sustaining attacks over distance without equivalent firepower.

## Consequence Analysis

Na San gave the French a much-needed morale boost and appeared to offer a model for holding key points without overextending on vulnerable roads. The apparent success of the concept influenced planning for Dien Bien Phu the following year — with ultimately very different results.

For Giap it was an expensive lesson in the limits of massed infantry attacks against well-supported defensive positions.

## Actors

- French: Raoul Salan (overall); local commanders at the base.
- Viet Minh: Vo Nguyen Giap (command).

## Historiography

Logevall notes the French jubilation and the subsequent irony when the same basic concept failed spectacularly at Dien Bien Phu. Vietnamese accounts treat Na San as a costly but educational defeat that informed later siege tactics (artillery, antiaircraft, engineering, sustained logistics).
