# Battle of Sempach (9 July 1386)

## Narrative

The battle where the [[swiss-confederation|Swiss Confederation]] destroyed a Habsburg army and
killed a duke. **Leopold III**, seeking a decisive battle, feinted at the small town of Sempach to
draw the Swiss into the open. His knights **dismounted** to storm the high ground against the Swiss
advance guard; the Swiss **main body then struck them in the flank** — "with a great shout and
throwing a hail of stones" — and overran the encumbered foot-knights. Leopold III "and a large
number of nobles and knights were slain." [[delbruck-medieval-warfare-vol-3-1982|Delbrück]] makes
Sempach his showcase for **source-criticism**, demonstrating that the legend of **Arnold von
Winkelried**'s self-sacrifice is a sixteenth-century literary accretion that "attributes their own
[Swiss] tactics to the enemy."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the Confederation's offensive and Leopold's plan to force battle. The Swiss win was
produced by the **fix-and-flank** method and by the knights' fatal **dismounting**, which stripped
them of mobility.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the decapitation of the regional Habsburg nobility (the duke himself dead) and the
consolidation of Swiss independence; `produced:` the durable Winkelried legend, which Delbrück
dismantles.

## Actors

[[swiss-confederation|Swiss Confederation]] · Duke Leopold III of Habsburg.

## Historiography

As at Morgarten, the earliest detailed narratives are Swiss chronicles written well after the battle, and the most famous episode associated with it — Arnold von Winkelried gathering enemy spears into his own body to break the Habsburg line — is, per Delbrück's source-critical analysis, a sixteenth-century literary invention that retrojects the Swiss army's actual flanking tactics onto a fictionalized individual sacrifice. This makes Sempach a textbook case in Delbrück's *Sachkritik* method: comparing the chronicled narrative against the physical and tactical plausibility of the terrain and troop dispositions to strip away later accretion. Even setting the Winkelried legend aside, casualty figures for the Habsburg nobility (including Duke Leopold III himself) and the size of the opposing forces are unevenly attested across the surviving chronicle tradition. The battle's use as a foundational moment of Swiss identity means, as with Morgarten, that later commemorative and nationalist retelling must be distinguished carefully from the more modest documentary record.
