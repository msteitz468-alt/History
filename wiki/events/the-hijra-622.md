## Narrative

In 622 CE, Muhammad ibn 'Abd-Allah, a merchant and prophet of Mecca, led his followers in a migration from Mecca to the agricultural oasis of Yathrib, approximately two hundred miles to the north. This migration — the *Hijra* (Arabic: "emigration" or "departure") — was not a flight but a calculated political relocation. It transformed Islam from a persecuted religious movement within Meccan society into the governing authority of a new community.

The immediate occasion was a crisis of protection. Muhammad's position in Mecca had depended on the *wala'* (tribal patronage) of his clan, the Banu Hashim, whose head, his uncle Abu Talib, had shielded him even without accepting Islam. Abu Talib's death removed this protection. Without tribal shelter in Mecca, Muhammad and his followers "would presumably have been" vulnerable to lethal violence.

The opportunity was an invitation. Delegations from Yathrib — an oasis with an ethnically mixed population of Arab pagans and Jewish clans — had contacted Muhammad during the annual pilgrimage season. The oasis "had been torn by bitter strife among its leading clans" for years; they sought an outside figure to arbitrate and command. In 621, a small group "pledged themselves to obey Muhammad in any wholesome command"; in 622 a larger group of "six times as many" reinforced the pledge, "promising Muhammad and his Meccan followers protection if they came to Medina."

In that same year, Muhammad "sent most of his followers, rather more than seventy, to move one by one to Medina, and finally went himself." The measured, individual-by-individual migration was deliberate: a sudden mass departure would have been stopped by the Quraysh.

## The Political Logic of the Move

Hodgson stresses that the Hijra was not merely a rescue operation: "To Muhammad, the move to Medina was not merely an escape from an untenable position." It was an opportunity to realize what had been impossible in Mecca — the construction of a genuinely Muslim social order. In Mecca, Muhammad had been a preacher within an existing and hostile social structure. In Medina he could become a founder.

The Medinan welcome itself had a political logic independent of religious conviction. The oasis needed what Muhammad could offer: "a solution to pressing problems. Medina had been torn by bitter strife among its leading clans." An outside arbiter from a distinguished family, claiming divine authority, could restructure the conflict without being absorbed by either faction.

Yathrib was also religiously prepared. "Medina had come to recognize and no doubt to respect monotheistic religion" through its substantial Jewish population, making the idea of a prophet neither alien nor incomprehensible, even if the Jews ultimately rejected Muhammad's specific claims.

## The Constitution of Medina

Upon arriving in Medina, Muhammad formalized the community's structure in a document Hodgson calls the "'constitution of Medina'" — a set of agreements establishing "the mutual obligations of the adhering clans." This document:

- Established Muhammad as commander of the Muslims (both Meccan emigrants and Medinan converts)
- Made Muhammad the acknowledged arbiter for "all the social groups at Medina"
- Defined the *umma* (community) as superseding tribal identity as the primary frame of solidarity
- Included both Muslims and some non-Muslim groups in a common framework of mutual obligation

The emigrants from Mecca were called **Muhajirun** ("emigrants"); the Medinan converts were called **Ansar** ("helpers"). This distinction structured social hierarchy and access to resources in the early Muslim polity: the Muhajirun "lacked resources when they arrived; they became the guests of the An*sar*, who received them as brothers." The distinction persisted through the first civil war (see [[first-fitna-656]]).

## Consequences and Historical Significance

The Hijra produced consequences far exceeding its immediate circumstances:

**Foundation of the Islamic calendar.** The year of the Hijra became Year 1 of the Muslim era (AH 1 = *anno Hegirae*). This calendrical choice encoded the political priority of the Medinan community over the Meccan revelation: not the year of Muhammad's first revelation (c. 610) but the year the community became a polity.

**Transformation of Islam's social form.** In Mecca, Islam had been a religious reform movement operating within tribal society. In Medina it became a polity: "by now clearly a state, like the states in the nations round about Arabia, with an increasingly authoritative government, which could no longer be ignored with impunity." Muhammad "sent out envoys, who taught the Qur'an and the principles of Islam, collected the *zakat*, and presumably arbitrated disputes."

**The Quran's character changed.** The Medinan surahs of the [[quran]] are noticeably different from the Meccan ones — more concerned with law, social regulation, and military organization, and more explicitly responsive to community events. The [[quran]] became inseparable from the community experience it accompanied.

**Precedent for the *umma*.** The Hijra established that the Muslim community was not defined by territory, ethnicity, or tribe but by adherence to Islam and to Muhammad's authority. This made the community potentially universal — "the new way is for everyone, not just" the Medinan Arabs. The structural innovation of the *umma* cutting across tribal lines would define Islamic political theory for centuries.

**Raiding and economic independence.** Almost immediately after settling, Muhammad "began sending the Muhajirun out to raid" — a strategic necessity. The emigrants had broken their economic ties with Mecca; they had no productive base in Medina; raiding provided income and tested group cohesion. It also escalated the conflict with Mecca toward open warfare. The raid at **Nakhla** (624, breaking a sacred month truce) and the **Battle of Badr** (624) flowed directly from the structural pressures created by the Hijra.

## Causal Analysis

The Hijra was caused proximately by the loss of tribal protection in Mecca and the opportunity presented by the Medinan invitation. But Hodgson situates these within deeper structural causes:

- **The Quraysh's structural incompatibility with Islam.** Mecca's political and economic order depended on religious neutrality among Arabia's tribes. Muhammad's monotheistic exclusivism threatened this directly. The Quraysh's persecution was not mere bigotry but a defense of a working political-religious system.
- **The structural need for a base.** Muhammad's vision required "a guided community" — not merely individual converts but an organized social body capable of embodying Islamic norms. Mecca offered no space for this; Medina did.
- **The weakness of the Medinan status quo.** The inter-tribal warfare at Yathrib created demand for exactly what Muhammad offered. Structural dysfunction in the host community was as important as the Muslim community's need to escape Mecca.

The Hijra should be distinguished from a mere *consequence* of Meccan persecution: it was a strategic choice that could have been made differently. Muhammad had earlier sent some followers to Abyssinia (c. 615), a move that "may have been a full alternative" to the Medinan migration. That the Medinan option was chosen, and not the Abyssinian, shaped the entire subsequent trajectory of Islamic history.

## Historiography

**Source problems.** The Hijra is documented by later Muslim biographical traditions (*sira*) compiled a century or more after the event, by Quranic allusions (which are oblique), and by the "constitution of Medina" — a document whose authenticity is generally accepted but whose precise original form is disputed. The chronology of events in the first years at Medina is contested in detail.

**Scholarly debates.** Major disputes include: the exact dating of the Hijra within 622; the nature of the "constitution of Medina" as a real founding document versus a later idealization; the degree to which the Medinan Jews' rejection of Muhammad was theologically principled versus politically driven; and the question of whether the Hijra represented a genuine break with Mecca or a temporary tactical retreat.

Hodgson treats the Hijra primarily as a political-institutional event and gives less attention to the spiritual and biographical dimensions emphasized in Muslim devotional tradition and in Western biographers such as W. Montgomery Watt (whose *Muhammad at Mecca* and *Muhammad at Medina* are cited in the text).
