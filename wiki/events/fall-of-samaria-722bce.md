# The Fall of Samaria (722 BCE)

The **fall of Samaria** ended the northern **[[kingdom-of-israel|Kingdom of
Israel]]** after two centuries and folded its territory into the
**[[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]]** as a province. It is the first of
the two great Assyrian destructions of the Hebrew kingdoms (the second being the
Babylonian [[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|fall of Jerusalem]] in 587/586).
[[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Chs. 22 & 29, Grayson & Mitchell)
covers it.

## Narrative

**Hoshea**, the last king of Israel — himself installed as an Assyrian vassal
after murdering Pekah under [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III]] — withheld
tribute and intrigued with **"So, king of Egypt"** (II Kings 17). The Assyrians
besieged **Samaria**; according to the Babylonian Chronicle and II Kings the city
fell after a siege of two to three years. **Shalmaneser V (726–722)** conducted
the siege and is credited by the Babylonian Chronicle with its capture; his
successor **[[sargon-ii|Sargon II]]** claimed the conquest in his own inscriptions
and carried out the **bulk of the deportation** during his reign. Grayson judges
the evidence "certainly in favour of Shalmaneser V" for the fall itself, with
Sargon completing the resettlement.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` [[kingdom-of-israel|Israel's]] withholding of tribute and its
appeal to Egypt — the standard fatal gamble of a Levantine vassal.
`contributed_to:` by [[tiglath-pileser-iii|Tiglath-pileser III's]] earlier
reduction of Israel (the loss of Galilee and Transjordan after 732), which left a
rump state with no margin for revolt.
`enabled:` by the Assyrian instrument of [[assyrian-mass-deportation|mass
deportation]], which made the destruction of a *nation*, not merely a dynasty,
possible.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the permanent **end of the Kingdom of Israel** and the deportation of
its elite to Mesopotamia and Media — the biblical and legendary **"ten lost
tribes."**
`produced:` the resettlement of foreign deportees in the province (II Kings 17),
the tradition behind the later **Samaritans** and the Judaean–Samaritan
estrangement.
`produced:` a Judah-centred theological reading — the prophets and the
Deuteronomistic historian interpret Samaria's fall as **divine judgment on the
"sin of Jeroboam,"** sharpening [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah's]] sense of being the
surviving remnant.

## Actors

- **[[kingdom-of-israel|Israel (Hoshea)]]** — the destroyed kingdom.
- **Shalmaneser V** — besieger and (most likely) captor; an obscure king with
  almost no inscriptions of his own.
- **[[sargon-ii|Sargon II]]** — claimant of the conquest and agent of the
  deportation.
- **[[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]** — the surviving southern kingdom, now Assyria's
  exposed frontier.

## Historiography

The **Shalmaneser-vs-Sargon dispute** over who actually took Samaria is a classic
problem, turning on the contradiction between the Babylonian Chronicle (Shalmaneser)
and Sargon's boastful annals; CAH III.2 sides with Shalmaneser for the capture,
Sargon for the deportation. The event is exceptionally well anchored by the
convergence of Assyrian records and II Kings.

## Dever (2017): a "floating" destruction and conquest by other proxies

[[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever (2017)]] complicates the neat 722/721 destruction
horizon. Ron **Tappy's** ~900-page reanalysis of Kenyon's Samaria records finds her dating
"precisely to 722/721 not credible" — no site-wide destruction blanket, the relevant pottery
"astonishingly slight" and best placed across "the last quarter of the eighth and the early
seventh century," a *floating* rather than fixed level. Across the north only **Hazor VA**
shows an unambiguous fiery destruction (~1 m of ash); Megiddo IVA shows Assyrian *presence*
but no clear destruction layer; Dan, Shechem, and Gezer show varied burning (the Nimrud
tablet even sketches "the siege of Gezer"). Dever's verdict: the **best proof of conquest is
not destruction debris but the post-conquest Assyrian footprint** — three new provinces
(Megiddo, Samarina, Dor), Assyrian-style palaces (Hazor III, Megiddo III), Assyrian Palace
ware, and cuneiform documents (Samaria, Gezer, Hadid — bearing Aramaean and Babylonian, not
Hebrew, names). Sargon II's claimed **27,290 deportees** Dever treats as an upper bound
(~10% of the region) that "may have been... only a few thousand."
