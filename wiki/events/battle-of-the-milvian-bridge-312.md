# Battle of the Milvian Bridge

The **Battle of the Milvian Bridge** (28 October 312), fought just north of Rome over the Tiber, was
[[constantine-the-great|Constantine's]] decisive victory over **Maxentius** — the battle traditionally
remembered as the turning point in the **Christianization of the Roman Empire**. Treated in
[[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 4 (**Averil Cameron**).

## Narrative

After fighting his way through northern Italy (the siege of Verona), Constantine marched on Rome,
where Maxentius held the city. Maxentius gave battle at the Milvian Bridge; his army was driven into
the **Tiber** and destroyed, Maxentius himself drowning. Constantine entered Rome in a grand
*adventus*, depicted (with the campaign) on the **Arch of Constantine**. The senate hailed him; both
pagans and Christians could read the victory as **divinely inspired** — the arch's inscription
ascribing it ambiguously to "divine inspiration" (*instinctu divinitatis*).

## Causal Analysis

The collapse of the [[roman-tetrarchy|tetrarchic]] succession after 305 had produced rival emperors;
Constantine, having secured Gaul, moved to eliminate Maxentius and gain Italy and Rome. The
**immediate trigger and Constantine's religious state of mind are bound up with the famous vision**,
which the sources report incompatibly: **Lactantius** places a dream on the eve of battle (the
chi-rho painted on the shields); **Eusebius**, writing years later, describes a **cross of light in
the sky** ("by this conquer") followed by a dream of Christ. The historicity and nature of the vision
are irrecoverable (`causes: [[unknown]]`).

## Consequence Analysis

The victory made Constantine master of the West and was followed within months by the alliance with
Licinius and the **[[edict-of-milan-313|toleration of 313]]**. It opened Constantine's career of
**active patronage of the church** — clerical privileges, church-building in Rome — and so stands at
the head of the long [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]] of the empire. Cameron
cautions, however, against reading the whole transformation back into this single day: Maximinus and
Licinius still ruled, and persecution continued in the East.

## Significance

Mythologized ever after as the moment a Roman emperor conquered "by the sign of the cross," the
Milvian Bridge is, in sober terms, the battle that put the empire's first **Christian** ruler on the
road to sole power — though its religious meaning was elaborated by hindsight in the partisan sources.
