# The Qin Unification of China (230–221 BCE)

The decade in which the state of **[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]** conquered the six rival
[[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]] and, for the first time in history, united the
**[[yellow-river-valley|Yellow]] and Yangzi river valleys under a single central government** —
"the birth of China," which took its name from Qin. From
[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]].

## Narrative

The campaigns of King **Zheng** of Qin and his general **Wang Jian** swept the rivals in sequence:
**Hann fell in 230 BCE**, **Zhao in 228**, **Wei in 225**, **Chu in 223** (the royal house
slaughtered at Shouchun), **Yan in 222**, and finally **Qi surrendered in 221 BCE** without a
fight. In 221 the victor rejected the old royal style *wang* and the premature title *Di* and
adopted a newly minted one — **[[qin-shi-huang|Qin Shihuangdi]], "First August Sovereign of Qin"**
— intending that his heirs reign "Second, Third… for ten thousand generations."

The conquest was the culmination of long structural advantages: Qin's **[[legalism|Legalist]]**
mobilization under **Shang Yang**, its **annexation of Shu (the Sichuan Basin) in 316 BCE** with the
agricultural wealth of the **Dujiangyan** irrigation works, and victories of attrition such as the
**Battle of Changping (260 BCE)**, where general **Bo Qi** encircled and starved the Zhao army and
reportedly butchered 400,000 prisoners.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the winner-take-all logic of the [[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]]
system, in which administrative and military reform — population registration, universal
conscription, merit-by-headcount, the appointed-official **commandery-and-county** structure —
made Qin the most efficient war-state. `contributed_to:` geography (Qin's defensible base "within
the passes" and its Sichuan breadbasket) and the **crossbow with interchangeable bronze triggers**
and mass infantry that Qin fielded. This is the canonical East Asian case for **structural over
"great-man" causation** (see [[great-man-vs-structural-causation]]).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **Chinese imperial system** — the title *huangdi*, the centralized bureaucratic
state of ~40 commanderies and ~1,000 counties, and the **standardization** of script (under **Li
Si**), weights, measures, coinage (the round square-holed *banliang*), and cart axle-widths — the
template that, with modifications, lasted until 1912. `produced:` the linking of frontier walls
into a precursor **Great Wall** against the [[xiongnu]] (under **Meng Tian**), and the expansion
south to northern Vietnam. `enabled:`, by its very harshness and the [[qin-dynasty|rapid Qin
collapse]] (from 209 BCE), the founding of the **[[han-dynasty|Han]]** under [[liu-bang|Liu Bang]],
which kept the machinery while disavowing the methods.

## Historiography

The unification is known mainly through hostile [[han-dynasty|Han]]-era sources —
[[sima-qian|Sima Qian's]] *Shiji* above all — which portray Legalist tyranny as the foil to Han
benevolence, so Qin brutality may be overdrawn; archaeology (excavated legal statutes, the
[[qin-shi-huang|terra-cotta army]]) supplies a corrective. Major & Cook stress that **early Han law
was initially almost identical to Qin law**, and rate the **commandery-county structure** as the
First Emperor's most consequential and enduring achievement.
