# Battle (Engagement) of Sphacteria (425/424 BCE)

## Narrative

The Athenians under Cleon and with Demosthenes executing landed on Sphacteria. The island is quite narrow (500–750 meters) and some 2 miles long, rising on all sides with steep slopes and cliffs. Only seven locations allow landing; the northern one immediately rises steeply and was unusable for a large force.

The landing succeeded because the Spartans were not on guard. Not once in the two months since the armistice had the Athenians attempted an attack. The Spartans had not established posts at all usable sites and had no signal system.

Athenians held back their hoplites and released a huge mass of unarmored men (archers to oarsmen of triremes) who threw stones. To these overwhelming numbers swarming from all sides the Spartans finally succumbed. The noise of the mass prevented the Spartans from understanding their leaders' commands. Athenians suffered no significant losses.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Spartan lapse in security and alertness; Athenian decision to use mass light troops rather than direct hoplite assault (to avoid heavy losses against desperate, skillful warriors).

**contributed_to:** Terrain (limited landing sites, narrow island) that allowed saturation once a foothold was gained; the two-month period of inaction that induced complacency.

**preceded_by:** The broader Pylos operations in the Peloponnesian War.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced:** Elimination of the Spartan force on the island as a fighting unit. Great deed by Cleon (decision and responsibility) paired with Demosthenes' technical execution.

**enabled:** Political capital for Cleon and demonstration that Athens could achieve results without ruinous hoplite casualties.

**concurrent_with:** Other actions of the period between Pericles and Alcibiades.

## Actors

- **Cleon:** Made the operation possible, bore moral responsibility, called in the technician Demosthenes. Thucydides' portrait (crazy demands yet brilliant execution) is the correct psychological picture.
- **Demosthenes:** Outstanding military technician who executed the plan.
- **Epitadas:** Spartan commander whose mission included keeping landing sites under observation.

## Historiography

Thucydides is unconditionally right. Modern scholarship that exalts Cleon or diminishes the deed to preserve consistency with his later nullity at Amphipolis misunderstands both the event and Thucydides. Study Clausewitz to understand the psychology of strategy and the nature of responsibility in command.

Topography (Grundy 1896) confirms the analysis of surprise requirements.

## Kagan Analysis (2003, Range 9001-13000)

Kagan emphasizes the strategic context and political consequences. The Spartan landing of 420 men (Epitadas commanding) on Sphacteria was to block Athenian use of the bay; their capture as hostages provided Athens “a virtual guarantee of peace” (no more invasions of Attica while held). Spartans immediately sued for truce then full peace: offensive/defensive alliance, no territorial changes mentioned, Athenians to drop claims on Plataea. Spartans framed the defeat as miscalculation, not power shift: “This misfortune we have suffered came not from our want of power... though our resources remained the same we miscalculated” (4.18.2). Athenians “grasped for more” (4.21.2); the offer “might seem that the Athenians should have accepted the Spartan offer as representing the kind of peace Pericles had had in mind from the beginning of the war”.

Tactically, Kagan highlights Demosthenes’ innovation with mass light-armed (including armed rowers): “It was not possible to attack them, for even as they fled they held the advantage, and when their pursuers turned, they were on them again.” Comon’s Messenian flanking path was decisive. The surrender of 292 (120 Spartiates) of 420 after 128 dead “shocked the Greek world. ‘In the eyes of the Greeks it was the most unexpected event in the war’ (4.40).” Cleon received highest honors (Prytaneum meals, theater seats) and dominated politics, pushing tribute assessment to 1,460 talents. “The promise of Cleon, mad as it was,” Thucydides remarks, “was fulfilled” (4.39.3).
