# Fall of Saigon (1975)

**Navigation**: Detailed operational analysis in the Warfare Hub [[hubs/warfare/cold-war/vietnam-war/vietnam-war]] (final phase / collapse mechanics section). Broader context in [[events/vietnam-war]]. This page owns the bounded event of the final offensive and collapse.

## Narrative

The Paris Peace Accords (27 Jan 1973) produced a cease-fire in place but left ~150,000 PAVN troops inside South Vietnam. "For the United States, the war was over. For the South Vietnamese, the cease-fire was but a momentary lull..."

Post-ceasefire fighting intensified. "the 'cease-fire war' claimed 26,500 ARVN dead in 1973 and would result in almost 30,000 the following year." South Vietnamese held most populated areas; PAVN held northern/western enclaves and supply corridors.

Nixon's 9 Aug 1974 resignation and subsequent US aid cuts (Congress appropriated $700M vs $1B request) devastated ARVN logistics and morale. "fighting a 'poor man’s war.'"

The North Vietnamese tested with the attack on Phuoc Long Province (13 Dec 1974). PAVN 301st Corps (3rd and 7th Divisions, tanks, artillery) rapidly overran the lightly defended province. "only 850 survived" of 5,400 committed. No US response. Ford did not mention Vietnam in his State of the Union; publicly foresaw no re-involvement. "The fall of Phuoc Long Province marked a major turning point in the Vietnam War. It demonstrated the impotence of both South Vietnam and the United States..."

Le Duan: "Never have we had military and political conditions so perfect or a strategic advantage so great as we have now." Politburo accelerated plans. Gen. Van Tien Dung assumed command of "Campaign 275" focused initially on Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands (MR II) to cut South Vietnam in two and open axes to the coast and Saigon.

**Ban Me Thuot (10–18 March 1975):** PAVN employed "blossoming lotus" tactic: diversionary attacks north/west of Kontum/Pleiku, then severed routes (19, 21, 14), main attack by 320th, F-10, and elements of 316th Divisions on the city center. Battle opened 0200 10 March. "By nightfall, the North Vietnamese controlled most of the center of the city while the South Vietnamese retained isolated positions..." ARVN counterattacks (21st Ranger Group, later 44th/45th Regts) failed due to leadership (Brig. Gen. Le Van Tuong halted relief to evacuate family), maintenance/parts shortages (CH-47s dwindled to one), lack of artillery/tank support, and friendly fire on own sector CP. "All of Darlac province had fallen to the Communist troops" by 18 March. "the South Vietnamese troops surprised us, too, because they became so disorganized so quickly."

**Thieu's truncation decision and Highlands disaster:** On 11 March, Thieu met Vien and Quang: "Given our present strength and capabilities, we certainly cannot hold and defend all the territory we want." MR III/IV "untouchable heartland"; I/II Corps "hold what you can" or phase lines back to Tuy Hoa. "light at the top, heavy on the bottom." He ordered II Corps (Lt. Gen. Pham Van Phu) to withdraw from Pleiku/Kontum via Route 7 "in such a manner as to reoccupy Ban Me Thuot at all costs."

The withdrawal (16–27 March) disintegrated into the "convoy of tears." Word leaked; panic at Pleiku ("troops were raping, burning things and committing robbery"). Over 200,000 troops and refugees packed along Route 7. PAVN ambushed flanks/rear (320th, 968th, B-1 Front). "Only some 20,000 of the 60,000 troops that had started out from Pleiku and Kontum finally reached Tuy Hoa, and most of these were not fit for combat; only about 700 of the original 7,000 Rangers escaped." "75 percent of II Corps combat strength…had been tragically expended." "Hundreds of artillery pieces and armored vehicles had been abandoned... Nearly 18,000 tons of ammunition, a month’s supply for the corps, was left in depots." "the greatest disaster in the history of ARVN." "one of the worst planned and the worst executed withdrawal operations in the annals of military history."

**I Corps collapse:** Lt. Gen. Ngo Quang Truong ("one of Thieu’s best field generals") had held well initially. Thieu pulled the Airborne Division to Saigon for capital defense and directed hold Da Nang "at all costs," implying rest of MR I expendable. Conflicting orders ("It was like a yo-yo"). Hue abandoned; 1st ARVN Division disintegrated ("We’ve been betrayed... sauve qui peu"). Da Nang (300k city) swamped by nearly 2M refugees + stragglers/deserters: "seized by convulsions of collective hysteria." "the pandemonium which overtook reason in Da Nang literally wrested control of the city from all official presence." Sea evacuation 29 March turned rout under artillery; "Only about 50,000 of the two million civilian refugees managed to escape." "Da Nang was not captured; it disintegrated in its own terror." By 30 March PAVN controlled all MR I; "not many [others] got out."

By 1 April, PAVN held MR I and most of MR II. "they had destroyed the preponderance of two ARVN corps, over one half of ARVN’s effective fighting strength." "the worst single disaster in the history of South Vietnam."

**Ho Chi Minh Campaign and final advance:** Politburo (Giap/Le Duan) accelerated: "make a big leap forward" to seize "once in a thousand years opportunity to liberate Saigon before the rainy season." Dung shifted forces 1000+ miles south; 16 infantry divisions in five corps closed on Saigon. Thieu could muster ~60,000 disorganized troops (III/IV Corps remnants + survivors).

Xuan Loc (Long Khanh, 8–20 April): Last major battle. 18th ARVN Division (Maj. Gen. Le Minh Dao) reinforced to >25,000 (incl. Airborne Brigade) fought hand-to-hand; "stiff fight." PAVN committed additional division. "The North Vietnamese had taken Xuan Loc in what would prove to be the last major battle of the war; after Xuan Loc fell, there was no longer any realistic hope of stopping the North Vietnamese advance on Saigon."

**Saigon and surrender:** Thieu resigned 21 April, bitterly blaming US. National Assembly turned government to Gen. Duong Van Minh ("Big Minh") to negotiate. US evacuation (Operation FREQUENT WIND) began 29 April; fixed-wing halted after rocket fire at Tan Son Nhut; chaotic helicopter lifts from embassy compound. Captured A-37s bombed the Presidential Palace. On 30 April, PAVN forces entered Saigon. Minh surrendered unconditionally. "NVA tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon."

Appendix chronology confirms key dates: 10 Mar Ban Me Thuot begins; 30 Mar Da Nang falls; 9–20 Apr Xuan Loc; 21 Apr Thieu resigns; 26–30 Apr Ho Chi Minh Campaign; 29–30 Apr FREQUENT WIND; 30 Apr Minh surrenders.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[phuoc-long-1974]]` (test case proved US abandonment and shattered confidence).
- `caused_by: [[ban-me-thuot-1975]]` and the subsequent "self-inflicted defeat" of the Highlands withdrawal (destroyed II Corps combat power and opened the center).
- `contributed_to: [[fall-of-da-nang-1975]]` (I Corps disintegration from conflicting orders and refugee panic).
- `caused_by:` US aid reductions post-Nixon + congressional refusal of Ford's $722M supplemental (Weyand assessment that more arms could stabilize Saigon heartland ignored).
- `contributed_to:` Thieu's truncation strategy executed too late and under fire ("withdrawal of forces under fire is one of the most difficult military maneuvers... poorly planned and even more poorly executed").
- PAVN `produced:` rapid exploitation via interior lines and captured mobility after ARVN collapse ("the army collapsed, as one observer wrote, 'like a house with its timbers eaten away by termites'").
- `concurrent_with:` Khmer Rouge victory in Cambodia (EAGLE PULL 17 Apr) which further eroded congressional support.

No significant US military response occurred despite earlier presidential assurances.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` unconditional surrender of the Republic of Vietnam on 30 April 1975; immediate reunification of Vietnam under the Democratic Republic of Vietnam.
- `produced:` end of the Vietnam War (1945–1975 Indochina conflicts).
- `produced:` successful US evacuation of Americans and many South Vietnamese (Operation FREQUENT WIND) but abandonment of vast equipment and allies.
- `produced:` communist control of all Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia).
- `produced:` profound demonstration of limits of external support when host force leadership, cohesion, and logistics collapse ("how many weeks will they fight?").
- `enabled:` broader Cold War shifts (see [[1991-end-of-cold-war]] context).
- Long-term: "Vietnam syndrome" in US policy; ARVN performance debates (Xuan Loc as exception proving prior systemic issues).

## Actors

- **Nguyen Van Thieu** (RVN President): Indecision after Phuoc Long; truncation order; conflicting directives to Truong. Resigned 21 April.
- **Van Tien Dung** (PAVN commander, Ho Chi Minh Campaign): Rapid recovery from Ban Me Thuot surprise; pursuit and concentration.
- **Ngo Quang Truong** (I Corps commander): Competent defense until orders and panic intervened; attempted orderly withdrawal.
- **Pham Van Phu** (II Corps): Executed disastrous withdrawal.
- **Duong Van Minh** ("Big Minh"): Installed 21 April; surrendered 30 April.
- ARVN remnants vs. PAVN main-force corps. See dedicated actor pages [[nguyen-van-thieu]], [[van-tien-dung]], [[ngo-quang-truong]], [[duong-van-minh]].

## Historiography

Willbanks (former advisor, CGSC historian) provides the most detailed operational account of the mechanics of the final collapse available in the collection: verbatim descriptions of "convoy of tears," "yo-yo" orders, disintegration ("not captured; it disintegrated"), leadership failures (family evacuations during battle), and PAVN exploitation. Strong on ARVN command friction, logistics dependence exposed by aid cuts, and the shift from "how many years" to "how many weeks." Vietnamese perspectives (via translators/official histories) included (Dung, Vien, Phu). 

Appendix chronology supplies precise dating. Contrasts with broader diplomatic sources by focusing on ground-level campaign mechanics rather than Washington/Hanoi high politics. Collection coverage for 1975 is now strengthened by this source for operational detail; gaps remain in South Vietnamese political dynamics and post-surrender immediate aftermath (per overall CLAUDE.md bias note for post-1750 and Americas/Asia coverage). No major revisionism here; aligns with prior sources on aid-cut impact and ARVN dependence.
