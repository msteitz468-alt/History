# The First Peloponnesian War, 460–446 BCE

## Narrative

The **First Peloponnesian War** was the irregular conflict (c. 460–446) in which Athens,
having broken with Sparta after the [[ephialtes-reform-462bce|reform of Ephialtes]],
attempted to extend its power onto the Greek mainland against Corinth, Aegina, and
ultimately Sparta. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 5, D. M. Lewis) reconstructs it,
necessarily, from Thucydides' compressed *Pentekontaetia* and the casualty inscriptions.

When the Spartans dismissed [[cimon|Cimon's]] expedition at Ithome (462/1), Athens
renounced the old alliance and allied instead with Sparta's enemies **Argos and
Thessaly**, and accepted the defection of **Megara** — gaining a foothold on the Isthmus
and building **Long Walls** from Megara to its port at Nisaea. This "first conceived" (says
Thucydides) the Corinthian hatred of Athens. The war's main episodes:

- **The Saronic war (459–457)**: against Corinth, Epidaurus, Sicyon, and above all
  **[[aegina]]** — a great sea-battle off Aegina, the capture of 70 ships, and the siege
  that finally reduced the island ("the eyesore of the Piraeus") to a tribute-paying
  member of the [[delian-league|League]]. While Corinth invaded the Megarid, Athens'
  reserves (the oldest and youngest) under **Myronides** held it off.
- **The Athenian Long Walls** linking the city to the Piraeus and Phalerum were begun —
  the strategic spine of Athenian policy for the rest of the century, making the
  city-port complex "as near to an island as anything on the mainland could be."
- **Tanagra and Oenophyta (458)**: a Spartan army, returning from protecting Doris,
  defeated Athens and its Argive allies at **Tanagra** (the Thessalian cavalry deserting),
  but withdrew; 62 days later Myronides won at **Oenophyta**, giving Athens control of
  **Boeotia, Phocis, and Opuntian Locris** — a short-lived **land empire**.
- **Tolmides' periplous (456)** burned the Spartan dockyard at Gytheum and planted the
  released Messenians of Ithome at **Naupactus**; **Pericles'** Gulf campaign followed.

## The dualism and the peace

Athenian overreach unravelled. A **Five Years' Truce** (451) paused the fighting; then in
**447–446** the land empire collapsed — defeat at **Coronea** lost Boeotia, **Euboea**
revolted, and **Megara** defected back with Corinthian help, exposing Attica. A
Peloponnesian army under the young king **Pleistoanax** invaded to Eleusis but withdrew
(its commanders, it was said, **bribed** by Pericles). The war ended in the
**[[thirty-years-peace-446bce|Thirty Years' Peace]]** (446/5): Athens surrendered its
mainland gains (Nisaea, Pegae, Achaea, Troezen) but kept its naval empire. In effect,
**Cimon's dualism** — Sparta supreme by land, Athens by sea — was accepted by both sides.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the [[ephialtes-reform-462bce|Ithome rebuff and the constitutional revolution]]
that ended Athenian–Spartan cooperation. contributed_to: Athenian mainland expansionism,
Megara's strategic value on the Isthmus, and the determination to eliminate the rival
fleet of [[aegina]]. Lewis reads Athenian policy as **expansionist opportunism** rather
than defensive reaction, with **Corinth** as the principal target of the new alliance.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the [[thirty-years-peace-446bce|Thirty Years' Peace]] and a fourteen-year
intermission; the **Long Walls**; the loss of the unsustainable land empire (the polis,
per Davies, could dominate the sea but not hold a land hegemony). It hardened the
**bipolar system** — maritime-democratic Athens vs. land-based oligarchic Sparta — whose
breakdown produced the [[peloponnesian-war|great Peloponnesian War]] of 431.

## Historiography

The chronology is among the most disputed in Greek history (the "notorious cruces of the
Pentekontaetia"): the dates of the Egyptian disaster, the end of the Third Messenian War,
Tanagra, and the truces are argued by years from Thucydides' temporal adverbs and the
epigraphic casualty lists. See [[classical-greek-historiography]].

### Osborne (2000)

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed., 2000)]] (Ch. 7, Kallet) confirms the
broad Athens-centred narrative — Athens allying with **Thessaly** and expanding its power on
land in Boeotia while Sparta, "slow to act unless forced," barely figures in the fighting,
and Corinth (rather than Sparta) felt most directly threatened by Athenian alliances and
expansion. Osborne dates the souring of Corinth's relations with Athens to Athens's alliance
with **Megara** (c. 460, after Megara left the Peloponnesian League over a border dispute
with Corinth), worsened two years later when Athens gained control of **Aegina**. The war's
end (446) with the **Thirty Years' Peace** required Athens to give up its mainland holdings
outside Attica and stipulated arbitration before either side resorted to armed conflict
again; Athens retained its naval empire intact.

### OHCW (Boardman/Griffin/Murray 1986) — Hornblower (Ch.6)

[[sources/boardman-oxford-history-classical-world-1986|OHCW (1986)]] (Hornblower): From c.460 to 446 the First Peloponnesian War pitted Athens against the Peloponnesian League; **Corinth, not Sparta, was more prominent early** (Sparta invaded Attica only in the war’s last year). Corinth’s hostility followed Megara’s adhesion to Athens. Formal hostilities with Persia ended c.449 with the Peace of Callias; the 446 settlement recognized the Athenian Naval Empire and was thus a victory for Athens, though she abandoned mainland gains (notably Boeotia). Sparta during the longer pentekontaetia stirred against Athens only thrice (465 Thasos promise unfulfilled; 446 Pleistoanax invasion then withdrawal; 440 Samos vote reversed by the league)—“reluctant” imperialism driven by helots, Arcadian unrest, Argive weakness, and [[themistocles|Themistocles]] fomenting democracy in the Peloponnese.
