## Narrative

The Irish War of Independence (21 January 1919 – 11 July 1921 truce) was an asymmetric guerrilla conflict between the Irish Republican Army and the British Crown forces in Ireland. It grew out of the political transformation produced by the [[easter-rising-1916]], the 1918 conscription crisis, and Sinn Féin's overwhelming victory in the December 1918 general election. The war officially began on the day Dáil Éireann held its first session in Dublin — by coincidence the same day two policemen were killed in an ambush at Soloheadbeg, County Tipperary, an attack undertaken at local initiative. It ended with the Anglo-Irish Treaty (December 1921), which gave dominion status to the Irish Free State within the Commonwealth while confirming the partition of Ireland established by the 1920 Government of Ireland Act.

From the beginning of 1917 to the end of 1921, 2,141 people were killed in Ireland by political violence; of that number 898 were civilians. The war was most intense in Dublin and Cork, which together accounted for 804 fatalities. From mid-1920 to mid-1922, however, Belfast was in fact the most violent place in the country, with over 460 people killed.

## Causal Analysis

The war had structural antecedents reaching back to the [[great-famine-ireland-1845]] and the failure of the [[home-rule-movement]], but its immediate causes operated in the post-1916 conjuncture:

**State legitimacy collapse**: The response to the [[easter-rising-1916]] (executions, mass internment), the 1918 conscription crisis, and the conduct of the war against labour in 1913 all eroded the British state's legitimacy in Ireland. Max Weber's condition for modern statehood — a 'monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory' — was dissolving. By mid-1920, RIC officers were resigning at nearly 200 per month.

**Republican state-building**: Sinn Féin and the Dáil 'sought to create an alternative state through the Dáil, the Irish republican police force, and the Dáil Courts, while at the same time enforcing a strict boycott of the RIC, the assize courts, and juries' (Whelehan in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], ch. 31). This dual strategy — building a parallel state while delegitimizing the existing one — was more politically sophisticated than conventional armed insurgency.

**[[first-world-war-1914]] context**: The war shaped IRA strategy (guerrilla tactics learned partly from the failure of the 1916 conventional rising), British counter-insurgency responses (paramilitaries with wartime experience), and the international environment (Wilson's fourteen points, self-determination doctrine).

## The IRA's Campaign

The IRA fought a guerrilla campaign designed to provoke counter-productive British responses. It targeted the Royal Irish Constabulary more than the army because 'they were considered to be more effective intelligence gatherers and they were also more vulnerable.' Attacks on barracks forced the constabulary to retreat to strongholds in towns, 'leaving no police presence in large parts of the countryside.'

[[michael-collins]] was the organizational genius of the IRA's campaign, running a sophisticated intelligence network that penetrated British intelligence in Dublin Castle, and organising the 'Squad' — a unit for assassinating informers and intelligence officers. Bloody Sunday (21 November 1920) was the most dramatic demonstration of Collins's approach: fourteen suspected British spies were shot in their beds in Dublin that morning; in retaliation, British forces fired into a crowd at a Gaelic football match at Croke Park that afternoon, killing twelve and injuring sixty.

Flying columns — mobile guerrilla units operating in the countryside — ambushed Crown forces, most famously at Kilmichael (28 November 1920), where Tom Barry's column killed seventeen Auxiliaries near Macroom, Cork. This ambush became the subject of intense historiographical controversy over whether some Auxiliaries were killed after a false surrender (Hart's contention) or whether the IRA correctly identified a genuine threat.

## British Counter-Insurgency

The British government reinforced the RIC with two paramilitary formations: the Black and Tans (over 11,000 new recruits drawn mainly from British urban working class, most veterans of the First World War) and the Auxiliary Division (ex-officers). These forces — 'products of the integration of political and military decision-making during the First World War' — conducted reprisals that 'descended into state terrorism, comprising collective punishment, indiscriminate violence, and torture.' Major reprisals included the burning of Cork city (December 1920) and towns such as Balbriggan, Tuam, and Mallow.

The brutalization thesis — that Black and Tans were desensitized by the First World War — has been nuanced by recent scholarship. D.M. Leeson argues that the reprisals are better explained by the confusing environment they found in Ireland, where 'the roles of policemen and soldiers were blurred, where the enemy could be selling you cigarettes or painting your barracks wall.' Official policy refused to fight a formal 'war,' demanding a 'policeman's job' that left paramilitaries operating with minimal discipline and 'almost free rein.' Irish-born RIC constables who did not fight in the war, he contends, 'instigated reprisals to a greater extent than has been traditionally acknowledged.'

## Social Composition and Local Studies

County-based studies initiated by David Fitzpatrick's groundbreaking study of Clare (1977) have illuminated the social diversity of IRA membership and the uneven distribution of violence. Peter Hart's controversial 1998 study of Cork argued that IRA members were 'Catholic almost without exception,' typically young, unmarried, unpropertied, and urban — with the implication that generational and socio-economic factors mattered as much as ideology. Hart further argued that some violence targeted Protestants not for what they did but 'because of who they were,' and discussed a 'Protestant exodus' in terms of ethnic cleansing — a characterization that generated fierce debate.

Critics (including Fitzpatrick and Bielenberg) showed that Protestant population decline in southern Ireland during the revolutionary period was largely consistent with long-term trends, that republican leaders took action to prevent sectarian killing, and that the Bandon killings Hart used as his central example were exceptional rather than representative. The 'ethnic cleansing' label is now generally rejected. Other county studies reveal that in Galway, Sinn Féin and the IRA comprised 'a coalition of the poorer classes' engaged in social as well as political revolution, while in Longford the IRA was strongest in the countryside among smallholders whose emigration had been interrupted by the war.

## Women in the War

Women experienced the war differently from men. Violence against women was relatively under-explored until recent scholarship. The most common police violence against women was hair shaving, 'designed to humiliate abettors of the IRA.' The IRA issued orders that female spies were not to be executed, though some women were killed and the classification of some as informers remains ambiguous. Cumann na mBan volunteers participated extensively in intelligence gathering, provision of safe houses, and arms smuggling; the IRA leadership's attitude toward women's combat roles 'became more chauvinist' than it had been in 1916.

## Northern Ireland

From mid-1920 to mid-1922, Belfast was the most violent place in the country. Over 460 people died there; 80 more died elsewhere in Northern Ireland. Violence in Ulster was distinguished by higher rates of rioting and inter-communal sectarian conflict rather than guerrilla ambushes. IRA flying columns were far less effective in Ulster than in the south. After the truce, an IRA border campaign provoked fierce unrest in Belfast: in the month of March 1922 alone, fifty-nine people were killed. Partition, established by the 1920 Government of Ireland Act and confirmed by the treaty, was not negotiated but presented as a fait accompli.

## The Treaty and Civil War

The Anglo-Irish Treaty (December 1921), signed after Lloyd George's ultimatum of 'war within three days,' gave dominion status to the Irish Free State — members of the new parliament were required to take a diluted oath to the King. It was endorsed by the June 1922 general election but led to a civil war (June 1922 – May 1923) between pro-Treaty and anti-Treaty republicans, in which at least 927 and possibly over 3,000 people died. The Civil War ended with the consolidation of the Free State under Cumann na nGaedheal, the marginalization of the anti-treaty republicans, and a bitter legacy of political division that shaped Irish party politics for the rest of the century.

## Historiography

The war has generated an exceptionally productive — and contentious — historiography. The social history turn, pioneered by Fitzpatrick, produced a wave of county studies that have illuminated diverse regional experiences. Peter Hart's Cork study (1998) and subsequent national work generated the most heated debate in recent Irish history — over the Kilmichael ambush, the targeting of Protestants, and the methods of historical inquiry itself, including accusations of source manipulation. The Bureau of Military History witness statements (opened 2003) and Military Service Pensions Collection files have created new methodological possibilities. Niall Whelehan's chapter (ch. 31) in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]] emphasizes transnational approaches: situating the Irish revolution in the European cycle of post-war violence and paramilitary politics (1917–mid-1920s), and in imperial circuits through which lessons from Ireland circulated to Palestine, India, and elsewhere.

Key works: David Fitzpatrick, *Politics and Irish Life* (1977); Peter Hart, *The IRA and its Enemies* (1998); Charles Townshend, *The British Campaign in Ireland, 1919–1921* (1975); Michael Hopkinson, *Green Against Green* (1988, on the Civil War); Bill Kissane, *The Politics of the Irish Civil War* (2005).
