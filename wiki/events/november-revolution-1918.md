## Narrative

The German Revolution of November 1918 began not in Berlin but in the naval base at Wilhelmshaven on 29 October 1918, when sailors mutinied against orders to sail on a suicidal last battle to salvage the navy's honor. The conflagration spread rapidly. Workers' and Soldiers' Councils formed across Germany, depriving local rulers and military authorities of their power. On 9 November, Kaiser [[wilhelm-ii]] abdicated. Philipp Scheidemann, the Social Democratic Reichstag deputy, proclaimed the republic from the Reichstag window just in time to forestall Karl Liebknecht's proclamation of a Soviet-style republic from the Berlin Palace. The Armistice followed on 11 November 1918.

The revolution's first stage comprised the toppling of the monarchy, recognition of military defeat, and preparation of a National Assembly. In its aims and social base, it was emphatically a popular democratic movement, not a Bolshevik one. As one Uhlan soldier at the field army's council meeting in Bad Ems (1–2 December 1918) summarized: "All front-line soldiers demand freedom, truth, justice and bread" (cited in [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 17).

Elections for the constituent National Assembly in January 1919 gave the "Weimar coalition" — SPD, the Catholic Center Party (Zentrum), and the left-liberal German Democratic Party (DDP) — almost three-quarters of the vote, a massive mandate for parliamentary democracy.

The revolution's second, more violent phase erupted when the Majority SPD leadership summoned the Freikorps to crush Communist uprisings in Berlin and Munich in early 1919. Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were murdered on 15 January 1919. This decision permanently poisoned relations between the SPD and the radical left throughout the [[weimar-republic]].

## Causal Analysis

The revolution's immediate trigger was the sailors' mutiny, but its preconditions had been building since at least 1916. The historian Wilhelm Deist identified the "military strike" — the mass self-demobilization of roughly 750,000 front soldiers who deserted between mid-August and Armistice Day — as "the decisive precondition for the revolution and determined its form and content" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 17). This was not merely physical exhaustion but political rejection: soldiers and workers had developed a language of democratic entitlement and social justice through four years of mobilization that transcended the Wilhelmine political order.

The January 1918 mass strike (approximately one million workers, 400,000 in Berlin alone), demanding immediate peace, full parliamentarization, lifting of the state of siege, and release of political prisoners, was the "dress rehearsal" for November. Critically, this movement bypassed the SPD and USPD party leaderships, suggesting its roots in popular mobilization rather than party organization.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: [[weimar-republic]] — Germany's first parliamentary democracy
- `produced`: Weimar Constitution (August 1919) — universal suffrage including women, proportional representation
- `enabled`: Treaty of Versailles (June 1919), which the democratic government was compelled to sign
- `produced`: SPD-Freikorps alliance that crushed the Communist uprising, generating lasting left-wing fragmentation
- `enabled`: the "stab-in-the-back" myth (Dolchstoßlegende), exploited by the right to delegitimize the Republic by associating it with defeat

The protest potential that built up during the war faded quickly once peace and a democratic framework were achieved. Many veterans glossed over their wartime grievances, ceding commemoration of the war to the political right, which used a "heroic, militant discourse about the legacy of the war as leverage against the new political system" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 17).

## Evans (2003): The Revolution's Poisoned Aftermath

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) stresses a deliberate strategic
calculation at the origin: Ludendorff and the Reich leadership, once convinced
the war was lost, pushed democratization precisely so civilian politicians —
not the Kaiser or army — would bear the blame for the peace (caused_by, not
incidental). The army then "simply melted away."

On the violence: the [[freikorps|Free Corps]] suppressed the Spartacist rising
with "unprecedented violence and brutality" (Liebknecht and Luxemburg
murdered; revolutionaries mown down or summarily executed in multiple cities);
in spring 1920, after the [[kapp-putsch-1920|Kapp Putsch]] was defeated by
general strike, the Ruhr workers' "Red Army" was crushed by Free Corps units
backed by the regular army and the SPD: "well over a thousand members of the
Red Army were slaughtered, most of them prisoners 'shot while trying to
escape.'" Evans's causal chain (attributed): the SPD's recruitment of the Free
Corps permanently poisoned SPD–Communist cooperation **and** armed a
paramilitary right that turned on its sponsors as "November criminals" —
Organization Consul murdering [[walther-rathenau|Rathenau]], Erzberger, and
Haase — a self-undermining dynamic that contributed_to
[[weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]]. See also [[stab-in-the-back-myth]].

## Historiography

**Source quality**: Chapter 17 of [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] (Benjamin Ziemann) draws on a large body of specialist scholarship including Wilhelm Deist's seminal essay on the "military strike," Volker Ullrich's social history of Hamburg, and Roger Chickering's urban history of Freiburg. The field is rich in primary sources — Feldpostbriefe (military postal letters) and soldiers' council records.

**Scholarly debates**: The key historiographical debate concerns the nature and radicalism of the revolution. Ziemann argues it was a genuine popular democratic revolution, not merely a product of "exhaustion," and that its supporters wanted participatory democracy, not Bolshevism. The Spartacus League (never more than 3,000 members) played "hardly any role in the chain of events" leading to 9 November, despite conservative hysteria treating Rosa Luxemburg and Liebknecht as vanguards of Bolshevik revolution.

**Recent revisionism**: The older interpretation emphasizing radical left-wing threat has given way to recognition that the revolution's core constituency was the democratic socialist labor movement, Catholic workers, white-collar workers, and lower-middle-class Germans who had shifted left during the war.

**Collection coverage**: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]] provides strong coverage. The broader Bibliotheca Alexandrina collection is thin on Weimar-era primary sources.
