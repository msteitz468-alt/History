# The Sack of Susa (646 BCE)

The deliberate destruction of **[[susa|Susa]]** and the Elamite heartland by
**[[ashurbanipal|Assurbanipal]]** of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]] at the climax of his
fifth and final Elamite campaign — the event that ended **[[elam|Elam]]** as an
independent great power after two millennia and one of the most thoroughly documented acts
of state destruction in the ancient Near East.

## Narrative

By the 650s BCE, [[elam|Elam]] and Babylon had long been **allies against Assyria**; once
the Babylonian army declined, Elamites "bore the brunt of campaigning on behalf of
Babylonia." After the Elamite king **Te-Umman** was defeated and decapitated at the
**battle of Til-Tuba on the Ulai river (653 BCE)**, Assurbanipal installed client kings at
Madaktu — but his nominee **Huban-nikash II joined the great revolt of
Shamash-shum-ukin** (Assurbanipal's brother, 652–648 BCE). A chaos of Elamite coups
followed. When the dust settled under **Huban-haltash III (Ummanaldash)**, Assurbanipal
launched a final campaign (647–646 BCE), broke the fortress of Bit-Imbi ("the fulcrum of
Elam, which bars Elam like a great wall"), and entered Susa.

The Assyrians spent **"one month and twenty-five days"** systematically destroying the
city: emptying its treasuries; carrying off the gods and goddesses of Elam with their
priesthoods; seizing **thirty-two statues of Elamite kings**; **opening the royal tombs**
and deporting the kings' bones to Assyria; and **demolishing the ziggurat of Susa,
breaking off its bronze "horns."** Assurbanipal's own boast: *"In a month of days I
levelled the whole of Elam, I deprived its fields of the sound of human voices… I turned
it into a pasture for wild asses, gazelles, and all manner of wild animals."* Among the
plunder was the cult statue of **Nanaya**, said to have been carried from Uruk by the
Elamites ~1,635 years earlier, now restored to Babylonia.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[elam|Elam's repeated military intervention]] in the Assyro-Babylonian
  wars — Elamite armies (often purchased: 80,000 bowmen for [[merodach-baladan]] in 703
  BCE) had backed Babylonian and Chaldaean resistance for a century.
- `caused_by:` the **Shamash-shum-ukin rebellion** (652–648 BCE), into which the Assyrian
  client-king of Elam defected — converting Elam from buffer to enemy.
- `contributed_to:` the long structural **enmity-and-exchange** dynamic between the Iranian
  highlands and Mesopotamia (see [[elam]]).
- `preceded_by:` the [[elam|battle of Til-Tuba (653 BCE)]] and the decapitation of
  Te-Umman — temporally prior and a proximate trigger, but the sack followed the *later*
  Babylonian revolt, not Til-Tuba directly.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **end of independent Elam** as a great power; the Neo-Elamite kingdom
  never recovered its former weight, though a reduced government persisted at Susa
  (Nabopolassar returned divine statues there in 625 BCE).
- `enabled:` the **Elamite–Persian transition**. With lowland Elam shattered, the
  initiative passed to the highland **[[anshan|Anshan]]** zone, where Persian dynasts
  styling themselves "king of Anshan" (Teispes, [[cyrus-the-great|Cyrus]]) absorbed the
  Elamite legacy. Assurbanipal's own annals (~643 BCE) record **Kurash of Parsumash**
  (often identified with Cyrus I) sending tribute and a hostage son to Nineveh on hearing
  of Elam's fall.
- `contributed_to:` the eventual rise of the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenid Empire]],
  whose administration would run in the **Elamite language** and whose kings made
  [[susa|Susa]] an imperial capital — the conquered culture surviving inside its conqueror.

## Actors

- **[[ashurbanipal]]** — Assyrian king, author of the destruction and of the vivid
  annals/reliefs that record it.
- **[[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyria]]** — at the height of its power.
- **Huban-haltash III (Ummanaldash)** — last effective Neo-Elamite king of the heartland,
  who fled to the mountains (and to [[chogha-zanbil|Dur-Untash]]) and survived to return
  to ruined Madaktu.
- **[[elam]]** / **[[susa]]** — the victim state and city.

## Historiography

The event is known overwhelmingly through **Assyrian royal inscriptions and the relief
cycles** of the North Palace at Nineveh (the Til-Tuba and Madaktu reliefs, the "ziggurat
relief," and the famous "garden party" where Te-Umman's severed head hangs from a tree) —
sources designed as triumphal propaganda. [[potts-archaeology-of-elam-2016|Potts]] stresses
their bias: the reliefs underline "the technical superiority of the Assyrian army" over
lightly armed, barefoot Elamite bowmen, and the annals mythologize (the Halule narrative
alludes to *Enuma Elish*). The **Babylonian Chronicle** offers a less prejudiced check.
Archaeologically, the post-646 "Neo-Elamite renaissance" at Susa is debated, and rich late
Neo-Elamite tombs (**Arjan**, **Jubaji**) show the highland and lowland elites were not
wholly extinguished.
