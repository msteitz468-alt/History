# The Warring States Period

## Narrative

The **Warring States period** (475–221 BCE) was the era of intense multi-state warfare and
state-building at the end of the **[[zhou-dynasty|Eastern Zhou]]**, when the formerly
semi-autonomous Zhou vassal territories had become fully sovereign kingdoms. In
[[benjamin-cwh-v4-2015|CWH IV]] (Holcombe, Ch 15) it is the crucible of the Chinese
imperial state: through relentless competition the number of states was consolidated to
**seven**, warfare evolved from aristocratic chariot dueling into "mass confrontations
between conscript armies of peasant infantrymen wielding deadly crossbows, sometimes
numbering in the hundreds of thousands," and "the stage was set for China's first imperial
unification."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[zhou-dynasty]]` — the decay of Zhou royal authority after the eastward
  flight of 771 BCE left the regional lords sovereign and mutually hostile.
- `contributed_to:` military-administrative competition that rewarded states able to
  mobilize their whole populations.

## The Engine of State Formation

Survival demanded the bureaucratic machinery of mass mobilization, developed most fully
under **Legalism** — codified written law, the first systematic agricultural tax (Qin,
408 BCE), **mandatory household registration** (375 BCE) enabling universal taxation and
conscription, centrally administered **counties and commanderies** (from 350 BCE), and a
merit-appointed bureaucracy replacing the hereditary aristocracy. The northwestern state
of **[[qin-dynasty|Qin]]** perfected these techniques.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[qin-dynasty]]` — in 230–221 BCE Qin conquered the other six kingdoms and
  unified China for the first time into a single centralized state.
- `enabled:` the imperial bureaucratic order that, with modifications, lasted until 1912.
- `concurrent_with:` the great age of Chinese philosophy — Confucianism, Daoism, Mohism,
  Legalism — China's contribution to the [[axial-age|Axial Age]], a creative response to
  the same chaos.

## Actors

[[qin-dynasty|Qin]] · the rival kingdoms · the declining [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]] court ·
[[confucius|Confucius]] and the [[axial-age|classical thinkers]]

## Historiography

The period is known from later Han-era histories (Sima Qian) and the texts of the warring
philosophical schools, which read it through the lens of the unification that followed.
The structural reading — that competitive state-formation, not any individual, produced the
imperial state — makes it a leading East Asian case in the
[[great-man-vs-structural-causation|structural-vs-contingent causation]] debate.
