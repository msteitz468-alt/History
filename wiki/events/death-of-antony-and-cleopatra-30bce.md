# The Fall of Alexandria and the Deaths of Antony and Cleopatra (30 BCE)

The end of **Mark Antony** and **Cleopatra VII** — and with them the **Ptolemaic
dynasty** and the last resistance to Octavian — at Alexandria, a year after
**[[battle-of-actium-31bce|Actium]]**. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 1
(Pelling).

## Narrative

After Actium the lovers fled to **Egypt**, their support collapsing: the Cyrene legions
declared for Octavian, the eastern client kings deserted (Herod, Amyntas, Archelaus,
Polemo), and Antony's fleet went over. Octavian, needing **Egypt's treasure** to pay
his veterans, advanced in a pincer (Cornelius Gallus from the west, himself from the
east through Pelusium). On **1 August 30** Alexandria fell.

**Antony**, hearing false reports that Cleopatra was dead, **stabbed himself** and died
in her arms (in the romantic tradition of Plutarch and Shakespeare). **Cleopatra** was
taken alive — Octavian wanting her for his triumph — but, dressed in royal regalia,
she **killed herself** (by the asp, the Ptolemaic *uraeus*-cobra), flanked by her maids
Iras and Charmion: "her final marvellous victory." Pelling defends the suicide
tradition against the modern view that Octavian connived in her death, noting that a
deliberate murder would have been easier and less embarrassing to him than a queen who
"outwitted" him.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the [[battle-of-actium-31bce|defeat at Actium]] and the total desertion of
Antony's cause; the timing driven by **Octavian's urgent need of Egypt's treasure** for
his ~40,000 veterans awaiting land.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **extinction of the Ptolemaic dynasty** and the **annexation of
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Egypt]]** as Octavian's personal domain (governed by an equestrian
prefect); the killing of **Caesarion** (Caesar's son) and **Antyllus** (Antony's heir),
removing rival claimants; the **end of the Roman civil wars**.
enabled: Octavian's **undisputed mastery of the Roman world** — the immense Egyptian
treasure funding the veterans and the transition to the **[[augustus|Augustan]]**
Principate (and the closing of the temple of Janus).

## Historiography

Plutarch's *Antony* (and Shakespeare after him), Cassius Dio, Horace's Cleopatra Ode —
all shaped by the Augustan tradition that made Cleopatra the great "eastern menace"
since Hannibal (Octavian's *evocatio* of the city's gods, the fetial war). Pelling
treats the splendid death-scenes as "history, not legend" in outline.
