# Wounded Knee Massacre

U.S. Army killing of Lakota people at Wounded Knee Creek on the [[pine-ridge-reservation]], South Dakota, **29 December 1890**—the bloodiest conflict in North America during Hoxie’s early reservation-period survey and a civilizational-scale deterrent against armed resistance.

## Narrative

The bloodiest conflict in North America during this period occurred in **1890** along **Wounded Knee Creek** on the Pine Ridge Sioux Reservation in newly admitted South Dakota. Pine Ridge and Rosebud communities were many times the size of small Apache and Crow groups previously intimidated by troop massing; their leaders were veterans of 1860s eastern Montana campaigns and of Custer’s defeat at the Little Bighorn (1876).

**Cascade:**
1. During summer 1890 growing numbers of Lakota performed the Ghost Dance; Indian Office massed troops around Sioux camps ([[ghost-dance-1890]]).
2. **15 December 1890**, Standing Rock: acting on orders of Agent **James McLaughlin** (supported by regular Army units), a detachment of **Indian policemen killed Sitting Bull**.
3. Miniconjou leader **Big Foot** fled Cheyenne River agency; apprehended by elements of the **Seventh Cavalry** near Wounded Knee Creek on **28 December**.
4. **Next morning (29 December)**, during disarmament, shots rang out over the frozen prairie. **Disputes continue over who fired the first shot**, but when the army’s repeating rifles and mobile artillery fell silent, **146 Sioux men, women, and children** (including the ill chief in his tent) lay dead; **25 soldiers** had perished.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by (proximate context): [[ghost-dance-1890]] — Hoxie frames government **nervous reaction** plus **mutual fear**, not a simple “Ghost Dance caused massacre” reduction.
- contributed_to: **1889 Crook commission** “agreement” opening millions of acres west of the Missouri after Teton Sioux had defeated two earlier reduction attempts; land-loss and statehood pressure for North/South Dakota.
- caused_by (immediate cascade): Sitting Bull’s killing at Standing Rock and Big Foot’s flight with his band.
- concurrent_with: broader assault phase—Courts of Indian Offenses, boarding schools, allotment, end of buffalo.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: deterrent effect—killing “drove home to Native people the reality of Euro-American domination”; clear that in the United States, as in Canada, **direct opposition to government policy would be treated as a challenge to national power and met with force**.
- Despite continued resistance elsewhere (Redbird Smith/Cherokee; Chitto Harjo/Creek; Chippewa, Cree, Winnebago flight to unclaimed lands), Wounded Knee marked the exemplary armed-suppression lesson of the assault phase.
- **Not** the end of Ghost Dance religion—practice continued under Wovoka into the 1930s among multiple nations.
- Long memory: site and name later mobilized in [[wounded-knee-occupation-1973]] (AIM) as historical symbol—beyond Hoxie’s 1890 narrative but part of wiki network.

## Historiography

Hoxie (1996) gives casualty figures **146 Sioux / 25 soldiers**, situates the event on Pine Ridge, preserves **first-shot dispute**, and interprets political meaning as domination signal rather than last “Indian war” romance. He links Sitting Bull’s death as cascade trigger and Ghost Dance as proximate religious/military context without adjudicating who fired first. Casualty counts and sequence remain contested in wider literature; wiki records Hoxie’s reported numbers with attribution.
