# The March of the Ten Thousand, 401–399 BCE

## Narrative

In **401 BCE** the Persian prince **[[cyrus-the-younger|Cyrus the Younger]]** marched
upcountry from Sardis to wrest the throne from his brother **[[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes
II]]**, at the head of a Persian army stiffened by some **13,000 Greek mercenaries** —
"the Ten Thousand" — recruited under the pretext of a campaign against the Pisidians. At
**Cunaxa**, near Babylon, the Greek hoplites routed the troops facing them, but **Cyrus
was killed** charging the king, and the coup died with him. The Greek generals were then
treacherously seized and executed by the satrap Tissaphernes, leaving the army stranded
deep in Mesopotamia without leaders.

Electing new commanders — among them the Athenian **[[xenophon]]** — the Ten Thousand
fought their way north up the Tigris and through the mountains of the Carduchi and
Armenia to the Black Sea at **Trapezus**, their cry of *"Thalatta! Thalatta!"* ("The sea!
The sea!") on sighting it becoming one of antiquity's famous moments. [[xenophon|
Xenophon's]] *Anabasis* is the eyewitness record, and "the closest thing to a Greek
*inside source*" on the Persian empire.

## Significance and interpretation

[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 3, Hornblower) reads the march less as a military
romance than as the **origin of a dangerous idea**. The near-success of Cyrus and his
Greeks at Cunaxa — and the army's ability to traverse the empire and escape — bred the
"**delusory myth**," much promoted by **Isocrates**, that the vast Persian empire was
"vulnerable to rapid *anabasis*, thrust up-country, from the west." Hornblower is at
pains to puncture it: Cyrus nearly won only because of his **anomalous position** as a
prince commanding all the western satrapies at once (see [[cyrus-the-younger]]), and
[[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]] proved in the 390s that an invader who could not first secure
Ionia and Caria would simply be "bottled up in the interior." Yet the myth outlived the
correction, feeding the panhellenist programme that **Philip and [[alexander-the-great|
Alexander]]** would eventually carry out.

## Historiography

The march is exceptionally well attested by ancient standards because its principal
source, **Xenophon**'s *Anabasis*, is a **rare first-person eyewitness account** by one
of the expedition's own elected leaders — but this intimacy is also its chief
methodological problem: Xenophon writes to justify and dramatize his own conduct and
leadership, and modern historians read the work with an eye to self-serving omissions
and shaping. Independent corroboration is thin: **Diodorus Siculus** (via Ephorus)
covers the same events more briefly and from a different tradition, while **Ctesias**'s
*Persica* — the account of a Greek physician resident at the Achaemenid court, surviving
only in fragments and later summary (notably in Plutarch's *Artaxerxes*) — offers a
Persian-court perspective but is notoriously unreliable and sensationalized. The size of
Cyrus's Greek and Persian forces, the circumstances of Cyrus's death at Cunaxa, and the
degree to which Xenophon inflates his own role in the retreat all remain debated, though
the overall itinerary and outcome are not seriously in question.
