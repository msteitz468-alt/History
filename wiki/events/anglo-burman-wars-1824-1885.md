## Narrative

Three wars between British India and the Konbaung kingdom of Burma across sixty years produced the complete absorption of Burma into the British empire. They are best understood not as three separate events but as three episodes of a single structural conflict between two incompatible political cultures: British India's insistence on explicit acknowledgment of paramountcy, and the Burman court's tradition of assertive sovereignty that had sacked Ayutthaya (1767) and defied the Qing armies.

### First Anglo-Burman War, 1824–1826

Frontier tensions in Arakan (where Burman conquest in 1784 had sent refugees into British Bengal who then used British territory for counter-raids), Assam, Manipur, and Cachar accumulated into a crisis. Governor-General Amherst declared war preemptively in March 1824, framing the aim as producing "such an impression of the power and resources of the British Empire in India as will deter the Court of Ava from any attempt again to disturb the friendly relations." The campaign was poorly executed. Initial reverses forced the British to escalate their demands to extract tangible proofs of victory needed to maintain Indian prestige.

The **Treaty of Yandabo** (1826) required Burma to: cede Arakan, Assam, and Tenasserim; acknowledge British rights in Manipur and Cachar; pay a large indemnity; receive a British Resident at Ava; send an ambassador to Calcutta. The core Burman kingdom was not annexed.

The underlying problem was not resolved. John Crawford, sent to negotiate a commercial treaty, recommended against a Resident: one would be "an object of jealousy to a government indescribably ignorant and suspicious." The Burman attitude — that a Resident was "a proof of our supremacy and a badge of his servility and vassalage" — persisted unchanged.

### Interwar Period: Tharrawaddy, Mindon Min, and the Shoe Question

King Tharrawaddy (r. 1838–46) explicitly repudiated his predecessor's treaties. Mindon Min (r. 1853–78) took a different path: he accepted the consequences of the Second War without a formal treaty, developed good relations with the British through commercial treaties and missions, and attempted "defensive westernisation." But he also asserted Burma's independence by pursuing diplomatic relations with France, the United States, and Russia — seeking to establish Burma as a recognized international actor. Britain could not accept full Burman independence and wanted subordination acknowledged. The "shoe question" — the requirement that British envoys remove footwear before royal reception — became a symbol of incompatible protocols; when an envoy made it an issue in 1875, the British could not back down. The Resident was effectively paralyzed and finally withdrawn in 1879 after an unrelated British diplomat was killed in Afghanistan.

### Second Anglo-Burman War, 1852

A commercial dispute between British merchants and the Burman governor of Rangoon, handled with "high-handedness" by a British naval commander, provided the immediate occasion. Governor-General Dalhousie, though annexationist in India, framed his motivation in prestige terms: "the Government of India could never, consistently with its own safety, permit itself to stand for a single day in an attitude of inferiority towards a native power." Pegu (Lower Burma, including Rangoon) was annexed. No treaty was signed: Dalhousie calculated treaty-making "would invite further quarrels" and would require a further advance to the capital.

Richard Cobden's pamphlet "How Wars are Got up in India" attacked the second war as an unjust commercial adventure. The debate over its causation — genuine security concern vs. manufactured commercial pretext — anticipates the broader debate on imperialism.

### Third Anglo-Burman War, 1885

The strategic context changed decisively in the early 1880s. France's establishment of a protectorate over Vietnam (1883–5) placed French power on Burma's eastern frontier. French agents and concessionaires appeared in Mandalay. In January 1885 Premier Jules Ferry sent a secret letter to the Burman ambassadors offering arms arrangements once Tonkin was pacified. The letter was publicized by Andreino, the Italian consul and agent for British timber interests, and the British determined on an ultimatum. Lord Randolph Churchill was direct: "It is French intrigue which has forced us to go to Burmah; but for that element we might have treated Theebaw with severe neglect."

The immediate pretext — a commercial dispute over the Bombay-Burma Timber Company's teak concessions — was a fig leaf. British-Indian forces crossed the frontier in November 1885; within two weeks they had occupied Mandalay. King Thibaw was exiled to India. Viceroy Dufferin chose outright annexation over protectorate, believing that a vacuum in Mandalay required direct control "to provide against European intrigue."

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Structural incompatibility between British-Indian paramountcy doctrine and Burman royal sovereignty tradition; French advance in Indochina (Third War specifically).

**contributed_to:** Successive Burman rulers' inability to accept managed subordination; Mindon Min's fatal error of diplomatic diversification at the wrong moment; personal high-handedness of British military and naval officers (Second War).

**preceded_by:** British conquest of India and development of the subsidiary alliance system that generated the security imperatives Burma resisted.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced:** [[british-burma]] — Burma incorporated into British India as a province, without protectorate, without surviving indigenous monarchy, without the Resident system's nominal deference to local sovereignty that characterized Malaya.

**produced:** A prolonged pacification campaign (1885–c.1895) demonstrating that the popular base of Konbaung loyalty exceeded British expectations. "It is a mistake to suppose that these people were anxiously awaiting annexation. The more I see and hear, the more convinced I am that they are very loyal, in their easy-going way, to the house of Alompra" (Brigadier-General George White, 1885).

**enabled:** Settlement of Burma's Chinese and French frontiers in the late 1880s–1890s.

**contrasts_with:** The Siamese case — where a comparable dynamic of encroachment and modernization demands was managed via the Bowring Treaty (1855) and subsequent territorial cessions without loss of core independence.

## Historiography

The Anglo-Burman Wars have been extensively studied in both British-Indian and Burmese historiography. Key debates: (1) the relative weight of strategic vs. commercial causation, especially in the Second War (Cobden vs. official British framing; subsequent scholars generally favor strategic prestige over commercial interest); (2) the character of Burman resistance — whether the pacification-era "dacoity" label obscures organized political resistance to annexation (Aung-Thwin argued strongly for the latter, M. Aung-Thwin, "The British 'Pacification' of Burma," JSEAS 1985); (3) the Mindon Min counterfactual — whether a less diplomatically adventurous successor to Mindon could have preserved the Burman kingdom.
