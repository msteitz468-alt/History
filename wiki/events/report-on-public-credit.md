## Narrative

The Report on the Public Credit was Alexander Hamilton's first major state paper as Secretary of the Treasury, submitted to Congress on 14 January 1790 after months of solitary drafting. It proposed that the federal government fully honor the face value of the domestic debt inherited from the Confederation period — rather than discount it, as many argued the depreciated certificates deserved — and that it assume the outstanding Revolutionary War debts of the individual states, converting a patchwork of federal and state obligations into a single funded national debt serviced by federal revenue (chiefly tariffs and, later, excise taxes).

Hamilton's argument, elaborated at length in the roughly 40,000-word report, was that scrupulous, undiscounted repayment would establish the young federal government's creditworthiness at home and abroad, attract capital, and bind wealthy creditors' interests to the survival of the new Constitution — a deliberate use of financial self-interest to secure political loyalty. The domestic-debt provisions passed relatively easily, but assumption of state debts provoked fierce opposition from [[madison-james|James Madison]] and other members who objected that it rewarded speculators (who had bought up depreciated certificates from the original, often veteran, holders) and unfairly benefited states, like Massachusetts and South Carolina, that had failed to pay down their own war debt, at the expense of states like Virginia that largely had.

The impasse over assumption was broken by the Compromise of 1790, negotiated at a dinner between Hamilton, Madison, and [[jefferson-thomas|Thomas Jefferson]]: southern votes for assumption were traded for locating the permanent national capital on the Potomac River. The Report's program, together with Hamilton's subsequent Report on a National Bank and Report on Manufactures, established the fiscal architecture of the early republic and crystallized the emerging Federalist–Republican partisan divide.

## Causal Analysis

The report was made necessary by the unresolved Revolutionary War debt and the credit and currency crises of the Confederation period, and made possible by Hamilton's Treasury appointment and Washington's confidence in him. Its specific assumption provision was shaped by the uneven distribution of unpaid state debts.

## Consequence Analysis

Assumption plus funding gave the federal government, rather than the states, primacy over public finance and created a durable national debt instrument that tied investor interests to federal survival. The political cost was the Compromise of 1790 (siting the capital) and the acceleration of Hamilton-Jefferson/Madison factionalism that hardened into the First Party System.

## Actors

[[hamilton-alexander]] authored and championed the report; [[washington-george]] backed it as president; [[madison-james]] led legislative opposition to assumption; [[jefferson-thomas]] brokered the eventual compromise despite his own reservations about Hamilton's fiscal program.

## Historiography

Hamilton's biographers (notably Ron Chernow) treat the Report as a masterstroke of state-building, crediting Hamilton's vision of using British-style public finance to enable the United States to eventually surpass Britain economically. Contemporary and later critics (Madison, Jefferson, and the Jeffersonian tradition) viewed assumption as favoring speculators and northern financial interests over ordinary citizens and debtor states, a debate that anticipated the deeper Hamiltonian/Jeffersonian dispute over the proper scale and character of the federal government.
