## Narrative

The **Roman-Persian Treaty of 561/2** was negotiated between [[sasanian-empire|Khusrau I]]
and Justinian's government following the death of the Persian Arab super-phylarch
[[al-mundhir-iii-lakhmid|al-Mundhir III (Alamoundaros)]] in 554. It is recorded in
unusual detail by the historian **Menander Protector**, who had direct access to the
imperial documents and copied the treaty's terms into his history — making it one of the
best-documented diplomatic instruments of the period.

For the first time, Arab allies of both empires were **specifically named** in a
Romano-Persian treaty: its terms explicitly forbade Roman Arab clients from attacking
Persian regular forces and vice versa. The treaty's language for these Arab allies also
shifted from *hypospondoi* — an inferior, subordinate relationship — to *symmachoi*,
"allies on more equal terms," a change Fisher reads as "remarkable testament" to the
growing legal and political standing the phylarchs of both empires had achieved by
mid-century. As part of the settlement, the Sasanians agreed to relax restrictions on
Christian religious practice within Persia.

Menander also records an ancillary dispute: **Amr (Ambrus)**, son of al-Mundhir III and
his successor as Persian Arab super-phylarch, unsuccessfully sought the same gold
payments Justinian had given his father, arguing that these had been diplomatic "gifts"
that obliged reciprocity — a claim the Roman side rejected. In the year following the
treaty, [[al-harith-ibn-jabala]] traveled to Constantinople both to discuss succession
arrangements with Justinian (per Theophanes) and to address Amr's treaty-skirting raids
on al-Harith's own territory, rather than direct Roman lands (per Malalas).

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by`: the death of al-Mundhir III in 554 removed the dominant, deniable Persian
agent whose decades of extralegal raiding had made a comprehensive Arab-inclusive treaty
impossible; his death opened space for a formal settlement that both empires, exhausted
by intermittent warfare since the 530s, had incentive to conclude.

## Consequence Analysis

The treaty represents the high-water mark of formal recognition for the Roman and
Persian Arab super-phylarchates as legitimate parties to inter-imperial diplomacy rather
than mere instruments of their patrons. It did not, however, end unauthorized fighting
among the Arab clients themselves: within a decade [[al-mundhir-ibn-al-harith]] (al-
Harith's son and successor) was contesting territory with Amr's brother Qabus in
violation of its spirit, if not its letter. The treaty's precedent of direct
super-phylarch diplomacy with Constantinople is visible again in al-Harith's 563 visit,
which secured an orderly succession — part of the broader "super-phylarchate" system
that would eventually collapse with al-Mundhir ibn al-Harith's exile in 582 and
[[al-numan-iii]]'s fall at al-Hira c. 602.

## Historiography

The treaty is unusually well documented by ancient standards because Menander Protector
had access to and transcribed the actual treaty text — a rarity for this period's
Roman-Persian diplomacy, most of which is known only through summary or hostile
paraphrase. The shift in Arab-ally terminology from *hypospondoi* to *symmachoi* is
treated by Fisher as significant evidence for the institutional maturation of the
phylarch system by the 560s, though the practical limits of that maturation are equally
visible in the treaty's failure to prevent the very Arab-on-Arab violence (Amr vs.
al-Harith's family) that followed within years.
