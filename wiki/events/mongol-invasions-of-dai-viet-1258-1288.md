## Narrative

Three distinct Mongol/Yuan campaigns struck Dai Viet between 1257 and 1288, each repelled by the [[tran-dynasty-vietnam|Tran dynasty]] under a stable core leadership of two co-reigning kings (senior and junior) and their extended family of princely commanders, chief among them [[tran-hung-dao|Tran Quoc Tuan (Tran Hung Dao)]].

**First invasion (1257–58).** Uriyangqadai, a Mongol general under Kublai (then campaigning against Southern Song), led roughly 25,000 men (5,000 Mongols, 20,000 Yunnan levies) down through Nan Zhao territory, aiming to pass through Tran lands to attack Song from the south. The Tran imprisoned his envoys and refused passage. After Tran forces failed to hold the invaders near Binh Xuyen (35 km from Thang Long), king Tran Canh withdrew downriver as general Le Phu Tran delayed the Mongols; Thuan Trinh (wife of Tran Thu Do) organized the evacuation of Tran women, children, and the crown prince. Uriyangqadai occupied an empty, provisionless Thang Long; within twelve days of the initial engagement, Tran Canh rallied his forces and drove the Mongols out. This first war was, per Taylor, "relatively insignificant" compared to what followed, but it prompted immediate Tran reforms: installation of a junior co-king within two months and intensified border vigilance.

**Interwar period (1258–1284).** Kublai proclaimed the Yuan dynasty in 1271 and escalated demands on Thang Long (personal submission, hostages, census/tax data, a resident garrison, and overseer authority via a Daruhaci). The Tran stalled for over a decade through diplomatic evasion, while completing the conquest of Southern Song (1279) removed Thang Long's longtime imperial suzerain and altered the regional balance. Provocative Mongol embassies, including the 1281–82 mission of Chai Zhuang (who rode armed into the palace and struck a guard), were met with calculated Tran patience — including Tran Quoc Tuan's disguise as a monk to defuse the envoy.

**Second invasion (1284–85).** Togan (Kublai's son) led a large combined force from Guangxi while the Mongol fleet under Omar came upriver, converging at Van Kiep and taking Thang Long within a month. Simultaneously the Mongol general Sogetu, having failed to subdue Champa, marched north through Nghe An and Thanh Hoa. Facing encirclement between Togan's and Sogetu's forces, the two Tran kings and their commanders evacuated by sea to the Ba Che River and then Bach Dang, drawing Mongol pursuit before doubling back by sea to Thanh Hoa to regroup. Several senior Tran figures (Tran Kien, Tran Ich Tac) defected to the Mongols during this crisis. From Thanh Hoa the Tran launched a rapid counteroffensive up the Red River, winning the battles of Tay Ket, Ham Tu, and Chuong Duong to retake Thang Long; Togan withdrew north through Lang Son under constant ambush, and Sogetu was cornered, captured, and killed at Tay Ket while trying to rejoin Togan.

**Third invasion (1287–88).** Togan again crossed into Lang Son to rendezvous with Omar's fleet at Van Kiep. Tran Khanh Du, commanding the coastal port of Van Don, could not stop the main Mongol war fleet but destroyed its supply fleet days later, dooming the campaign logistically. After Tran forces besieged Togan and Omar at Van Kiep and starved them into retreat, Omar's fleet was ambushed and annihilated by Tran Quoc Tuan at the [[battle-of-bach-dang-1288|Bach Dang estuary]] (1288); Togan again escaped overland through Lang Son. Kublai continued planning further invasions until his death in 1294, but none were launched, diverted by campaigns in Burma and Java and by his followers' war-weariness.

## Causal Analysis

The wars were **caused_by** Mongol/Yuan imperial strategy treating Dai Viet first as a transit corridor to Southern Song (1257) and then as a target for direct submission once Song fell (1279 onward). Tran refusal to grant passage to Mongol forces bound for [[champa|Champa]] in 1281 **contributed_to** the outbreak of the second invasion. The failure of the second invasion **caused_by** superior Tran use of strategic withdrawal, harassment of supply lines, and coordinated counterattack **contributed_to** Kublai's vengeful decision to launch a third invasion in 1287.

## Consequence Analysis

Tran victory across all three invasions **produced** the enduring independence of Dai Viet from Yuan rule and cemented Tran dynastic prestige for a generation, evident in the honors and land grants distributed to veteran commanders after 1288. However, the wars also **produced** severe socio-economic strain: the 1290–92 famine, land dispossession, and a documented rise in self-enslavement and large slave-worked estates. This economic dislocation **contributed_to** the longer-term social changes (aristocratic estate consolidation, slave-labor economy) that shaped fourteenth-century Tran decline. The wars are **analogous_to** the earlier [[ly-song-war-1075-1077|Ly–Song War]] in their reliance on strategic withdrawal, river-line defense, and negotiated/forced settlement, but **contrasts_with** it in scale and the existential nature of the threat (imperial conquest rather than border dispute).

## Historiography

Taylor's account draws heavily on Tran-court chronicle tradition (Dai Viet Su Ky Toan Thu), which preserves vivid individual anecdotes (the "ate the mangos" line, Tran Thu Do's "my head has not yet fallen to the ground") that carry didactic and morale-building coloring; Taylor treats the broad strategic narrative (repeated evacuation-and-counterattack pattern, the decisive role of supply-line destruction) as reliable while flagging some material — notably defection counts and casualty figures — as impossible to verify independently. The wars became foundational to a later Vietnamese historiographical tradition (beginning with Le Van Huu's 1272 history) asserting a distinct southern imperial mandate tracing to Zhao To, explicitly framed against Yuan universalist claims once the Song, Thang Long's longtime imperial reference point, had fallen.
