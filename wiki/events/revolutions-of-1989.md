# The Revolutions of 1989

The **Revolutions of 1989** were the largely peaceful collapse, within a single year, of the
communist regimes of Eastern Europe — Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia,
Bulgaria, and (violently) Romania. They were, in Jacques Lévesque's words in
[[leffler-westad-cold-war-v3|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. III]], "the single
most significant event leading to the end of the [[cold-war|Cold War]]." From the Cold War's
final act.

## The decisive factor: Soviet tolerance

Lévesque's central argument is striking: "it is not so much what happened in Eastern Europe
itself in 1989 that was historically significant" — the fragility of the regimes had long
been evident — but **Soviet tolerance for change**. Since the crushing of the
[[hungarian-revolution-1956|Hungarian Revolution (1956)]] and the Prague Spring (1968), two
thresholds were assumed to trigger Soviet tanks: ending the Communist Party's monopoly, and
leaving the Warsaw Pact. **Mikhail Gorbachev** abandoned the **Brezhnev Doctrine** — signaling
that Moscow would **not** use force to hold the bloc — and the revolutions' "pace and scale
were largely shaped by the gradual discovery of the scope of Soviet tolerance."

## Narrative

- **Poland** led: the independent trade union **Solidarity** (born in the Gdańsk shipyard,
  1980, under Lech Wałęsa) — the first such in the communist world — survived martial law and,
  via round-table talks, won **semi-free elections in June 1989** that it swept, producing the
  bloc's first non-communist-led government.
- **Hungary** opened its border with Austria (cutting the first hole in the **Iron Curtain**)
  and moved to multiparty democracy.
- **East Germany**: mass protests and the exodus through Hungary culminated in the **fall of
  the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989** — the iconic image of the year.
- **Czechoslovakia's** "**Velvet Revolution**" peacefully ousted the regime (Václav Havel to
  the presidency); **Bulgaria's** fell from within; only **Romania's** Ceaușescu was
  overthrown violently (and executed, December 1989).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **end of communist rule in Eastern Europe** and the dissolution of the
  **Warsaw Pact** (1991).
- `produced:` the **[[1991-end-of-cold-war|reunification of Germany]]** (1990) and, by
  demonstrating the hollowness of Soviet power, accelerated the **collapse of the USSR**
  itself (1991).
- A foundational moment of the [[contemporary|post-Cold-War]] order and the "third wave" of
  democratization.

## Historiography

The events are a touchstone in debates over the Cold War's end: how much credit belongs to
Western pressure (Reagan's arms build-up), to Gorbachev's choices, to the Helsinki
human-rights process and civil-society movements, and to the structural exhaustion of the
communist economies. Lévesque's emphasis on **Gorbachev's restraint** is now central.
