# The Battle of Himera (480 BCE)

At **Himera** on the north coast of Sicily, the combined armies of
**[[gelon|Gelon of Syracuse]]** and **Theron of Acragas** destroyed a great
**[[carthage|Carthaginian]]** expeditionary force under the Magonid **Hamilcar** —
the western counterpart of the Greek victories over Persia, fought (Greek tradition
held) on the very day of **[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]** (or, for others,
[[battle-of-thermopylae-480bce|Thermopylae]]). [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]]
(Ch. 16, David Asheri, *Carthaginians and Greeks*) treats it as the climax of the
volume's western half.

## Not a clash of civilizations

Asheri is emphatic that the period down to Himera was **not** a Greek–Carthaginian
struggle for Sicily but **continuous war between Greeks**, with the Carthaginians
rarely taking sides. Sixth-century Sicily was a country of "manifold ethnic
elements, all at some stage of levelling, uniforming **hellenization**" — Greeks,
Sicels, Sicans, Elymians and Punics in constant cultural osmosis. Greek parties
"had no qualms about allying themselves with Punics or Sicels against other Greeks":
Himera and Selinus were under **pro-Punic governments**, and Selinuntian cavalry
fought *for* Carthage in the battle.

## The collision of two blocs

The war grew out of Sicilian power-politics. **[[gelon|Gelon]]**, the Deinomenid
who had made himself master of [[syracuse|Syracuse]] (485) and most of Greek Sicily,
allied with the **Emmenid Theron of Acragas** (sealed by intermarriage). When Theron
**expelled Terillus, the tyrant of Himera** (c. 482), and annexed the city, the
exiled Terillus appealed to his **guest-friend Hamilcar**, the Magonid ruler of
Carthage; **Anaxilas of Rhegium** (Terillus' son-in-law) joined Carthage, giving his
children as hostages. The "very delicate balance of power" broke, and both sides
prepared for a decisive conflict. In 481 Gelon had **rebuffed the mainland Greeks'**
appeal for help against Xerxes (demanding supreme command as an impossible
condition) and even sent Cadmus to offer Xerxes earth and water should Persia win —
his real enemy was at home.

## The battle

Hamilcar's army — its rank-and-file a **multi-racial mercenary force** of
phoenicized Libyans, Iberians, Ligurians, Sardinians, Corsicans and Selinuntian
Greeks (Herodotus' figure of 300,000 is as incredible as his millions for Xerxes) —
sailed via Panormus to Himera and pitched two camps, one for the army and one for
the fleet. Our only narrative, **Diodorus** (following Ephorus), is "very unreliable
and rhetorical." After an initial Carthaginian success, Theron summoned Gelon, who
came up with a large force; his **cavalry** roamed the countryside taking prisoners,
then — using **intercepted letters** from Hamilcar to his Selinuntian allies as a
ruse — rode into the Carthaginian naval camp, **fired the ships and killed Hamilcar**
(who, by the Carthaginian account Herodotus knew, cast himself into his sacrificial
pyre when he saw the rout, becoming an object of **heroic cult** at Carthage). The
main battle was then won on the banks of the Fiume Torto; the Carthaginian army was
said to be almost annihilated.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: **Theron's seizure of Himera** and expulsion of Terillus; Terillus'
appeal to his Magonid guest-friend **Hamilcar**.
contributed_to: the **bloc rivalry** of the Syracusan-Acragantine alliance versus the
pro-Punic cities (Himera, Selinus, Rhegium) that Carthage worked to keep divided.
This was *not* caused by any concerted Perso-Punic plan against Hellenism — Asheri
treats that synchronized-onslaught story as later **panhellenic propaganda**.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: a **moderate peace** — Carthage paid a 2,000-talent indemnity and built two
temples (one perhaps the "Tempio della Vittoria" at Buonfornello) to hold copies of
the treaty; she **kept all her Sicilian possessions**. The much-cited clause banning
human sacrifice is probably tendentious (the custom continued).
enabled: the apogee of **[[gelon|Gelon's]] Deinomenid hegemony** and the spectacular
florescence of **[[syracuse|Syracuse]] and Acragas** (temples, the "Damareteion"
coinage, Pindar's odes). For Carthage, defeat "was not a catastrophe": she
**withdrew into Africa** for seventy years, phoenicizing the Libyans and launching
the Atlantic voyages — "defeat may sometimes regenerate the vanquished."

## Historiography

The sources are a problem: **Herodotus** (VII.165–7) frames Himera narrowly as
Hamilcar's duty to restore his guest-friend; **Diodorus/Ephorus** supplies an
unreliable, rhetorical battle-narrative; and **Pindar** (Pyth. 1.72–80, naming
Salamis, Plataea, Himera and Cyme in one breath) shows how fast Gelon's court turned
the victory into **myth**. The **Himera–Salamis synchronism** — already current in
Herodotus' Sicily — was disputed in antiquity: Aristotle (Poetics) called it merely
**accidental**, while a "less rationalistic mind" read it as Divine Providence
arranging "the fairest victory and the most glorious defeat" on one day. Asheri reads
the synchronism as a deliberate vehicle for **panhellenic propaganda**. A sober
corrective the chapter insists on: more than two generations before any Carthaginian
army destroyed a Greek city in Sicily, **Gelon himself had annihilated three**
(Camarina, Megara Hyblaea, Euboea).

## Miles: a Magonid private war, retrospectively mythologised

Richard Miles ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) reinforces this page's
source-critical reading and sharpens one point: the 480 expedition "remained a private
enterprise, underwritten by the Magonids rather than by the Carthaginian state." The
Himera-as-western-Salamis synchronism, on his account, was Syracusan propaganda
(Deinomenids → Antiochus, Philistus, **Timaeus**) that conveniently masked **Gelon's own
refusal to aid mainland Greece** and his hedging embassy to Persia — and seeded the longer
[[punic-faith|construction of Carthage as the barbarian Orient in the West]]. The defeat's
chief domestic legacy was the early-5th-century Carthaginian **constitutional reform** (the
Tribunal of One Hundred and Four, suffeture, Popular Assembly); see
[[carthage-syracuse-rivalry]].
