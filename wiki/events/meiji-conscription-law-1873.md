## Narrative

On January 10, 1873, the Meiji government officially promulgated a law establishing universal military conscription. All men who had reached their twentieth year and were in good physical condition would be conscripted into the army and navy for active service followed by reserve duty. The law had been decided upon toward the end of 1872 and the promulgation formalized that decision.

The law had been anticipated by earlier reformers. Ōmura Masujirō (1824–1869), the "father of the Japanese army," had advocated the conscription of commoners during the early Meiji period, "a development that enraged samurai who believed it threatened their special, privileged position." His advocacy earned him enemies who eventually assassinated him in 1869. After Ōmura's death, Yamagata Aritomo carried the project forward and was the chief architect of the 1873 law.

### The "Blood Tax" Misunderstanding and Popular Revolt

The proclamation announcing conscription employed the term *ketsuzei* (literally "blood tax") as a circumlocution for military service. Among peasants in the Hōjō region, this term was catastrophically misunderstood: they interpreted it to mean that their blood would literally be extracted by the government. The misunderstanding was intensified by rumors of sightings of white-garmented medical personnel. More than 3,000 men began rampaging through the countryside. Their first target was an eta (outcast) village that they burned to the ground; anger was also voiced over "paying taxes for schools, cutting hair in the Western style, and slaughtering cattle." Keene notes that "although a misunderstanding over military service was the direct cause of the uprising, it was essentially an expression of resentment of the changes decreed by the government in its efforts to achieve modernization."

The government suppressed this and subsequent uprisings (in Hokkaidō and a much larger one in Fukuoka Prefecture, where up to 300,000 participants allegedly joined protests against rice merchants), but the revolts signaled a deep social fracture: the pace of Meiji transformation was outrunning popular comprehension.

### Significance for the Samurai Class

Conscription struck at the identity of the samurai class more directly than almost any other Meiji reform. For centuries the samurai had possessed a monopoly on the bearing of arms and the practice of war; their social status and economic stipends rested on this monopoly. A conscript army of commoners rendered them obsolete as a military class. Combined with the abolition of the domains ([[abolition-of-han-1871]]), the impending end of samurai stipends, and the permission (later mandate) to cut their topknots and discard their swords, conscription was one of several blows that stripped the samurai of the institutional foundations of their identity.

The samurai discontent thus generated would find political expression in Saigō Takamori's advocacy of the Korean expedition in 1873 — which he himself acknowledged as partly a means of providing discontented samurai with a foreign war as an outlet — and ultimately in the Saga Rebellion of 1874 and the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877.

## Causal Analysis

Caused_by: [[meiji-restoration-1868]] — the new government's goal of making Japan a modern state capable of defending itself required a national army not dependent on domain forces.

Contributed_to: the precedent set by Ōmura Masujirō and observations of European conscript armies, particularly the Prussian army, which had demonstrated in 1870–71 that mass conscription produced devastating military effectiveness.

Preceded_by: [[abolition-of-han-1871]] — the dissolution of domain armies made conscription both necessary and administratively possible; without prefectural administration, a national draft was unenforceable.

## Consequence Analysis

Produced: a national army that would within decades evolve into the force that defeated China (1895) and Russia (1905).

Contributed_to: the delegitimization and eventual abolition of samurai class privileges.

Enabled: the Saga Rebellion of 1874 and Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, as samurai channeled their resentment into armed resistance.

Part_of: [[meiji-reforms]] — the conscription law was one pillar of the broader Meiji modernization program alongside land-tax reform and compulsory education.

## Historiography

Keene emphasizes the popular reaction to conscription — particularly the "blood tax" misunderstanding — as evidence that government reformers had moved faster than popular culture could absorb. The episode illustrates the gap between the oligarchs' vision of Japan as a modern state and the lived experience of peasants who had no framework for understanding national military service. The Cambridge History of Japan volumes confirm that conscription, together with haihan chiken, constituted the two most structurally significant changes of the early Meiji decade.
