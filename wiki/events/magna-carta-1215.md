# Magna Carta (1215)

**Magna Carta** ("the Great Charter") was the settlement that the rebel barons of England
forced on **[[john-of-england|King John]]** at Runnymede on 15 June 1215. It failed as an
immediate peace but, through its **reissues** of 1216–1225, became the foundation document
of the English constitutional tradition. [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (D.A. Carpenter,
Ch. 12) treats it as the central event of thirteenth-century English politics.

## Narrative

The Charter had sixty-one clauses. Their main thrust was to **limit the "money-getting"
activities of the king**, make royal justice more equitable and available, correct the
abuses of local agents, and assert the fundamental principle that the king could not act
arbitrarily "by will" (*per voluntatem*) but was **subject to the law**. That principle was
encapsulated in the famous **chapter 39** (still on the statute book in the twentieth
century): no freeman was to be deprived of property, outlawed, imprisoned, exiled or
otherwise proceeded against "save by lawful judgement of his peers or by the law of the
land." The early chapters benefited the barons (limiting reliefs to £100, restricting
wardships, regulating scutage); but others promised freedom to the **Church**, safeguarded
**London**, gave **knights** a role in local justice, and limited judicial fines for **all
freemen, even unfree peasants**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **John's intensified exploitation** of England after the loss of Normandy
  (1204) threw him back on English resources; his ordinary revenue rose by over a third in
  real terms, much of it extorted from individual barons (reliefs, proffers for justice,
  Jewish debts) and "miserable provincials" in the shires. 1215 was "a rebellion of the
  king's debtors" (Holt), strongest in the north.
- `contributed_to:` John's **lawless acts** (the murders of Arthur and the Braose family,
  dispossession "by the king's will") went "far beyond what seemed customary and
  acceptable" — at a time when ideas like John of Salisbury's distinction between the just
  ruler and the tyrant (*Policraticus*) were "gaining increasing bite."
- `enabled:` the **widening political community** — barons, an expanding gentry of county
  knights, burgesses, the Church — could now co-operate to force a *general* charter
  (unlike the individual charters of Stephen's reign), reflecting ties of faction, common
  grievance, and (possibly) the idealism of Archbishop **Stephen Langton**.

## Consequence Analysis

In the short term the Charter **failed utterly**: a negotiated document rather than one
dictated to John on his knees, it left him his army and castles, making the enforcement
clause (a committee of twenty-five barons) impossible. John had Innocent III quash it (24
August 1215), and civil war resumed.

But Magna Carta **took root in the minority of [[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]]**.
To win the war and secure the great tax that saved Gascony, Henry's regents performed an
"astonishing volte face," reissuing the Charter in **1216, 1217, and definitively 1225**
(the 1217 reissue paired with a separate **Charter of the Forest**). Copies were sent to
the counties, proclaimed in the county courts, and copied into private notebooks. Key
clauses (the £100 relief, protection of widows, the ban on selling justice) were "largely
obeyed, thus reducing the king's revenue and his political power." The Charter's principle
— that **the king is subject to the law** — was reinforced when even Henry III's bouts of
"plenitude of power" rhetoric collapsed in political crisis. It became the rallying point
of all later reform, including the [[english-parliament|baronial reforms of 1258]].

## Historiography

J.C. Holt's studies are foundational. Debate centres on whether the Charter "restored old
law" or **imposed new** (in many areas, like the size of relief, "there was no precise law
and custom for John to break"); on how far the gentry, not just the magnates, drove it; and
on Langton's role. Carpenter stresses that its later force came less from 1215 than from the
**minority reissues**, when the crown itself was the guarantor.
