# The Great Persecution

The **Great Persecution** (303–313) was the **last and fiercest** state persecution of Christians in
the Roman Empire, launched by [[diocletian|Diocletian]] and the [[roman-tetrarchy|tetrarchy]]. Treated
in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 3 (**Alan Bowman**); the third-century persecutions and their
aftermath are covered more fully in Ch. 18b (Cycle 6).

## Narrative

Bowman cautions that the image of a "sudden and violent outburst" is partly a rhetorical construct of
the pro-Christian sources (**Lactantius, Eusebius**). There had been forewarning: enforced sacrifice
at court and in the army from the later 290s, and the **Epistle against the Manichees** (302). The
persecution proper unfolded in a **series of edicts** from **23/24 February 303** (the Terminalia, at
Nicomedia), reportedly after Galerius pressed Diocletian and the oracle of Apollo at Didyma was
consulted:

1. **First edict** (303): razing of churches, surrender and burning of scriptures, loss of civil
   rights.
2. **Second edict** (303): arrest of clergy.
3. **Third edict** (303): clergy who sacrificed to be freed — an amnesty tied to Diocletian's
   *vicennalia*.
4. **Fourth edict** (304): universal sacrifice demanded.

Enforcement was **highly uneven**: severe in the East (especially under **Galerius** and, later,
**Maximinus**), but in the West under **Constantius** "little or no action" was taken.

## Causal Analysis

Lactantius assigns the chief blame to **Galerius**, casting Diocletian as a reluctant senior emperor
won over in private discussion (a verbatim scene Bowman judges fictional). Structurally, the
persecution fits the tetrarchy's **traditionalist, *romanitas*-centered** ideology — the demand for
religious unity and divine favor, of a piece with the Edict on Marriages and the anti-Manichaean
measures — and the long association (since [[third-century-crisis|the crisis]]) of Christian refusal
to sacrifice with **disloyalty**.

## Consequence Analysis

The persecution **failed** to eradicate the church and left deep scars (the later **Donatist** schism
in Africa grew from disputes over those who had lapsed). It was called off in the East by **Galerius'
deathbed edict of toleration (311)**, which provided the precedent for the
[[edict-of-milan-313|toleration of 313]]; **Maximinus** briefly renewed it before his defeat. The net
effect was to leave Christianity "greatly strengthened" and to set the stage for the Constantinian
turn.

## Significance

The last great persecution proved the **limits of coercion**: a generation after it began, the empire
had a Christian emperor. It is the dark prelude to the [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]]
of the Roman world.

## Historiography

### From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Mitchell situates the 303 outlawing of Christianity within tetrarchic religious temper (Jupiter/Hercules ideology; demonizing of foreign sects as in the ~297 Manichee letter). Lactantius’s haruspices anecdote and Galerius/mother influence lead to the **23 Feb 303 Terminalia** edict from Nicomedia: churches destroyed, scriptures burned, Christians stripped of rank and legally downgraded — terms resembling the Manichee outlawing; initial effects relatively limited outside zealous localities.

Everything depended on local and imperial zeal: Christian tradition is unanimous that **Constantius** in Trier did little (Lactantius: some church destruction; Eusebius denies even that); almost no western persecution outside Africa; **Galerius** reluctantly rescinded the edict on the eve of his death (May **311**), after similar actions by Constantine (as early as 306) and Maxentius (~308).

Broader reframe of pre-Constantinian church–state conflict: persecution/martyrdom as Christian “rite of passage” narrative is **misleading** — state persecution episodes were relatively short; both Christians and pagans were powerful enough that rulers could not lightly alienate either. Christianity was illegal as such (Pliny–Trajan: crime of being Christian; sacrifice test as practical proof of apostasy). Numbers before 200 small; Rome 251: 155 clergy + 1,500 widows on charity. Decius 249 universal sacrifice; Valerian 257; Gallienus restored churches/cemeteries. By 300 Christians in Senate, entourage, army — Barnes: no emperor secure without Christian acquiescence. Tetrarchic persecution 303–312 as shock tied to reinvented Olympian religion; enforcement uneven.
