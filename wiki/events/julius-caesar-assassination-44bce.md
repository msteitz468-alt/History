# The Assassination of Julius Caesar (15 March 44 BCE)

The murder of **[[julius-caesar|Caesar]]** by a conspiracy of senators who hoped to
restore the Republic — and instead opened a new round of civil war that ended it for
good. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs. 11–12 (Rawson).

## The conspiracy and the murder

Some **sixty senators** conspired, led by the praetors **M. Brutus** (Cato's nephew,
who claimed descent from the tyrant-slaying L. Brutus) and **C. Cassius** — a mix of
**pardoned ex-Pompeians** and disillusioned **Caesarians** (D. Brutus, Trebonius).
The trigger was Caesar's **[[caesars-dictatorship|dictatorship *perpetuo*]]** and the
swelling **honours and rumours of kingship** (the Lupercalia diadem, the divine
*flamen* Antony). Resolving to kill him "as a tyrant" in full Senate (like Romulus),
they struck on the **Ides of March**, in the **Curia of Pompey** — Caesar fell at the
**foot of Pompey's statue**. Brutus called out Cicero's name, "congratulating him on
the recovery of liberty."

## The failure of the liberation

The "liberators" had **no plan beyond the murder** (Cicero: "the spirit was as manly
as the plans were childish"). The Senate fled; the conspirators got no enthusiasm
from the people and occupied the **Capitol**. The surviving consul **Antony** seized
Caesar's papers and treasure; at the **Liberalia (17 March)** he engineered a
**compromise** — amnesty for the assassins, but Caesar's *acta*, will, and **public
funeral** confirmed. Antony's funeral oration and the reading of Caesar's **will**
(legacies to the plebs) turned the crowd to fury; the conspirators were driven from
Rome.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: Caesar's **autocracy and the perpetual dictatorship** — *regnum*,
overweening one-man power — affronting the senatorial class's attachment to
*libertas*; the honours and the kingship rumours brought matters to a head. Rawson
notes many conspirators acted from **genuine Republican principle**, not merely
private grudge.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: **not** the restoration of the Republic but a power vacuum filled by Caesar's
heirs.
enabled: the rise of the nineteen-year-old **[[second-triumvirate|Octavian]]** (named
heir in Caesar's will) and **Antony**; the **war of Mutina** (43); the
**[[second-triumvirate|Second Triumvirate]]** and the **proscriptions** (the death of
**[[cicero]]**); and the war against the liberators ending at **Philippi** (42, CAH
X). Rawson: "Caesar's ghost" destroyed the Republic the conspirators meant to save.

## Historiography

The two ancient verdicts — the assassins as **sacrilegious parricides** (forsworn,
killers of the *parens patriae* and a god) or as **tyrannicides/"liberators"** — were
forming at once. Cicero's **letters** and the **Philippics**, with the later Appian,
Dio, Plutarch, and Suetonius, are the sources; Cicero himself, hailed by Brutus at
the murder, was not in the plot.
