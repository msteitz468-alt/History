# The Assyrian Sack of Thebes (c. 663 BCE)

The **sack of Thebes** — Egyptian **No-Amon**, the southern capital and cult-city
of Amun — was the **high-water mark of [[neo-assyrian-empire|Assyrian]]
expansion**, the deepest penetration of the empire's reach and the event that
ended **[[kush-meroe|Kushite]]** rule in Egypt. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol.
III, Part 2]] (Ch. 24, Grayson; Ch. 35, James) covers it.

## Narrative

[[esarhaddon|Esarhaddon's]] conquest of Memphis (671) had not subdued Egypt. After
his death, **Taharqa** reoccupied Memphis; [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]]'s army
drove him back and **recaptured Memphis (667)**. When Taharqa's nephew
**Tantamani** marched north and retook Memphis, killing the Assyrian client
**Necho I** in battle, the Assyrians invaded a third time. Tantamani abandoned
Memphis and fled to Thebes; the Assyrians pursued, **captured Thebes and looted
it** (c. 663), carrying off vast spoil — including, by tradition, two obelisks.
Tantamani fled south into Nubia; the Kushites never returned.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the persistent [[kush-meroe|Kushite]] effort to expel Assyria from
Egypt and the revolt of Delta princes who had sided with Tantamani.
`enabled:` by [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's]] freedom of action once Egypt's
northern approaches were secured.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **definitive end of Kushite (25th-Dynasty) power** in Egypt;
Kush withdrew to Napata and later Meroë (see [[kush-meroe]]).
`produced:` the elevation of **[[saite-egypt|Psammetichus I of Sais]]**, son of
Necho I, as Assyrian client — the founder of the **Saite 26th Dynasty**, who would
soon reunify Egypt and shake off Assyrian control as the empire weakened.
`produced:` a lasting cultural memory: the biblical prophet **Nahum (3:8–10)**
invokes the fall of No-Amon as the type of total ruin, warning [[nineveh|Nineveh]]
of its own coming fate — a striking historical irony, since Nineveh fell within
fifty years.

## Historiography

Dated "c. 663" because Ashurbanipal's annals number campaigns out of chronological
order; the synthesis of Assyrian and Egyptian evidence is set out by Spalinger and
in CAH III.2 Ch. 35 (James). The event marks both the **maximum extent** of the
Assyrian empire and, in retrospect, the point past which it could only contract —
Egypt was effectively independent again within two decades.
