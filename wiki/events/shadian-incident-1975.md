# Shadian Incident (1975)

A military assault by the People's Liberation Army on Hui Muslim villages in Shadian
(Gejiu, Yunnan) in July 1975, near the end of the Cultural Revolution, that killed
**hundreds of Hui civilians** — one of the deadliest episodes of state violence
against Muslims in the People's Republic of China.

## Narrative

During the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), the Maoist state treated religion as
"feudal superstition" and foreign infiltration; mosques were closed, Qur'ans burned,
and religious practice criminalized. When Shadian's Hui community pressed to reopen
their mosque and resume worship, the confrontation escalated. In July 1975, per
Formichi, "thousands of Chinese soldiers led a military attack on several villages in
Shadian (Yunnan), killing hundreds of local Muslims belonging to the Hui ethnic
minority group." Much of the settlement was destroyed.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[cultural-revolution]] — the campaign against religion that
  criminalized Hui worship (temporal-and-causal antecedent).
- `contributed_to:` the specific local trigger — the community's insistence on
  reopening the mosque against state prohibition.
- The episode sits within the longer tension, traced in [[islam-in-china]], between a
  centralizing Chinese state and the distinct religious identity of the [[uyghurs|Hui
  and Turkic Muslim]] populations.

## Consequence Analysis

- After Mao's death, the reform state under Deng Xiaoping formally **rehabilitated**
  the victims and acknowledged the killings as a wrong of the Cultural Revolution —
  an unusual official reversal.
- The rehabilitation helped catalyze the post-1979 [[islamic-revival-late-20th-century|Islamic
  revival]] in China: the Bureau of Religious Affairs reopened (1979), mosques were
  rebuilt (by the 1980s arguably more mosques than before the Cultural Revolution),
  and the hajj resumed.

## Actors

[[islam-in-china|Hui Muslims]] · People's Liberation Army · the Maoist party-state.

## Historiography

Formichi treats Shadian as emblematic of the swing from Cultural-Revolution
repression to reform-era accommodation of Islam in China. The event is documented
chiefly through post-Mao Chinese acknowledgements and later scholarship; casualty
figures ("hundreds") are approximate. It is a rare case of the PRC officially
conceding state atrocity, which shapes how it is remembered.
