# Expulsion of the Germans from Eastern Europe (1945–1950)

The flight and forced expulsion of some 12–14 million ethnic Germans
from Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary and the former German East at the
end of the Second World War — the largest forced population movement in
European history.

## Narrative

Between twelve and fourteen million ethnic Germans were forcibly
expelled from Eastern Europe at the end of the war, or prevented from
returning if they had fled — "the largest action of what later came to
be known as 'ethnic cleansing' in history" (Evans, following Douglas).
Probably half a million and perhaps as many as a million died.

The expulsions were not spontaneous mass revenge: they were **ordered by
the Allies and planned long before the war ended**, growing out of the
perceived failure of the interwar minority-protection regime. Beneš
persuaded the Western Allies that Czechoslovakia's three million
Sudeten Germans would be "a million or more 'young, incorrigible
Nazis'"; sympathy hardened after the Lidice massacre (1942), and by
mid-1942 Britain had accepted the transfer principle. The decision to
compensate Poland with German territory up to the Oder–Neisse followed
Stalin's retention of eastern Poland; Potsdam (July 1945) ratified a
fait accompli and sanctioned further expulsions only if "orderly and
humane."

Phases: (1) flight before the Red Army (rape, looting, killing of
German civilians), 1944–45; (2) the "wild expulsions" of spring–summer
1945, in reality — Douglas's central claim — centrally planned and
directed by troops, police and militia rather than spontaneous (Brno:
at least 300 killed at Kaunitz College, public torture-killings, 28,000
death-marched to the Austrian border; reprisals were markedly rarer in
Poland despite the harsher occupation); (3) internment — ethnic Germans
held in camps including former Nazi sites (Majdanek, Theresienstadt,
and Auschwitz itself, reused within two weeks of liberation), ~250,000
in Czechoslovak camps alone, with atrocious conditions, forced labor,
sexual violence Douglas documents as exceeding that of the German camps
in the Czech and Polish cases, and ~6,500 dead at Lambinowice; (4) the
"organized expulsions" of 1946–47 under the 20 November 1945 Allied
allocation (2.75m to the Soviet zone, 2.25m to the American, 1.5m to
the British, 150,000 to the French), which nevertheless "defied the
efforts of the countries involved to impose any kind of order."

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: demographic homogenization of East-Central Europe; "whole
  villages without an inhabitant" in the Sudetenland, agricultural
  dereliction, looting and banditry in the emptied zones; enrichment of
  those who (as *The Economist* noted in 1946) profited "by looting the
  property first of murdered Jews and then of expelled Germans."
- enabled: paradoxically rapid integration of expellees into West
  Germany — Adenauer's Lastenausgleich compensation tax, an expellee
  ministry, social-insurance eligibility, and the economic miracle
  brought expellee unemployment to near the West German average by the
  early 1960s, defusing the revanchist potential of the expellee league.
- Cold War reframing: as anti-Communism took hold, expellees were
  redefined from potential "fifth columnists" to "oppressed victims of
  a Godless dictatorship."
- Douglas's conclusion (endorsed by Evans, attributed): "Expulsions are
  not practicable unless they are carried out quickly; and if they are
  carried out quickly, they cannot be carried out humanely." Evans
  draws the wider lesson that Versailles-style national
  self-determination, applied to Central Europe's intermixed
  populations, led via minority grievance and Nazi exploitation of it
  ([[occupation-of-czecho-slovakia-1939|Sudeten crisis]]) to thirty
  years of suffering.

## Historiography

Long dominated in the Federal Republic by expellee documentation
projects and largely ignored in Anglophone scholarship until R. M.
Douglas's *Orderly and Humane* (2012); politically charged between
German victimhood narratives and East European justifications.

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] give a somewhat different
aggregate framing worth preserving alongside Evans/Douglas above: as many as **9
million** Germans left Czechoslovakia and Poland alone in the immediate postwar years
(the Sudetenland expulsion plus Poland's newly annexed western territories, 1946–47) —
consistent with, though narrower in geographic scope than, the 12–14 million figure
given above for the full expellee population. They situate this within a much larger
regional churn: **total Eastern European population displacement 1944–1950 reached
roughly 31 million people, about one-third of the region's population**, including
returning POWs and forced laborers and Jewish survivors fleeing renewed antisemitic
violence, not only ethnic Germans. They also corroborate and specify the mass-rape
figures given above from Evans: an estimated **1–2 million German women raped in spring
1945** (up to one-third of Berlin's women) — publicly condoned by Stalin and never
punished, consistent with the 1.4 million/100,000-in-Berlin figures already documented.

## The wartime flight — Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

The trilogy's final volume supplies the 1944–45 flight phase in detail (recorded at full fidelity). Soviet troops crossed into Germany carrying the memory of 1941–44 and the fresh discovery of Majdanek and [[auschwitz]] — "Fire for fire, blood for blood, death for death," in a soldier's letter. **Mass rape** followed: an estimated 1.4 million women across East Prussia, Pomerania, and Silesia, gang-rape "the norm rather than the exception"; Berlin's two largest hospitals estimated at least 100,000 victims in the capital; rape was often staged before husbands and fathers, interveners killed (the diary later published as *A Woman in Berlin* records the survivors' gallows humor). By mid-February 1945 the Nazi authorities themselves counted ~8 million Germans fleeing west — columns Evans parallels to the refugee treks the Wehrmacht had created from Belgium to Belarus, "only this time they consisted of Germans"; Gauleiters who forbade timely evacuation left civilians to trek through −20° cold, one train reaching Schleswig-Holstein "riddled with lice," with "many dead lying in the wagons." The liner **Wilhelm Gustloff**, evacuating ~6,600 from Gdynia, was torpedoed on 30 January 1945 with ~5,300 dead — among history's deadliest sinkings. Soviet occupation stripped the zone industrially (80% of Berlin's machinery removed) and culturally (the Sistine Madonna; the Pergamon Altar). Evans's total: "perhaps 11 million German refugees and expellees arrived in the 'Old Reich' between 1944 and 1947." Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]
