# The Vietnam War (the Indochina Wars, 1945–1975)

The **Indochina wars** were a thirty-year struggle for Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia that, in
Fredrik Logevall's words in [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|*The Cambridge History of the Cold
War*, Vol. II]], "occupies a central place in the international history of the twentieth
century" — capturing "in microcosm all of the grand political forces" of the age:
**colonialism, nationalism, communism, and democratic-capitalism**. Both an **East–West**
([[cold-war|Cold War]]) and a **North–South** ([[decolonization]]) conflict, it became the
era's great hot war and the most damaging US defeat of the Cold War.

## Nagl's Organizational Culture Analysis (pre-campaign, Ch. 1 & Intro)

Nagl (building directly on [[krepinevich-the-army-and-vietnam-1986]] and [[army-concept]]) argues the U.S. Army's failure to develop successful counterinsurgency doctrine "is best explained by the differing organizational cultures of the two armies; in short, that the British army was a learning institution and the American army was not." "The United States Army resisted any true attempt to learn how to fight an insurgency during the course of the Vietnam War, preferring to treat the war as a conventional conflict in the tradition of the Korean War and World War II." "It was as a result of this failure to become a 'learning institution' that the American army did not learn how to fight a counterinsurgency war in Vietnam, and that the British Advisory Mission to Vietnam was ineffective in passing on counterinsurgency lessons from the recent British success in Malaya." Pre-existing American culture ("essence" = ground combat by regular divisional units; preference for conventional mid-intensity war and firepower substitution) produced the Army Concept rigidity. See [[nagl-learning-to-eat-soup-with-a-knife-2005]] (Ch. 1–2 theoretical framework and Question Sets), [[learning-institution]], [[counterinsurgency]] (direct/Jominian approach), and Krepinevich for detailed implementation.

## Narrative

- **The First Indochina War (1946–54).** After the Japanese collapse, the Communist-led
  **Vietminh** under **Ho Chi Minh** proclaimed independence (1945), but France sought to
  restore colonial rule; large-scale war broke out in late 1946. The conflict was at first a
  **Franco-Vietnamese** affair (Stalin "showed scant interest"), but French diplomacy
  reframed it as an East–West struggle, and after the Communist victory in China (1949) and
  the [[korean-war-1950|Korean War]], the **US bankrolled the French** under the logic of
  **[[containment]]** and the **domino theory**. France's defeat at **Dien Bien Phu (1954)**
  ended its empire in Indochina; the **Geneva Accords** partitioned Vietnam at the 17th
  parallel.
- **The Advisory Years (1954–1965).** The United States assumed primary responsibility for South Vietnam's security. MAAG (authorized 1950, ~342 advisers by 1954) oversaw transition from French influence via TRIM. Despite CIA warnings that "even if the U.S. defeated the Viet Minh field forces, guerrilla action could probably be continued indefinitely," the Army organized the ARVN around seven standard divisions modeled on WWII U.S. divisions for a conventional cross-border invasion threat. Light divisions were eliminated; paramilitary forces (Civil Guard ~53k, Self Defense Corps ~43k) were neglected or poorly supported. Training mirrored U.S. courses, emphasized large-unit maneuvers, and omitted language/cultural preparation. "It was easier for the Army to envision a Korea-type threat... than the insurgency threat." The NLF was declared in 1960; MAAG continued to describe VC as "remnants." Kennedy administration pressure for counterinsurgency (Flexible Response, NSAMs, SGCI, Special Forces expansion) produced rhetorical compliance and padded training statistics but little doctrinal or organizational change. "Any good soldier can handle guerrillas." The Strategic Hamlets program (1962–63), influenced by British advisor R. K. G. Thompson but executed with MAAG emphasis on sweeps, featured forced resettlement (e.g. Operation SUNRISE), falsified statistics (8,000 hamlets claimed, most shams), and alienation of the population. By late 1963 U.S. personnel exceeded 16,000. MACV was activated February 1962 under Lt. Gen. Paul Harkins (selected despite no COIN experience). Per Nagl, Harkins embodied the "traditional army 'can-do' spirit of professional optimism, even at the expense of reality": “I am optimistic, and I am not going to allow my staff to be pessimistic.” On napalm: “It really puts the fear of God into the Viet Cong. And that is what counts.” Neil Sheehan (A Bright Shining Lie): generals "had become so accustomed to winning from the later years of [WWII] that they could no longer imagine they could lose."

**Willbanks on advisory phases and structural mismatch (Ch. 1, exclusive range):** Five phases. Post-Geneva US built three-pillar system (Self-Defense Corps, Civil Guard, ARVN apex from VNA remnants ~150k 1954 with "complete breakdown of combat capabilities," politicized officers, corruption, desertions). "In the early years of the US training mission in Vietnam, there was some effort to train the South Vietnamese forces for civic action and pacification. However, over time as the insurgency gained momentum, less and less emphasis was given to the special aspects of counter-guerrilla warfare; the focus shifted to preparing the force for the worst threat scenario—guerrilla main force units and an external invasion from North Vietnam." "the ARVN was structured and trained to meet a possible external attack by the PAVN, not the actual struggle against the National Liberation Front." Korea precedent dominated; US doctrine imposed. Individual training succeeded (18 centers ~20k/yr + offshore); collective failed (political leaders, ops tempo, paramilitary under civilians forcing ARVN into internal security). Advisors (to battalion 1961+) ill-prepared (few COIN/lang/culture exp); "revolving door" short tours. Post-1965 US troops: training "relegated to 'a secondary endeavor'"; ARVN shunted aside. Vietnamization surge too late to fix leadership/corruption/dependence on US support. "conscious decision to form the South Vietnamese forces in the image of US forces" produced fatal logistics/firepower dependence by 1975. (See [[sources/willbanks-vietnam-the-course-of-a-conflict-2018]] Range 01.)ey could no longer imagine they could lose"; Korea rationalized as civilian fault; "they assumed that they would prevail in Vietnam simply because of who they were." "time and the bureaucratization of the officer corps had distorted the memory of how World War II had been won. Harkins’ strategy was a fantasy of the past, but the fantasy was real because it had been institutionalized." Created "chilling effect" demanding positive ARVN reports. 
- **The American War (c. 1965–1973).** The US backed an anti-communist South under Ngo Dinh
  Diem; faced with a southern insurgency (the **Viet Cong/NLF**) backed by the North, it
  escalated from advisers to a major war after the **Gulf of Tonkin** episode (1964), committing over half a million troops and a vast bombing campaign. Nagl (Ch. 7) documents how the Army's pre-existing concept of war — oriented to past conventional experience rather than “the realities of the situation faced” — precluded successful COIN doctrine throughout the fighting years. MACOV recognized need for “Area Type Warfare” (elusive enemy, contact from any direction) but doctrine stayed rooted in “experience gained during World War II and the Korean War.” Indiscriminate air/artillery use by U.S. and ARVN forces harmed population; RF/PF remained “poor stepchild” until post-Tet Vietnamization priority. Army retained corps/division organization despite CIDG/CAP small-unit successes. Figure 7-3 records failure on every COIN doctrine criterion (Victory, Objective, Unity of command, Minimum force, Mass/appropriate structure: all No). “In short, the U.S. Army’s concept of how to fight and win precluded the development of a successful counterinsurgency doctrine in South Vietnam.”
  committing over half a million troops and a vast bombing campaign. The **Tet Offensive**
  (1968) was a military defeat for the Communists (37,000+ body count by MACV, VCI mauled up to 30%) but a political disaster for the U.S. Army's approach: despite Westmoreland's pre-offensive promises of "turning the corner" in 1968, the attacks on 36 of 43 provincial capitals (plus Saigon and Hue) and the Khe Sanh diversion exposed the limits of search-and-destroy and produced a full civilian review. Wheeler reported the enemy "has the will and the capability to continue" with "RD program... suffered a severe setback"; no plan for military victory existed beyond "pour[ing] troops in... for an unknown period of time". The result was a frozen troop ceiling at 549,500 (no additional 200,000), Westmoreland recalled to CSA, partial bombing halt, and LBJ's withdrawal from the race. Tet was "a victory for Hanoi" in the dimensions that mattered for insurgency warfare: pacification setback, 800,000 new refugees, 1,001 U.S. KIA that eroded American will faster than MACV wore down the enemy. The Army viewed it as "the Battle of the Bulge" and required "no soul-searching". 

**Willbanks Range 02 (Tet turning point + Hue):** "In the first two days of the Tet Offensive, which began on 30 January 1968, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong troops attacked 39 of South Vietnam’s 44 provincial capitals, five of six largest cities to include Saigon, 71 of 242 district capitals..." "the Tet Offensive resulted in a great psychological victory that proved to be the turning point of the war..." "the worst intelligence failure of the war." MACV downgraded estimates (300k→235k), fixated on Khe Sanh as "main event"; >half US battalions to I Corps. Captured docs (TCK-TKN General Offensive-General Uprising, Ho Chi Minh order) dismissed as wishful or incompatible with preconceptions. Communist planners guilty of parallel "subjectivism": "set inappropriate, unreasonable goals"; "We had somewhat underestimated the capabilities and reactions of the enemy"; planned only for decisive victory, ignored branch plans for adversity. (See [[sources/willbanks-vietnam-the-course-of-a-conflict-2018]] Range 02.)

Nixon's policy of **"Vietnamization"** (RVNAF buildup to ~801k but leadership/corruption problems unaddressed) and the widening of the war into Cambodia and Laos preceded the **Paris Peace Accords (1973)** and US withdrawal. Post-Tet "one war" rhetoric under Abrams produced limited change; main-force units continued attrition-style operations (e.g., SPEEDY EXPRESS 10,883 claimed kills but 14.6:1 kill-to-weapons ratio suggesting many noncombatants; Hamburger Hill for exchange ratios). Population security gains derived primarily from VCI self-decimation at Tet, CORDS efforts, and RF/PF augmentation, not U.S. main-force ops. Komer: "There was no change in strategy whatsoever... it didn’t really change until we began withdrawing."

**Willbanks Range 03 (Vietnamization, Lam Son 719, An Loc 1972; /tmp/willbanks-ranges/range_3_6501-9500.txt exclusive):** "In terms of the sheer volume of materiel and modern equipment, Vietnamization worked." RVNAF one of world's largest/best-equipped by 1970. Gaps: fighting ability/leadership/motivation. CORDS advisory teams (province/district) supported pacification/development; ~half of 13,500 advisors in CORDS orgs. Late training upgrades (Abrams pressure, Bolton findings, Laird "highly qualified" directive). "One war" + combined ops (I Corps best). Cambodia 1970 test "somewhat misleading." Lam Son 719 (1971 Laos incursion, no US ground/advisers): "fled in panic... in disarray"; 3,800 KIA etc. "demonstrated that Vietnamization had not been the success." 1972 Easter: ARVN "fared better" at An Loc but "horrendous casualties"; Abrams: airpower (not ARVN arms) decisive. Nixon proclaimed success.   Under COMUSMACV [[westmoreland-william]], the Army pursued a strategy of attrition centered on large-unit "search and destroy" operations. Westmoreland's June 1965 44-battalion request argued "The GVN cannot stand up successfully to this kind of pressure without reinforcement" and that "we have reached a point in Vietnam where we cannot avoid the commitment to combat of U.S. ground troops." "No more niceties about defensive posture and reaction"; the Army "had to forget enclaves and take the war to the enemy." The explicit MACV concept: employ U.S. forces "against the hardcore DRV/VC forces in reaction and search and destroy operations and thus permit the concentration of Vietnamese troops in the heavily populated areas..." (Krepinevich). Force ratios were calculated with ARVN battalions rated equal to VC, U.S. as 2x (Marines 3x); conflict declared "phase 3" conventional war. The la Drang Valley battle (Nov 1965) was hailed as validation: "the ability of the Americans to meet and defeat the best troops the enemy could put on the field of battle was once more demonstrated beyond any possible doubt, as was the validity of the Army’s airmobile concept." By mid-1967 86% of battalion operations time was search-and-destroy; 96% of engagements were company strength or less and 88% enemy-initiated. Large operations (ATTLEBORO, CEDAR FALLS, JUNCTION CITY) employed massive firepower (e.g. JUNCTION CITY: 3,235 tons of bombs and over 366,000 artillery rounds). Body count became the primary metric: "if the commander before you had killed X number of vc, you had best kill X + 1." (Krepinevich Ch. 6).
- **The fall of Saigon (1975).** See dedicated page [[events/fall-of-saigon-1975]]. North Vietnamese forces overran the South; Saigon fell on **30 April 1975**, **reunifying Vietnam under communism**. Neighboring Cambodia fell to the genocidal **Khmer Rouge** the same year. Per Willbanks Ch. 11 (Range 04 exclusive): The final phase began with Phuoc Long (test case proving US abandonment and shattered confidence) and Ban Me Thuot (10–18 Mar 1975, "blossoming lotus" tactic, ARVN counterattacks failed due to leadership e.g. family evacuations mid-battle and friendly-fire CP loss). Thieu's "light at the top, heavy on the bottom" truncation order triggered the "convoy of tears" withdrawal from Pleiku/Kontum via Route 7 ("75 percent of II Corps combat strength…had been tragically expended"; "greatest disaster in the history of ARVN"; equipment/ammo abandoned). I Corps collapsed under conflicting "yo-yo" orders (Truong); Hue abandoned unopposed; Da Nang "disintegrated in its own terror" amid 2M refugees (29 Mar sea evac disaster). PAVN "Ho Chi Minh Campaign" (Dung, 16+ divisions) exploited: Xuan Loc last major battle (stiff 18th ARVN fight); rapid close on Saigon. Thieu resigned 21 Apr; Duong Van Minh installed to negotiate; Operation FREQUENT WIND evac 29–30 Apr; unconditional surrender 30 Apr at Presidential Palace. "the army collapsed, as one observer wrote, 'like a house with its timbers eaten away by termites'." Over half ARVN fighting strength lost in weeks. ## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Vietnamese **anticolonial nationalism** and the French (then American) refusal
  to accept it; the **Cold War overlay** that turned a decolonization struggle into a
  superpower test — Washington acted on "worst-case assumptions" despite finding "no evidence
  of mass popular support for Communism," driven by [[containment]] and the **domino theory**.
- `contributed_to:` In the advisory phase (1954–65), U.S. Army institutional choices: building ARVN as conventional force for external invasion rather than internal security; neglect of paramilitaries and police; training and doctrine that remained conventional despite Kennedy pressure. "the Army successfully insulated itself against reports of the failure of its counterinsurgency efforts." This rigidity `contributed_to` the growth of the insurgency to phase 2 operations by 1960–61 and the decision to introduce U.S. combat troops in 1965.
- `contributed_to:` In the intervention years, the "Army Concept" produced search-and-destroy dominance (86% of battalion ops time in 1967), body-count metrics, lavish firepower (multiple tons ordnance per kill), deliberate refugee creation (3+ million, 20% of population 1964–69, prime cause per PROVN), and marginalization of pacification/CORDS ("military operations would be given first priority in every case"). Per Daddis (Range 03), however, search-and-destroy was explicitly framed as concurrent with population security ("Friendly forces still must find, fix, fight, and destroy the enemy, and concurrently provide the necessary security for the population" — Westmoreland). The "parallel war" elements (CORDS, Revolutionary Development, clearing/holding) were integral to the concept but operated at cross-purposes: "The elements of the parallel war in fact tended to work against one another. The destruction caused by military engagements undermined the construction aims of pacification planners." Tet `contributed_to` the political collapse of support when the Army could offer only "more of the same" or wider war (Laos/Cambodia incursions); Wheeler admitted no victory plan existed. Komer reported post-Tet losses: "we lost 38% of our Tet holdings; ... 2.7 million people" from secure category. Alternative paths (enclaves or demographic frontier focusing on population security and denial of access to rural base) were not taken; invasion and Laotian barrier strategies would likely have cost more and prolonged U.S. exposure while still neglecting internal threat. `contributed_to` high-cost, low-payoff outcome and Vietnamization as extraction mechanism rather than victory path.
- `contributed_to:` (Daddis Range 03) Border battles (Khe Sanh, Dak To) diverted resources from populated areas ("to lure US forces to the periphery") precisely because main-force NVA had to be contested or it would expand bases and support local forces/VC "in the vital battle for the people." Implementation revealed inherent tensions rather than simple neglect of pacification. "Without continuous local security to keep the VC away from the farmer, the rest of pacification can’t even get underway." "There must be a parallel pacification offensive if we are to attain our goal of winning over the people" (Komer). Yet "Tet, more than any other battle... exposed the fragility of MACV’s pacification program."
- `contributed_to:` Post-Tet persistence of Concept under Abrams (Ewell ratios, SPEEDY EXPRESS, Hamburger Hill) despite "one war" rhetoric; RF/PF ("Ruff-Puffs") and CORDS bore disproportionate burden for population security while consuming tiny fraction of resources (12–30% enemy deaths for 2–4% cost). VCI targeting remained secondary to big-unit OOB focus.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` a communist, reunified Vietnam; a profound **US strategic and moral defeat**
  ("the Vietnam syndrome"); **millions of dead** (mostly Vietnamese); the Cambodian
  catastrophe; and a lasting demonstration of the **limits of superpower military power**
  against a determined nationalist insurgency.
- `produced:` Missed opportunity in advisory years to build RVNAF for COIN. Force structure, training, and command choices locked in conventional orientation; Strategic Hamlets and similar programs undermined by emphasis on sweeps over sustained population security and infrastructure destruction. This set conditions for large-scale U.S. intervention and the application of the Army Concept at scale.
- `produced:` Tet as "defeat in victory" for the U.S. Army approach: military body counts advanced attrition metrics but produced "substantial setback" to pacification, massive new refugees, urban terror, and erosion of U.S. public will (1,001 KIA in short period measured against prior rates, not enemy losses). Civilian leadership explicitly rejected the strategy; troop ceiling imposed; Vietnamization launched as political/military necessity rather than victory condition. Army itself saw no need for reevaluation.
- `produced:` In the withdrawal years, Vietnamization transferred burden to RVNAF (buildup to 801k) but could not fix leadership, corruption, or promotion pathologies; U.S. main-force withdrawal proceeded while Concept-driven ops continued until resources ran out. Ultimate `produced:` communist victory in 1975 after U.S. public support for further intervention had been exhausted. "the Army, in fighting the war it was not prepared to fight, lost the opportunity to fight the war it knew how to win." (Krepinevich). Post-war, the service extracted few durable lessons for low-intensity conflict.

**Range 04 (Summers Ch. 15, Epilogue — coalition and lessons):** `produced:` fatal coalition overreach — attempt to both repel external (NVN) aggression *and* perform nation-building for South Vietnam (unlike Korea, where internal resolution was left to the ROK despite worse 1950 conditions). "We failed in Vietnam because we attempted to do too much." Resulting ARVN dependence ("they try to do everything... So the Vietnamese don’t rely on themselves") and no doctrinal/organizational adjustments during Vietnamization for reduced US participation. "Vietnamization was actually 'the model or paradigm of a new strategy of retreat.'" Irony: original anti-Chinese-expansion rationale produced a hostile (Soviet-aligned) state on China's southern border and disappearance of the RVN. "In war the result is never final."

`produced:` enduring lessons on force employment: military as "battle-ax" (designed "to fight and win on the battlefield"; not for nation-building); requirement for "tangible, obtainable political goals" and concrete "definition of 'victory'" *before* commitment (not platitudes); honest public framing of interests ("enlightened self-interest") rather than apocalyptic language that produces credibility gaps; principles of war as "military planning interrogatories" (judgment framework, not rules) with Objective first; need to master the profession of arms rather than confuse preparation/management with war proper. "The quintessential 'strategic lesson learned' from the Vietnam war is that we must once again become masters of the profession of arms."

## U.S. Army Doctrinal Dimension (Krepinevich)

The American ground effort was shaped by the Army's "Army Concept": preference for conventional, large-unit, high-firepower attrition warfare optimized for mid-intensity conflict against peer enemies. This produced:

- Search-and-destroy operations and body-count metrics under Westmoreland. Westmoreland's concept explicitly subordinated population security: U.S. forces against "hardcore DRV/VC" in search-and-destroy to free ARVN for populated areas. Ia Drang "validated" the approach for MACV. By 1967, 86% battalion time on search-and-destroy; 96% engagements at company or less. Operations such as JUNCTION CITY required "several tons of ordnance... to kill one vc." Body count "quickly became the criterion for measuring success"; "if the commander before you had killed X number of vc, you had best kill X + 1." Incentives drove artillery/air units to expend allotments regardless of verified targets.
- Marginalization of pacification ("the other war") despite programs such as CORDS. "to a great extent, the problem of U.S. strategy in Vietnam was a problem of resource allocation." Army "rarely allowed them to interfere with its top priority, namely, winning the war through the destruction of enemy forces on the battlefield." PROVN study (1966) recommending pacification priority was downgraded to "conceptual document"; officers forbidden to discuss it. Even after CORDS (May 1967), with Komer as deputy COMUSMACV, the military component dominated numbers but civilians held top positions; pacification claims often falsified (e.g. Vinh Long "80 percent" to "70 percent" pacified).
- Heavy use of firepower, defoliation, and airmobility within a conventional frame. Westmoreland: "Firepower." "infantry found the enemy and the artillery and the air killed the enemy." ROE often misunderstood or "creatively applied"; H&I fires routine with little value. Defoliation (RANCH HAND): 1.57M acres in 1967; "over 500 civilians experience crop loss for every ton of rice denied the vc." "we destroyed more than ten times as much rice as the VC/NVA grew."
- Short tours and career incentives that worked against long-term local knowledge and population security. 12-month tours, 6-month commands ("the worst personnel policy in history"). "The United States has not been in Vietnam for nine years, but for one year nine times." Advisory duty viewed as career-detrimental ("out of the mainstream"); only 24% requested in 1969-70 samples. "a competent and ambitious officer considers a command position in a US unit as preferable to any other position, including advisory positions."

Even the post-1968 reorientation under Abrams faced institutional limits. The result was a strategy that could not translate battlefield successes into lasting control of the rural population or political victory.

**Ch. 6–8 specifics (intervention years, Krepinevich):** 44-battalion request and "phase 3" framing; PROVN suppression; McNamara's 1966 assessment that "the large-unit operations war... is largely irrelevant to pacification"; 9th Infantry Division (Ewell) kill-ratio focus ("We really blew a lot of civilians away"); "go-it-alone" sidelining of ARVN ("We never really paid attention to the ARVN Army. We didn’t give a damn about them"); "counterinsurgency American-style" via technological fixes rather than population security. "Army operations in South Vietnam were oriented overwhelmingly toward the Army Concept... It was far easier to fall back on the logistical and technological elements of strategy—sensors, ground radars, firepower on call, helicopter gunships, herbicides and defoliants."

**Nagl Ch. 7 application — non-learning thesis in the fighting years (1965-1972):** Nagl documents the Army Concept in full operational effect and the institutional mechanisms that blocked learning. Suggestions from below (USMC CAPs; PROVN questioning core assumptions) continued, but “the learning cycle stopped at the level of the Chief of Staff of the Army in Washington and COMUSMACV in Vietnam.” CSA Harold K. Johnson (Christmas 1965) philosophically agreed with junior officers’ small-unit “bait” proposals but rejected them over response-time public outcry and casualty “callousness” concerns. ARCOV (1966) produced recommended light/mobile TO&E changes that were “adopted eventually,” yet “We were not into pacification at all” (Hutton); all within conventional combat orientation. Institutional barriers: “can-do” attitude (“once decisions are made... it is not the business of subordinates to criticize... but to put all shoulders to the wheel” — Rosson); Komer on impossibility of candid “this war stinks” reporting; civilians in DoD freer to assess because “not fettered with a rigorous belief that firepower yielded results.” Bad data and reporting systems (“data is bad” — Abrams); alternate strategies briefing (Jan 1966) yielded “Nothing significant” because run by authors of existing approach. MACV size “made not only change but also learning extremely difficult”; Westmoreland blocked intermediate HQ; subordinates screened leaders from reality (Vann: “even his best subordinates... continually screen him from the realities”). Komer: “Viet Nam was a tragedy of bureaucratic inability to adapt to unconventional situations.”

Only Tet forced political direction for change (CORDS; Abrams for Westmoreland). Abrams had flagged problems in PROVN and pursued “One War,” but “confronted a culture that by then was so entrenched in its attitude that even the MACV commander could not change it.” “The organizational culture of the United States Army precluded organizational learning on counterinsurgency during the Vietnam War.” Figure 7-4 shows theoretical thinking and local doctrine remained low-level only. Nagl (building directly on Krepinevich sources, interviews, Pentagon Papers) uses these specifics to demonstrate the negative case for the “learning institution” test in the main war years. Cross to [[nagl-learning-to-eat-soup-with-a-knife-2005]], [[army-concept]], [[learning-institution]].

**Advisory phase (1954–1965) specifics:** MAAG/early MACV efforts (1954–65) prioritized conventional ARVN divisions and offensive operations ("find, fix, fight, and finish") over counterinsurgency requirements. Despite explicit recognition that "We cannot hope to match vc foot mobility with mechanical mobility alone," training and force design replicated U.S. mid-intensity models. Kennedy-era pressure produced Special Forces expansion and some doctrinal language but was met with resistance ("Any good soldier can handle guerrillas") and superficial compliance (relabeled conventional instruction, padded school statistics). Programs such as Strategic Hamlets were supported only insofar as they did not divert from strike-force development; execution suffered from MAAG preference for sweeps. MACV commander selection (Harkins) reinforced continuity with the Concept. "the Army successfully insulated itself against reports of the failure of its counterinsurgency efforts." This phase represented a critical missed opportunity to adapt before escalation.

**ARVN/RVNAF pillar and advisory effort (Daddis Ch. 6, Range 04 exclusive):** The "development of the Republic of Vietnam’s Armed Forces (RVNAF)" was a core pillar of Westmoreland's three-mission strategy, intended to keep the war Vietnamese in character. "the bulk of the military support of Revolutionary Development must rest on the shoulders of the ARVN." RVNAF missions were diverse and simultaneous (conventional, territorial, RD/pacification support). Early MAAG built conventional divisions for external threat; paramilitaries neglected. Persistent issues: leadership, desertions, corruption, political reliability, US-ARVN combined command refusal by Saigon, advisor cultural/language barriers, and perception of ARVN as dependent/puppet force. Post-1965 shift to MATs (Mobile Advisory Teams) for embedded training and RD integration; 40% of ARVN battalions in direct RD support by Mar 1967. Tet showed some gains but "the problem of ARVN leadership did not go away." Vietnamization expanded numbers (to ~801k) but could not fix underlying pathologies. "Training an uncertain army" exposed the limits of external advisory effort when GVN base was weak. (verbatim Ch. 6 notes grounding)

**Limits of strategy and nation-building (Daddis Conclusion, Range 04 exclusive):** "It is possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose a war." The US "attempted to do too much" (repel external aggression + nation-build a fragile post-colonial state), producing ARVN/GVN dependence ("they try to do everything... So the Vietnamese don’t rely on themselves"). "There was no magic solution to the VC/NVA threat." Fundamental post-colonial limits: GVN legitimacy/corruption/leadership weaknesses; enemy sanctuary and will. Vietnamization framed by some as "paradigm of a new strategy of retreat." Comprehensive strategy (containment + ARVN pillar + parallel war) still failed when host political/institutional base and adversary advantages made endurance contest unwinnable for external power. `produced:` ultimate outcome; `contributed_to:` historiography debates on whether strategy itself or environment doomed the effort. Cross [[sources/daddis-westmorelands-war-2014]], [[hubs/warfare/cold-war/vietnam-war/vietnam-war]], [[actors/westmoreland-william]], [[periods/cold-war]].


Nagl (Ch. 6) details the Ap Bac debacle (2 Jan 1963) as emblematic: despite heavy superiority, VC downed five helicopters, inflicted ~200 ARVN casualties (killing three US advisers). Vann called it “a miserable damn performance, just like it always is.” York (visiting site): “They got away, that’s what happened.” ARVN artillery (18 hours later) killed five of its own and wounded fourteen. Harkins (off-site): claimed a trap hours after VC had departed; later “I consider it a victory. We took the objective.” CINCPAC Felt called it success and told critics to “Get on the team.” Vann’s detailed 91-page after-action (via Porter) recommending national seminars was not acted on; Porter’s report on ARVN counterproductive methods (pre-op bombardments alienating civilians; rare night operations) was ordered destroyed by Harkins. Halberstam: “Consciously or unconsciously, the Army staff system tends to reflect the thinking of the senior officer.” Advisers at risk for accurate reporting; institutional instruction to new arrivals (May 1963): “Avoid gratuitous criticism. Emphasize the feeling of achievement...” Nagl (building on Krepinevich) notes ARVN design was "in direct insubordination" to civilian guidance for internal threat; "indiscriminate use of airpower and artillery made many peasants more inclined to favor the Viet Cong." Organizational culture (big, standardization-driven, long memory, skepticism toward "crash" new ideas) plus career disincentives blocked bottom-up learning despite field input. "It’s much easier to change one regiment [in the British Army] than the U.S. Army" (Hutton). Doctrine developed stateside; high command resisted even Kennedy push, convinced "the essence of the problem is military." Nagl assessment (Fig 6-2): no success on victory/objective/unity/minimum force/appropriate structure. Learning metrics (Fig 6-3): bottom-up input yes but theoretical thinking low-level only; no local doctrine, training centers, or responsive staff. 

**Tet Offensive and post-Tet (Ch. 9, Krepinevich Range 4 + Willbanks Range 02):** Pre-Tet Army confidence in attrition unshaken; sought wider war. Khe Sanh diverted forces from populated areas. Tet (30-31 Jan 1968): 100,000 troops struck 36 provincial capitals + major cities; body count 37k+ (VCI up to 30% losses). MACV: "disastrous failure for the Communists"; Westmoreland likened to Battle of the Bulge. Army: "the Communists had utterly failed"; "enormous casualties proved it: the Army was winning." No internal reevaluation. Civilians shocked (Westmoreland had promised 1968 turning point). Wheeler: enemy retained "will and the capability to continue"; RD "severe setback." Requested more troops or wider operations. ISA (Warnke) alternative: demographic frontier/coastal focus for population security, spoiling raids, reduced casualties. Clifford: "What is our plan for military victory in Vietnam? The fact is we do not have a plan for military victory." Wise Men recommended RVNAF upgrade + U.S. phaseout.

**Willbanks on Tet/Hue (exclusive Range 02):** Attacks on "39 of South Vietnam’s 44 provincial capitals, five of six largest cities... 71 of 242 district capitals..." "great psychological victory that proved to be the turning point..." "worst intelligence failure of the war." Detailed indicators (TCK-TKN docs, unusual Loc Ninh/Dak To stands, CIA "Big Gamble") ignored due to downgraded estimates, Khe Sanh focus, preconceptions, poor fusion. Communist side: "subjective" overestimation of uprising + underestimation of US/ARVN reaction; "set our goals too high"; no plans for failure cases. Battle of Hue (26 days): divided C2 (ARVN north/Citadel, Marines south; no unified commander until too late) produced "three separate battles", piecemeal commitment, fratricide risk, inequitable resupply. Initial ROE ban + weather limited fires; Marines (no MOUT training) adapted house-to-house with ONTOS/106mm; "room by room"; province HQ turning point 5 Feb; Citadel flag 24 Feb. Casualties: Marines 147 KIA, ARVN 384 KIA, ~5k+ enemy claimed in city; 40% city destroyed, 5,800+ civilians killed/missing + mass graves from VC death squads. "textbook study of the difficulties involved in combat in an urban area." (See source Range 02 for verbatim.) + negotiations. Decisions: ceiling frozen 549,500; Westmoreland to CSA; LBJ withdrawal from race. "The Army approach to the war in Vietnam had lost its credibility with the political leadership."

From insurgent view Tet succeeded on key dimensions: "pacification program was dealt... a 'substantial setback'"; 800k refugees; 1,001 U.S. KIA eroded will "more quickly than MACV could wear down the insurgent forces"; terror in cities (Hue). "Tet was a victory for Hanoi." "the Army had convinced itself that it had won the Tet Offensive; therefore, no soul-searching was necessary."

Vietnamization: focus on "transferring responsibility... and withdrawing American troops." RVNAF paper strength to 801k but "not so much a shortage of men as a lack of leaders" due to political promotion practices. Abrams "one war" plan (pop security criterion) but Ewell continued kill-ratio emphasis (158:1 peaks); SPEEDY EXPRESS (9th Div) 10,883 claimed kills vs. 14.6:1 kill-to-weapons (indicating noncombatants); "population security... remained basically unaffected." Hamburger Hill conventional for ratios. Komer: "no change in strategy whatsoever... myth of a change... figment of media imagination." Security gains from VCI Tet losses + CORDS + RF/PF, not U.S. main units.

**Ruff-Puffs and "other war" details (remainder Ch. 8):** RF/PF ("Ruff-Puffs") locally recruited, part-time PF especially tied to population; performed best for security but received WWII weapons, low-quality ARVN leadership, 30% support when attacked; higher casualties than ARVN most years. Yet "accounted for roughly 30 percent of the VC/NVA combat deaths inflicted by the RVNAF" at "2-4 percent of the total annual cost." Army still prioritized ARVN/main-force; Ewell "military operations would be given first priority." Operations (MASHER/WHITE WING, THAYER II) created refugees (thousands per district) via firepower then departed; "US-RVNAF bombing and artillery fire... are the immediate and prime causes of refugee movement." 3M+ (20% pop) refugees 1964-69. VCI neglected ("total neglect of the guerrillas and the so-called Viet Cong infrastructure"); Phoenix ineffective (3% above-district Party; ~20% eliminations by dedicated forces). Special ops diverted from pacification support.

**Paths untaken evaluation (Ch. 10):** Army failed to buy time cheaply or defeat insurgents acceptably. Attrition high-cost in LIC because required quick win before public fatigue. Invasion of North: ignored southern VC base, China risk, French precedent, prolonged occupation. Laotian incursion (EL PASO/Summers): 8-division barrier to cut Trail/"wither" VC; support requirements prohibitive, Thailand bypass possible, fixed positions vulnerable; would prolong U.S. role. Enclaves (Taylor): coastal security for ARVN inland ops; "lowest cost... in casualties and resources"; avoided worse "firebase psychosis." Demographic frontier (Warnke): continuous coastal belt + strike teams + reserves; "best hope for strengthening the GVN"; lengthened LOCs, better pop protection. "the strategies of attrition, invasion, and incursion were all heavily weighted toward... phase 3" while "emphasis be placed, first and foremost, on the internal threat." "the Army, in fighting the war it was not prepared to fight, lost the opportunity to fight the war it knew how to win."

**Post-war lessons (Conclusions, Ch. 10):** Army "renaissance" in SOF/LIDs but adopted Weinberger conditions ("vital" interests, sufficient force, "clear intention of winning," public support) that "represent the Army’s lessons from Vietnam" yet "learned many of the wrong ones." Support existed pre-Tet for years; "vital" subjective; doctrine (FM100-20 1981 restating 1967) still favored "strike campaigns" and main-force destruction over sustained security ("hearts and minds finishes a poor second"); LIDs mid-intensity heavy; rotations blocked expertise. No persistent high-level interagency FID mechanism. "Low-intensity warfare represents the most likely arena... counterinsurgency the most demanding contingency." "America’s enemies are not going to play to its military strong suits; rather, they will exploit its weak points." "in spite of its anguish in Vietnam, the Army has learned little of value. Yet the nation’s policymakers have endorsed the service’s misperceptions... This represents a very dangerous mixture."

## Summers, On Strategy (1982) — Strategic-Level Clausewitzian Analysis

See the dedicated high-resolution strategic/campaign analysis in the Warfare Hub: [[hubs/warfare/cold-war/vietnam-war/vietnam-war]] (reciprocal link; template-aligned treatment of objectives, principles of war, nature-of-war diagnosis, and lessons).

Summers (U.S. Army colonel, Vietnam veteran, AWC strategist and negotiator) supplies the canonical Clausewitzian diagnosis. The war produced "tactical victory, strategic defeat": the U.S. succeeded in logistics and battlefield engagements ("we succeeded in everything we set out to do") yet lost because it misidentified the nature of the war. The famous 1975 exchange in Hanoi with a North Vietnamese colonel: "You know you never defeated us on the battlefield." Reply: "That may be so, but it is also irrelevant."

**Range 03 (Offensive onward, lines 4801-7100):** Strategic-level principle applications (verbatim). U.S. entered strategic defensive (containment; Korea precedent) but "confused that posture with the strategic offensive"; "purely defensive strategy ... no allowance for decisive offensive action" (Lung). NVA consistent strategic offensive (conquest SV): 1964 3 NVA divs Cambodia bases (Pleiku-AnKhe-QuiNhon slice); post-Ia Drang tac def (VC screen preserve regulars); Tet68/Eastertide72 tac off; 1975 17-div final. 1975 Dung: >20k km trails/pipeline/vehicles; corps 1974; Politburo "United States could hardly jump back in"; Central Highlands main; Ban Me Thuot ~3 div vs 1 regt+3 CG (5.5:1 inf, 1.2:1 armor, 2.1:1 arty); 4 corps closure Saigon 30 Apr. Alternatives (Palmer 5-div DMZ-to-Thai "wither on the vine"; Vien isolation; JCS Lao denial; Westmoreland Laos/Cambodia) rejected over reserves. Graduated response vs JCS hard/fast. Clausewitz "defense without active purpose self-contradictory."

Mass/EoF/Maneuver: US wrong COG (VC not NVA alliance); global dilemma; tactical superiority "fritter[ed] away" tactically. NVA classic: 1964-65 highlands mass (Ia Drang defeat); 1968 VC cities ("attacking everywhere ... superior strength nowhere" ~100k tac fail/strat win); 1972 12 div/150k; 1975 interior lines/screen "little ability to mass" ARVN; II Corps 900km/18d "could not draw maps"; 15 vs 5; 4 corps. ARVN families "inhibited ... maneuver ... impossible to mass"; Xuan Loc 18th ARVN destroyed 3 NVA. Flexibility key.

Unity: NVA Giap Politburo dual; 1975 Politburo+CMC joint; Le Duc Tho "not allowed to return"; named commands; "seamless web." US NSC fragmented ("piecemeal"); Komer "everybody and nobody"; CINCPAC 5k mi; Westmoreland "not I but [Sharp]" theater cdr; "three separate ... struggles"; "Without common goal ... impossible ... unity of command."

Security/Surprise (will-based): 1965 US (Ia Drang off track); Tet68 NVA ("complete surprise"; "not credible"; US resolve "wavered"); 1972 Christmas ("will in spades"; B-52 vital centers; "decisive factor" Jan73).

Simplicity: US "conflicting objectives"; "fragmented" direction; "convoluted" command; "inability to explain ... undercut ... support"; "poisonous flower" (Herr Saigon); "base camp mentality"; Clausewitz activity must relate to combat. NVA simple objective/unity. (Full in [[sources/summers-on-strategy-1982]] Range 03; [[hubs/warfare/cold-war/vietnam-war/vietnam-war]].)

Core argument (Ch. 8 and throughout):
- The Second Indochina War was an interstate conventional war of aggression by the regular armed forces of North Vietnam (NVA), not a Maoist people's war / revolutionary insurgency whose center of gravity was the southern population and VC infrastructure. The decisive forces that captured Saigon in 1975 were four NVA corps.
- U.S. strategy therefore applied counterinsurgency tools and the "Army Concept" of attrition inside South Vietnam to the wrong problem. The enemy's true COG lay in the North's will and conventional military power.
- This violated Clausewitz's "first, the supreme, the most far-reaching act of judgement": correctly establish the kind of war.
- **Range 02 specifics (Ch. 8):** "it is essential to distinguish the First Indo-China war between France and the Viet Minh from the Second Indo-China war between North Vietnam and South Vietnam. The first was a revolutionary war. The second was not... the forces that captured Saigon did not grow out of the Viet Cong but were the regular armed forces of North Vietnam."
- Guerrilla as economy-of-force screen: "the guerrilla war was a kind of economy of force operation on the part of North Vietnam to buy time and to wear down superior U.S. military forces... we responded mainly to Hanoi’s simulated insurgency rather than to its real but controlled aggression, as a bull charges the toreador’s cape, not the toreador."
- Ia Drang 1965: US 1st Cav vs. three NVA regiments "a brilliant spoiling attack had completely derailed Hanoi’s hopes... Now was the time for the United States to take the offensive." But "instead of orienting on North Vietnam—the source of war—we turned our attention to the symptom—the guerrilla war in the south."
- **Ch. 7 dogma (Range 02):** Westmoreland: "The Communists in Vietnam waged a classic revolutionary war." Contradicted by Thompson's own definition and results: no mass uprising; "at least 80 percent of the day-to-day combat... by regular People’s Army of Vietnam (PAVN) troops" (Johnson); "Even at the very end there was no popular mass uprising."
- Part I analyzes the "Environment" (national will of people/Congress; multiple frictions including doctrine and dogma that regressed toward 18th-century cabinet-war attitudes). Doctrine shift: 1968 FM changed purpose to "preserve, restore, or create an environment of order or stability within which the instrumentalities of government can function" (vs. prior focus on defeating enemy armed forces).
- Part II applies the principles of war (Objective, Offensive, Mass/Economy of Force/Maneuver, Unity of Command, Security/Surprise, Simplicity, Coalition) at the strategic level and finds systematic mismatch.
- **The Objective (Ch. 9, Range 02):** FM 100-5 1962: ultimate objective "the destruction of the enemy’s armed forces and his will to fight." Historical loss of strategic dimension; 1968 edition dropped "will to fight." Political object "will determine both the military objective to be reached and the amount of effort it requires" (Clausewitz). MACV mission: two divergent tasks (defeat external aggression + attain independent secure South Vietnam — the latter political). US had ~22 shifting rationales (Arnold); enemy objective remained simple and consistent (control all Indochina). Ridgway 1954: military must not compromise judgment on costs (Dien Bien Phu would cost ≥ Korea). Sanctuaries a "self-inflicted wound." Kinnard: ~70% of generals "uncertain of its objectives."
- Preparation for war (organize/train/equip, systems analysis, management) was confused with war proper (employment of force for political ends). Civilian limited-war theorists and military abdication of strategy produced the gap.

**National will and congressional authorization (Part I Ch. 1–2, Range 01 exclusive):** President Johnson made a deliberate choice not to mobilize the American people "to invoke the national will" for fear of jeopardizing the Great Society: "... History provided too many cases where the sound of the bugle put an immediate end to the hopes and dreams of the best reformers... And the generals. Oh, they’d love the war, too... That’s why I am suspicious of the military." (Johnson quoted, lines ~770-780) McNamara: "The greatest contribution Vietnam is making—right or wrong is beside the point—is that it is developing an ability in the United States to fight a limited war, to go to war without the necessity of arousing the public ire." (lines ~1020) Gulf of Tonkin Resolution substituted for declaration of war (88-2 Senate, 416-0 House); Sen. Javits later: "The power of decision... had not been stolen. It had been surrendered." (lines ~1235-1280) This recreated 18th-c. cabinet war (people as instrument). Clausewitz: "In the eighteenth century... war was still an affair for governments alone, and the people’s role was simply that of an instrument... governments parted company with their peoples and behaved as if they were themselves the state." (lines ~530-555) Result: "to many Americans the Vietnam war was 'Johnson’s war' or 'Nixon’s war' or 'the Army’s war' rather than the American people’s war." (lines ~555-560) The trinity was unbalanced.

**Friction: people and bureaucracy (Ch. 3–4):** "The student draft deferments... were part of a deliberate Presidential policy not to arouse the passions of the American people. The effect of this was that we fought the Vietnam war in cold blood." (lines ~1740-1750) "We did not kill the enemy, we 'inflicted casualties'; we did not destroy things, we 'neutralized targets.'" (lines ~1775) Korea lessons ignored (Taylor: "no thorough-going analysis ever made of the lessons to be learned from Korea"; lines ~940). Bureaucratic friction: PPBS and systems analysts "dominated military strategy formulation"; "the military... became neophyte political scientists and systems analysts and were outclassed by the civilian professionals"; PPBS excelled at "preparation for war" but "was neither designed for, nor was it capable of, fighting the war itself." (lines ~2080-2190) Clausewitz: theories aiming at "fixed values" clash with war's uncertainty and "continuous interaction of opposites." (lines ~2430-2450) "The failure to invoke the national will was one of the major strategic failures... It produced a strategic vulnerability that our enemy was able to exploit." (lines ~1060)

See full extraction and principle-by-principle analysis in the source page [[sources/summers-on-strategy-1982]] (Range 04 block) and the hub [[hubs/warfare/cold-war/vietnam-war/vietnam-war]] (Epilogue lessons and coalition synthesis).

**Range 04 additions (Ch. 15 Coalition Warfare, Epilogue, Appendix — exclusive cache grounding):** Coalition analysis: US attempted "to do too much" (repel external aggression + nation building); "This was an error we never made in Korea"; "What the United States could never do was 'solve the internal problems' of South Vietnam. Only the Vietnamese themselves could accomplish that task." CORDS produced short-term pacification gains but "serious long-term disadvantages" (ARVN lost initiative and "rely on the Americans"). Vietnamization was "essentially a strategy that would require the Vietnamese to survive with greatly reduced American participation" with no RVNAF adjustments; "paradigm of a new strategy of retreat." Clausewitz: allies "will never take [the cause] so seriously as it takes its own." Ironies: Nixon Doctrine mirrored adversary practice; original anti-China rationale produced "a hostile state allied with its most dangerous enemy... on its southern border" and disappearance of RVN.

Epilogue lessons (priority): Mission analysis (National Security Act 1947 missions); military forces as "battle-ax" ("designed, equipped and trained for a specific task: to fight and win on the battlefield"; not nation-building); "Combat is the only effective force in war..." Principles as "military planning interrogatories" (Paret/Hines) — framework of questions for judgment, not rules. Objective requires "tangible, obtainable political goals" and concrete "definition of 'victory'" (not total conquest; example: status quo in Korea). Public support demands honesty ("enlightened self-interest") not "apocalyptic language" (Javits on credibility gap). Security/Surprise paradox of open society + media; presidential volatility aids surprise (Truman Korea example). Simplicity for public understanding and execution (Sassoon poem; MacArthur on timeless principles). "The quintessential 'strategic lesson learned'... is that we must once again become masters of the profession of arms."

Appendix supplies canonical strategic/tactical definitions for each principle (Objective as derivative of political aim; Offensive as initiative/maintenance even on strategic defensive; Mass/Economy/Maneuver against COG; etc.) and their use in planning/force/contingency processes.

Cross-references and tensions: contrasts with Krepinevich/Nagl emphasis on the Army's failure/refusal to adapt to COIN requirements (Summers argues the war should not primarily have been fought as COIN). Both perspectives are retained; the war contained elements of both insurgency and conventional interstate conflict, but Summers insists the decisive instrument in the end was NVA regulars and the failure was at the level of correctly naming the war. Laotian barrier proposals (EL PASO) associated with Summers' analysis are critiqued in Krepinevich Ch. 10 on logistical and bypass grounds.

## Historiography

Logevall and the post-revisionist consensus stress that the war's roots lay in
**decolonization, not Soviet expansion** — Ho was "too independent and nationalist-oriented"
for Stalin's taste, and the US misread an anticolonial nationalism as a Cold War domino.
Debate continues over whether the war was "winnable," and over the relative weight of Cold
War ideology versus local Vietnamese dynamics.

Krepinevich's institutional analysis focuses on the U.S. Army side: the service's adherence to the **"Army Concept"** (conventional mid-intensity warfare, attrition via firepower, big-unit search-and-destroy) made successful counterinsurgency structurally difficult. The advisory period (1954–65) represented a missed opportunity to adapt; the intervention years (1965–68) saw the Concept applied at scale under Westmoreland; even the post-Tet shift under Abrams was limited by institutional preferences and declining U.S. resources. Tet 1968 was "defeat in victory" for the Army: military metrics advanced but produced political collapse of support without internal reevaluation of the Concept. Vietnamization and withdrawal followed civilian rejection; four alternative paths (invasion, Laotian incursion, enclaves, demographic frontier) were weighed in Ch. 10, with population-centric coastal strategies offering lower costs and better focus on the internal threat than conventional or cross-border options. Post-war the Army extracted mostly wrong lessons (civilian will rather than doctrinal/force-structure failures); doctrine and LIDs remained oriented on transforming LIC into conventional fights the service knew how to wage. "in spite of its anguish in Vietnam, the Army has learned little of value" while policymakers endorsed the misperceptions—a "very dangerous mixture" for future low-intensity conflicts (Conclusions).

Nagl's comparative study reinforces this with organizational culture theory: the British Army in the Malayan Emergency functioned as a "learning institution" and adapted; the U.S. Army in Vietnam largely did not (see [[malayan-emergency-1948]] and [[nagl-learning-to-eat-soup-with-a-knife-2005]]). Ch. 7 supplies the detailed negative-case evidence for the fighting years: MACOV/ARCOV limits, Johnson junior-officer rejection, screening, Komer bureaucratic-inability diagnosis, post-Tet entrenched culture, and explicit Figures 7-3/7-4 showing COIN doctrine and learning-institution failure on key tests. This complements Krepinevich and diplomatic/Vietnamese perspectives; cross-links [[army-concept]] and [[learning-institution]].

**Daddis reassessment (2014; Range 01 + Range 02 grounding)**: In *Westmoreland's War*, Gregory Daddis offers a major counter-narrative grounded in MACV records and contemporary doctrine. He contends the "attrition" label is a myth: "the word has become synonymous with American strategy in the Vietnam War" yet "evolved into an unquestioned truth" via reductive shorthand ("search and destroy," "body counts"). Instead, "Westmoreland developed a comprehensive military strategy for Vietnam, one not confined to simple attrition of enemy forces." "It is possible... to devise a sound strategy and still lose." 

Westmoreland confronted "not one battle, but three to fight: first, to contain a growing enemy conventional threat; second, to develop the Republic of Vietnam’s Armed Forces (RVNAF); and third, to pacify and protect the peasants in the South Vietnamese countryside." The command tracked population security, economic development, political stability, and HES metrics alongside body counts. "Political, economic, and psychological victory is equally important..." (Introduction, Preface)

Daddis situates this in Cold War context: containment, decolonization, Maoist theory, and limited-war doctrine led US Army officers (including Westmoreland at West Point) to study and codify broad counterinsurgency approaches encompassing "political, social, and economic rather than military problems" — "civic actions," "pacification committees," and "revolutionary development." Early transition (MAAG to MACV, Williams/McGarr) already featured "clearing and holding" and integrated political-military plans; "by the time William Westmoreland arrived in Saigon in mid-1964, key US officials had been debating strategic issues for nearly five years." While not denying execution shortfalls or post-colonial limits, Daddis shows the strategy was far more multifaceted than the dominant critiques allow. ## Capstone from Nagl Part IV (Range 06): Why Culture Precluded Learning

Nagl's conclusions frame the U.S. experience as the classic case of cultural mismatch. The organizational culture “that makes an institution effective in one area may blind it to the possibility that its strengths in that field are crippling deficiencies in a different situation.” Conventional-war optimization (firepower, large formations, centralized control) directly crippled adaptation to COIN. “The U.S. Army in Vietnam is a classic example.”

Even when “organizational failures were obvious to much of the army,” “no consensus emerged among the army leadership that change was required.” “Only organizational self-awareness can change organizational culture.” Senior officers quoted: Army culture “can also be a liability”; “innovators are forced out”; promotion of “people who aren’t like us” is required for health; generals must listen “even to majors and colonels,” foster open thought, curiosity, and humility.

Contrast with British: their culture (colonial policing) already embodied the flexibility needed. Nagl cross-references Krepinevich heavily for the detailed U.S. narrative while supplying the cultural mechanism. Contrasts Galula’s prescriptive “how” with the “why some armies can” analysis. Implications: in the post-Cold War era of internal wars, armies “must learn how to eat soup with a knife.” The process is uncomfortable but essential.
