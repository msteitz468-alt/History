# The Vietnam War (the Indochina Wars, 1945–1975)

The **Indochina wars** were a thirty-year struggle for Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia that, in
Fredrik Logevall's words in [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|*The Cambridge History of the Cold
War*, Vol. II]], "occupies a central place in the international history of the twentieth
century" — capturing "in microcosm all of the grand political forces" of the age:
**colonialism, nationalism, communism, and democratic-capitalism**. Both an **East–West**
([[cold-war|Cold War]]) and a **North–South** ([[decolonization]]) conflict, it became the
era's great hot war and the most damaging US defeat of the Cold War.

## Narrative

- **The First Indochina War (1946–54).** After the Japanese collapse, the Communist-led
  **Vietminh** under **Ho Chi Minh** proclaimed independence (1945), but France sought to
  restore colonial rule; large-scale war broke out in late 1946. The conflict was at first a
  **Franco-Vietnamese** affair (Stalin "showed scant interest"), but French diplomacy
  reframed it as an East–West struggle, and after the Communist victory in China (1949) and
  the [[korean-war-1950|Korean War]], the **US bankrolled the French** under the logic of
  **[[containment]]** and the **domino theory**. France's defeat at **Dien Bien Phu (1954)**
  ended its empire in Indochina; the **Geneva Accords** partitioned Vietnam at the 17th
  parallel.
- **The American War (c. 1955–1973).** The US backed an anti-communist South under Ngo Dinh
  Diem; faced with a southern insurgency (the **Viet Cong/NLF**) backed by the North, it
  escalated from advisers to a major war after the **Gulf of Tonkin** episode (1964),
  committing over half a million troops and a vast bombing campaign. The **Tet Offensive**
  (1968), though a military defeat for the North, shattered US public support. Nixon's
  policy of **"Vietnamization"** and the widening of the war into Cambodia and Laos preceded
  the **Paris Peace Accords (1973)** and US withdrawal.
- **The fall of Saigon (1975).** North Vietnamese forces overran the South; Saigon fell on
  **30 April 1975**, **reunifying Vietnam under communism**. Neighboring Cambodia fell to the
  genocidal **Khmer Rouge** the same year.

## Causal & Consequence Analysis

- `caused_by:` Vietnamese **anticolonial nationalism** and the French (then American) refusal
  to accept it; the **Cold War overlay** that turned a decolonization struggle into a
  superpower test (Washington acted on "worst-case assumptions" despite finding "no evidence
  of mass popular support for Communism," driven by the domino theory).
- `produced:` a communist, reunified Vietnam; a profound **US strategic and moral defeat**
  ("the Vietnam syndrome"); **millions of dead** (mostly Vietnamese); the Cambodian
  catastrophe; and a lasting demonstration of the **limits of superpower military power**
  against a determined nationalist insurgency.

## Historiography

Logevall and the post-revisionist consensus stress that the war's roots lay in
**decolonization, not Soviet expansion** — Ho was "too independent and nationalist-oriented"
for Stalin's taste, and the US misread an anticolonial nationalism as a Cold War domino.
Debate continues over whether the war was "winnable," and over the relative weight of Cold
War ideology versus local Vietnamese dynamics.
