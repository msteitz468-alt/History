# The Muslim Conquest of Sicily (827–902)

## Narrative

In June **827** an Aghlabid force under **Asad ibn al-Furat** landed in Byzantine **Sicily**,
reportedly at the invitation of a rebel Byzantine commander. The conquest was protracted — fought
against Byzantine resistance and amid chronic strife within the Muslim camp (between the Aghlabid
governors and rival Sicilian families) — but inexorable: Palermo fell early, and the fall of
**Taormina (902)** completed Muslim control of the island. From Sicilian and African bases, Muslim
forces also raided and briefly held footholds on the Italian mainland, most notably the **emirate of
Bari** (c. 847–871) and the spectacular **sack of the basilicas outside Rome (846)**, including
**St Peter's**. The reconquest of Bari (871) was a signal achievement of the emperor **Louis II**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the expansionism of the [[umayyad-caliphate|Aghlabid emirate]] of Ifriqiya (Qayrawan),
  for which rich, nearby Sicily was an obvious target as Berber resistance in Africa declined
- `contributed_to:` an invitation from a rebel Byzantine commander and the weakness and division of
  Byzantine and Lombard southern Italy
- `enabled:` continuous Muslim naval dominance in the central Mediterranean

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **Muslim Sicily**, which endured until the Norman conquest of the eleventh century and
  became a distinctive zone of Arab-Islamic culture and agriculture
- `produced:` Muslim raiding bases on the Italian mainland (the emirate of Bari) and the trauma of the
  **846 sack of Rome's basilicas**, which spurred the fortification of the Leonine City around St Peter's
- `contributed_to:` the militarisation of papal and Lombard southern Italy and the interventions of the
  Carolingian emperor Louis II

## Historiography

NCMH II (Kennedy, Ch. 10) notes that Muslim Sicily, unlike Muslim Spain, has left a thin record of its
internal social and administrative life; the conquest narrative survives in both Arabic and Greek texts
covering 827–965 but is dominated by military events and the chronic internal conflict between the
Aghlabids of Qayrawan and the Sicilian Muslim factions.

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb, *The Race for Paradise* (2014)]] stresses **process length over a single start year**: Muslim raids began early, but “the island was not fully conquered until the middle of the ninth century by the ʿAbbasid governors of Tunisia.” When the Fatimids conquered Tunisia in the early tenth century, Sicily was already “a virtual dependency of the province” and passed to Fatimid claim—aligning with this page’s **827–902** span as the conventional start-to-completion window, while underlining that full control was a mid-century achievement under Tunisian ʿAbbasid governors rather than the 827 landing alone. See also [[sicily]] for the later Kalbid autonomy, Fatimid fragmentation, and Norman conquest that ended Muslim rule.
