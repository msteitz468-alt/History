# Lelantine War

Conflict tradition between [[places/chalcis|Chalcis]] and [[places/eretria|Eretria]] over the fertile Lelantine plain of Euboea, usually placed in the late eighth / early seventh century BCE. [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]] uses the episode as a **method case**: a vivid composite assembled from Archilochus, Herodotus, Thucydides, Aristotle, Strabo, Plutarch, and a few graves does **not** survive source-critical stress tests for a single “Panhellenic” war.

## Narrative

In the conventional modern reconstruction that Hall first summarizes and then dismantles, [[places/chalcis|Chalcis]] and [[places/eretria|Eretria]] lay about 24 km apart on either side of the Lelantine plain. The two cities initially cooperated: Strabo reports a joint foundation of [[places/pithecusae|Pithecusae]] in the second quarter of the eighth century. In the final decades of that century, amid expanding populations, they came to blows “over possession of the plain that lay between them.”

Thucydides (1.15) presents the entire Greek world as divided between the two sides—a collective effort unmatched until the great wars of the fifth century. Modern narratives stack allies from Herodotus and later notices: Miletus with Eretria against Samos with Chalcis; Corinth with Chalcis; Megara with Eretria; Thessalian horsemen with Chalcis; and colonial mother-city patterns that extend the roster still further.

History does not record the outcome. Traditional arguments that Eretria fared worse cite the apparent destruction of [[places/lefkandi|Lefkandi]] around 700 (sometimes identified with Strabo’s “Old Eretria”) and the end of Eretrian–Chalcidian overseas cooperation, with Chalcis dominating western ventures thereafter. Hall challenges both the “destruction” and the Old Eretria identification (see [[places/lefkandi|Lefkandi]]).

Literary color pieces include Hesiod’s attendance at funeral contests for “wise” Amphidamas at Chalcis; Plutarch’s claim that Amphidamas “inflicted many ills upon the Eretrians and fell in the battles for the Lelantine plain”; and Plutarch’s story of Thessalian Kleomakhos aiding Chalcis and receiving an agora burial under a tall pillar. At Eretria’s West Gate, an anonymous warrior burial ca. 720 with Homeric-style funerary honors has been read as war-related elite display.

**None of this composite is secure.** Full deconstruction belongs on [[controversies/lelantine-war-historicity]]. Hall’s closing verdict: “we do not know when – or even whether – the Lelantine War occurred.”

## Causal Analysis

- `preceded_by:` late Geometric expansion and overseas ventures of Euboean poleis (e.g. Pithecusae); elite competition and plain agriculture as structural background, not proven triggers.
- `contributed_to:` later alliance memories (Eretria–Miletus; Chalcis–Samos) that may have been retrojected from fifth-century politics (Hall on Herodotus 5.99).
- Do **not** assert `caused_by:` a “Panhellenic” alliance system — that is part of the modern fantasy under critique.

## Consequence Analysis

- Local elite burial practices at Eretria’s West Gate have been read as war heroes; Hall notes Snodgrass’s “positivist fallacy”: archaeologically visible need not equal historically significant, and the burials might mark any of thousands of unknown Eretrian episodes.
- Historiographically: model of how modern reconstruction over-combines isolated notices spanning some nine centuries (Hesiod/Archilochus/Theognis to Aelian).
- `enabled:` later rhetorical use of an “early great war” (Thucydides’ parallel structure with Troy and the Persian War).

## Actors

[[places/chalcis|Chalcis]] (Hippobotai elite tradition); [[places/eretria|Eretria]] (Hippeis elite tradition); alleged allies in late and modern tradition only; [[places/euboea|Euboea]] as regional setting.

## Historiography

Primary discussion: [[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall, *Archaic Greek World* (2014)]] Ch.1. Positions range from accepting a substantial multi-ally war (following Thucydides’ rhetoric) to treating the “Lelantine War” as largely a modern scholarly construct. Hall applies tests of temporal proximity, independent tradition, intentionality, and contextual fit: late authors elaborate earlier ones we already possess; alliance rosters mix non-contemporary hostilities; Aristotle’s “cavalry war” may be inferred from elite class names (Hippeis / Hippobotai) rather than Archilochus; dating Hesiod via the war is circular. See [[controversies/lelantine-war-historicity]].
