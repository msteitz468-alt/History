# The Francoist Repression, 1936–c. 1944

The systematic political violence of the Nationalist side during and after the [[spanish-civil-war-1936|Spanish Civil War]] — mass executions in the wartime zone, the postwar juridical purge under the Law of Political Responsibilities, and the prison/penal-labor system — treated here through Payne (1987), ch. 11, alongside the parallel Republican-zone ("Red") terror with which it is inseparably entangled in the sources and the historiography.

## Narrative

**Wartime terror, both zones.** Violence against civilians began in the first days of the July 1936 rising in both zones and "atrocity then followed atrocity." Payne rejects the common partisan distinction (Red terror spontaneous and soon suppressed; White terror centralized and systematic): in the early months the Nationalist repression was *not* centrally organized, while the Popular Front zone's killing "had more planning and organization than it is given credit for," and Republican-zone executions continued past 1936 at reduced volume. Mola's Instrucción Reservada No. 1 (April 1936) ordered action "violent in the extreme," with "exemplary punishments" for all leaders of hostile parties and unions; Mola himself remarked, "A year ago I would have trembled to sign a death sentence. Now I sign more than ten a day with an easy conscience." Queipo de Llano broadcast the Seville reprisals nightly to terrorize leftist listeners. Franco set the tone by approving the execution of his own first cousin, Major Ricardo de la Puente Bahamonde, who had resisted the rising at Tetuán; 25 of Spain's 85 active-list generals were executed during the war (17 in the Republican zone, 8 in the Nationalist).

**Instruments.** Total martial law from 28 July 1936 (not repealed until 7 April 1948); summary courts-martial applying the inverted charge of "military rebellion" to those who had refused to join a military rebellion. Executions were carried out largely by the Civil Guard and Falangist militia; Hedilla's Falangist leadership repeatedly (September 1936 – January 1937) tried to restrain Falangist participation and protect *gente de alpargatas* — ordinary workers — noting that in many areas there were "rightists who are worse than the Reds." Public-spectacle executions drew even a Nationalist newspaper's protest at children attending (Valladolid, September 1936), and the pro-Nationalist bishop of Pamplona preached "No More Bloodshed" (November 1936) — asking only that killings stay within the (summary) juridical process. Even Nazi and Fascist observers were sometimes appalled; Farinacci reported to Mussolini in March 1937 that "Red and Nationalist atrocities are equivalent here... almost a sport."

**Centralization.** After Franco's elevation (October 1936), and decisively after the Málaga massacres of February 1937 horrified Spain's Italian allies, a regularized court-martial system was imposed: from about the end of March 1937 all capital sentences went to Martínez Fuset's Asesoría Jurídica for Franco's personal review — Franco initialing lists *E* (Enterado, approved) or *C* (Conmutado), occasionally adding *garrote y prensa* for heinous common crimes. Payne: of the two terrors, the White was "probably the more effective, not because it killed more people but because it was more concerted."

**Postwar systematization.** Victory brought "neither reconciliation nor political disarmament" but more efficient repression: the Law of Political Responsibilities (9 February 1939, retroactive to October 1934, covering even "grave passivity"), the Law for the Suppression of Masonry and Communism (1 March 1940 — anti-Masonry being Franco's "number-one individual mania"), and the 1943 decree making any public-order infringement "military rebellion." The prison population peaked at 270,719 at the close of 1939 (plus camps processing 400,000+ surrendered Republican troops); the "victors and vanquished" division was institutionalized (the DNI identity card, 1938). Penal labor with sentence remission ("redemption through work" — Franco, 31 December 1939: liquidation of hatreds "must not be accomplished in the liberal manner, with enormous and disastrous amnesties... It must be Christian") built projects including the Valle de los Caídos, later Franco's tomb. Reprisals could be ferocious: after the killing of a military police inspector (July 1939), at least 63 of 67 arrested members of the United Socialist Youth were executed, including eleven young women — the "Trece Rosas" case among them. Staged amnesties from October 1939 ran the prison population down to c. 43,800 by end-1945 (c. 17,000 political); about half of some 50,000 death sentences passed were commuted by December 1943; executions largely ceased after 1944.

## The Numbers (contested)

Payne's anchor is Ramón Salas Larrazábal, *Pérdidas de la guerra* (1977), the only systematic registry-based study, with Payne's own corrections:

| Category | Figure (Salas, adj. Payne) |
|---|---|
| Nationalist executions during the war | 35,021 recorded; likely ≥42,000 with under-registration (+20% per the Navarre re-study) |
| Postwar executions (1939–c. 1945/50) | ~28,000 (Salas's revised figure; 28,000–30,000 to 1950) |
| **Total Nationalist executions** | **~70,000–72,000** |
| Republican-zone executions during the war | 72,344 recorded; perhaps ~10% too high |
| Combat deaths (both armies) | ~120,000, roughly equal per side |
| Total violent deaths, war years | under 300,000 (~1% of population of 25 million) — the *millón de muertos* is myth |
| Excess deaths from disease/malnutrition | ~165,000 wartime + ~159,000 in 1940–43 (heavier in the Republican zone/postwar poor) |
| Permanent Republican exile | ~162,000; ≥5,000 Spaniards died in German camps after deportation from France |

Highest provincial execution counts in the Nationalist zone: Córdoba and Málaga (3,864 each), then Zaragoza, Badajoz, Seville, Granada, Oviedo. Regional studies (Solé i Sabaté on Catalonia; Galicia c. +30%; Moreno Gómez's 7,700 for Córdoba; Gibson's 5,000+ for Granada) variously confirm or exceed Salas; Reig Tapia mounts the severest methodological critique. Payne's comparative placement: middle range among twentieth-century revolutionary civil wars — worse than Hungary 1919, less than Russia, proportionately about equal to Finland 1918.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

- caused_by: [[spanish-civil-war-1936]] — but with roots in pre-war reciprocal political violence (both left and Falangist).
- Solé i Sabaté's verdict, quoted by Payne as that of "the most thorough scholarly investigator": "The repression was constant, regular, and methodical. It was not arbitrary in character, even though it often seemed so. The repression was frightful, but it was also selective and rational."
- produced: the postwar "victors and vanquished" order that bound Franco's coalition to him and cowed the defeated for a generation — Payne treats this as a foundation of regime stability into the 1950s.
- contrasts_with: Stalinist/Nazi liquidation — Payne insists it was not applied by involuntary category of race or class, "recognized limits and normally respected its own rules," and grew milder yearly; he judges a victorious Communist-hegemonic Republic would "undoubtedly" have been worse. This comparative apologia is itself part of the historiographical dispute (below).

## Historiography

- **Sources**: wartime records for the early months barely exist; formalized post-March 1937 records were apparently destroyed in the 1940s "for reasons of political prudence." Registry-of-deaths methodology (Salas) vs. local exhumation/testimony studies (Colectivo Afán etc.) yield diverging totals.
- **Positions**: (1) Nationalist executions far exceeded Republican (Jackson; later the post-2000 Spanish "memoria histórica" school, which pushed documented Francoist totals well above Salas); (2) rough parity, with the Nationalist total higher only via postwar executions (Thomas; Payne's own earlier works; Payne 1987 lands at near-exact parity ~70–72,000 each); (3) Nationalist propaganda's half-million Red-terror victims, officially reduced by the late regime to 61,000 including ~7,000 clergy — rejected by all scholarship. Payne's comparative framing and his counterfactual ("no particular reason to think that conditions would have been significantly better had the Republic triumphed") are exactly the points on which Preston (*The Spanish Holocaust*, 2012) and the memoria-histórica literature attack him; positions preserved without adjudication.
