# Pontiac's War (1763–1766)

## Narrative

Immediate Native reaction to France’s expulsion from the Ohio/Great Lakes after [[seven-years-war-1756]], already alarmed by General Jeffrey Amherst’s postwar policies. Salisbury (CHNPA I.1 ch. 7).

### Background grievances

Amherst ordered cessation of present-giving at posts—including food and ammunition needed to hunt—demanded return of all British captives including adoptees content in their new homes, and seized Seneca land for officers. Connecticut settlers invaded Wyoming 1762, expelled Delawares, burned Teedyuscung alive in his cabin. Epidemics and dissension; many hoped for French return.

### Neolin and Pontiac

A new religious movement of **Neolin** (“Delaware Prophet”) fused Native traditions and Christian revivalism, calling for united rejection of European ties to restore material well-being, autonomy, and cultural integrity; anti-European resentment focused on the British. Ottawa war leader **Pontiac** was among the most articulate. Spring–summer 1763 Great Lakes and upper Ohio Indians attacked occupied forts starting with Pontiac’s Detroit assault. Salisbury stresses he was “hardly the sole instigator implied in ‘Pontiac’s Rebellion’” but helped coordinate near Detroit then sought Illinois/Mississippi enlistment.

### Course and biological warfare at Fort Pitt

Most assaults succeeded, but key British positions—Detroit, Niagara, Fort Pitt—held. **Fort Pitt** was retained after English officers presented smallpox blankets to a Delaware delegation urging its surrender, “setting off an epidemic that spread among upper Ohio Indians during the ensuing year.” Food and ammunition shortages, Native dissension, and recognition that the French were not returning led most to cease fighting by 1765; Pontiac submitted to Sir William Johnson the next year.

### Imperial sequel

October 1763 royal Proclamation: land west of the Appalachian Proclamation Line remained Native, alienable only with Crown approval; licensed traders; superintendents of Indian affairs to enforce—but limited effect; trade and settlement largely unenforced. 1768 Fort Stanwix: William Johnson persuaded the Iroquois to cede vast [[shawnee]], [[lenape-delaware]], and Cherokee Ohio hunting lands the Six Nations had never occupied or controlled—setting conditions for Dunmore’s War and Revolutionary frontier violence.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: temporary multi-nation military surge; failure to take key forts; smallpox epidemic from Fort Pitt blankets among upper Ohio Indians
- produced: Proclamation of 1763 as imperial regulatory attempt (weak enforcement)
- enabled: later Shawnee-led anti-Stanwix coalition efforts and Dunmore’s War 1774
- contributed_to: British–colonial tension over western lands feeding into the American Revolution
- did not restore French presence or reverse long-run colonial demographic pressure

## Actors

Pontiac (Ottawa); Neolin (Delaware Prophet); Great Lakes and upper Ohio nations including [[ojibwe]], [[lenape-delaware]], [[shawnee]]; British officers Amherst, and later Johnson; Delaware delegation at Fort Pitt.

## Historiography

Name “Pontiac’s Rebellion/War” over-personalizes a multi-nation movement (Salisbury’s caution). Ethnohistory stresses nativist religion (Dowd *Spirited Resistance*), gift-economy rupture, and biological warfare at Fort Pitt as documented policy choice. Ties to Proclamation Line and Fort Stanwix place the war inside the collapse of multipolar Indian diplomacy rather than as an isolated “uprising.”
