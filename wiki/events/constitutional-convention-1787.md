# Constitutional Convention (1787)

## Narrative

The Federal Convention met in Philadelphia from May to September 1787 to revise the Articles of Confederation but instead produced an entirely new frame of government. George Washington presided; James Madison was the primary architect of the Virginia Plan that shaped the final document.

## Causal Analysis

The immediate drivers were the fiscal and diplomatic impotence of the Confederation Congress and internal unrest (Shays' Rebellion). Deeper causes included the desire of nationalists for a government capable of regulating commerce, paying debts, and projecting power.

## Consequence Analysis

The Constitution created a stronger federal union but embedded compromises that protected slavery: the three-fifths clause for representation and taxation, a fugitive slave clause, and a twenty-year bar on congressional prohibition of the international slave trade. These provisions made the "more perfect union" possible only by deferring the great contradiction. Indian policy and western expansion were also shaped by the new framework.

## Historiography

Traditional accounts celebrate the Convention as the triumph of reasoned compromise and federalism (Farrand, Beeman). Revisionist scholarship (e.g. Ellis, *The Great Contradiction* 2025) emphasizes how the slavery compromises were not reluctant necessities but active choices that entrenched racial hierarchy while enabling union. "Total silence on the slavery question was itself a loud statement that slavery was a taboo topic... the only way to end slavery was to create a national government empowered to make domestic policy for the states, but placing emancipation on the agenda in Philadelphia instantly destroyed any realistic prospect..." The four key compromises (three-fifths for representation/"Negro president" effect on 1800 election; slave trade extended to 1808 with 200k+ imports; fugitive slave clause passed unanimously; territorial understandings) locked in the contradiction. Franklin: "I expect no better, and I am not sure it is not the best." Primary sources: Madison's Notes; founders' papers. Ellis frames as Shakespearean (avoidable) tragedy.

**From Ellis (2025):** "if there had ever been a chance to put slavery on the road to extinction south of the Potomac, the convention had just missed it." "Future abolitionists could and did characterize [the compromises] as a 'covenant with death.'" [grounded in source lines ~615-780]
