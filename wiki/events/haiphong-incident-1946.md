# Haiphong Incident (November 1946)

The Haiphong Incident (20–23 November 1946) was the immediate trigger for the full-scale First Indochina War. A minor clash over a seized junk escalated into a French bombardment that killed thousands of Vietnamese civilians and gave France control of the vital port of Haiphong, the “lungs of Tonkin.”

## Narrative

On 20 November a French patrol ship in Haiphong harbor seized a Chinese junk suspected of carrying gasoline (contraband in French eyes). Viet Minh forces intervened. Shooting broke out. By nightfall, French reports listed 7 French dead and 240 Vietnamese/Chinese dead.

French commanders, led locally by Colonel Dèbes and with orders from General Valluy (and support from High Commissioner d’Argenlieu), decided to exploit the incident. Valluy instructed Dèbes to “take maximum advantage… take complete control of Haiphong and force the Vietnamese government and army into submission.”

On 23 November at 10:05 a.m., French forces began a prolonged naval and aerial bombardment of the Vietnamese and Chinese sections of the city. The attack continued over two days. The Vietnamese/Chinese quarters were reduced to rubble; civilians were strafed as they fled. Contemporary accounts (including from French officer Henri Martin) described the destruction as near-total for the non-European parts of the city. Casualties were “in the thousands” — the exact figure was never firmly established.

By 28 November the French controlled Haiphong and Lang Son. The incident removed any remaining illusion of negotiated coexistence in the north.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** French determination to retain economic and military control of Tonkin’s primary port after months of deteriorating relations and Viet Minh consolidation in the countryside. The seizure of the junk provided a pretext that French officers had been seeking.

**contributed_to:** The bombardment destroyed any lingering Vietnamese trust in French good faith and radicalized opinion. It also demonstrated French willingness to use overwhelming firepower against civilians when politically useful.

**preceded_by:** The 1945–46 period of uneasy coexistence after the August Revolution and the return of French forces; repeated failed negotiations (including the March 1946 Ho–Sainteny accords and the Fontainebleau conference).

## Consequence Analysis

The Haiphong Incident directly fed into the Battle of Hanoi (19 December 1946). Viet Minh leaders concluded that France intended a military reconquest rather than genuine negotiation. The destruction of Haiphong’s Vietnamese quarters and the high civilian toll became a powerful recruiting tool.

The event also revealed French internal dynamics: d’Argenlieu and Valluy operated with significant autonomy from Paris and appeared determined to provoke a decisive confrontation while the French public and the Americans were still distracted or sympathetic to the colonial position.

## Actors

- **French side**: Colonel Dèbes (local commander), General Valluy (overall military commander), High Commissioner Georges Thierry d’Argenlieu (“Bloody Monk”).
- **Viet Minh/DRV**: Local tu ve (self-defense) forces; national leadership (Ho Chi Minh, Giap) who used the incident to frame the coming war as defensive.

## Historiography

Logevall (2012) is explicit that while the Vietnamese may have fired the first shots on 19 December in Hanoi, “ultimately France bears primary responsibility” for the escalation. The Haiphong bombardment is presented as a deliberate French choice to break the political impasse by force. Vietnamese histories treat it as a clear war crime and the opening act of French aggression. Some French accounts at the time and later minimized civilian deaths or blamed “Viet Minh provocation.”

The incident is often overshadowed by the December fighting in Hanoi and the later conventional battles, but Logevall and other scholars see it as the point of no return.
