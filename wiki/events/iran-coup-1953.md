# Iran Coup of 1953 (Operation Ajax)

## Narrative
In August 1953 the American Central Intelligence Agency (Operation Ajax) and British MI6 jointly planned and executed the overthrow of Iranian Prime Minister [[mossadegh-mohammad|Mohammad Mosaddegh]], restoring the full authority of Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. The operation followed Mosaddegh's May 1, 1951 nationalization of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company and his subsequent legal victory over Britain at the International Court of Justice (June 1952). CIA officer Kermit Roosevelt Jr. (Near East division chief) entered Iran under the alias "James Lochridge" with roughly $100,000 in Iranian currency, while MI6 officer Norman Darbyshire directed street-mob mobilization through the Rashidian brothers and recruited General Fazlollah Zahedi as coup leader. Total cost: roughly $20 million from the CIA and £700,000 from MI6.

A first coup attempt (August 15–16) failed when General Taghi Riyahi's countercoup forces arrested the plotters. A second attempt on August 19 succeeded through CIA/MI6-funded mobs, bribed clerics (including Ayatollah Kashani), and pro-shah military units; the assault on Mosaddegh's residence killed roughly 300 Iranians (McCoy's figure). Prior to the coup itself, the CIA had already run Operation BEDAMN — stay-behind tribal networks and anti-Tudeh psychological warfare, including payments to Kashani documented in a March 1951 memo. An April 1953 assassination of Iran's national police chief, General Mahmoud Afshartous, orchestrated by Darbyshire, enabled pro-shah police appointments ahead of the coup.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` Mosaddegh's nationalization of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company and the resulting collapse of British oil revenue, which drove Britain to seek US assistance in his removal.
- `contributed_to:` Cold War fears in Washington that instability in Iran could open the door to Tudeh party influence and Soviet alignment.
- `enabled:` prior CIA network-building in Iran (Operation BEDAMN) and the April 1953 assassination of the national police chief, which cleared the way for pro-shah forces to seize the streets on the second attempt.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` restoration of the Shah's full authority and a new oil consortium (40% American companies, 40% British Petroleum, down from Britain's prior near-monopoly).
- `produced:` formation of SAVAK (with CIA assistance), which rounded up more than 3,000 Tudeh members, tortured many, and executed roughly 40 (McCoy's figures).
- `contributed_to:` (per McCoy's interpretive thesis, not settled consensus — see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]) the long-run trajectory of repression, the 1963 anti-shah riots (suppressed with rifle fire killing hundreds), the 1977–78 unrest and general strike (troops fired on crowds, killing roughly 1,500 per McCoy), the Shah's flight into exile in January 1979, and Ayatollah Khomeini's establishment of an Islamic theocracy.
- `followed_by:` decades later, Secretary of State Madeleine Albright publicly acknowledged the coup "was clearly a setback for Iran's political development."

## Actors
- **[[mossadegh-mohammad|Mohammad Mosaddegh]]** — nationalist prime minister, overthrown.
- **[[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]]** — Kermit Roosevelt Jr. directed the second coup attempt; later awarded the National Security Medal by Eisenhower as (per McCoy) the first CIA officer to accomplish regime change through covert action; later became a vice president of Gulf Oil.
- Norman Darbyshire (MI6) — directed mob mobilization and the recruitment of General Zahedi.
- Mohammad Reza Pahlavi — restored Shah; on hearing of Mosaddegh's overthrow reportedly said "Can it be true?... I knew it! I knew it! They love me," to which US Ambassador Loy Henderson replied "The Shah is living in a dream world."

## Historiography
McCoy's account draws on CIA's own official history, Darbyshire interview transcripts, and the Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS) series, all declassified decades after the event. McCoy frames Iran (alongside [[guatemala-coup-1954]]) as establishing a durable Washington "mystique" that cheap covert regime-change was reliably effective — an institutional-overconfidence thesis he argues later shaped, and undermined, the [[bay-of-pigs-1961|Bay of Pigs]] planning. This causal claim connecting the coup's "success" narrative to later CIA overreach is McCoy's interpretation, not a documented mechanism; see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]. Source: Alfred W. McCoy, *Cold War on Five Continents* (2024).
