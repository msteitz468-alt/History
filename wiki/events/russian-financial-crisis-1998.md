# 1998 Russian Financial Crisis
## Narrative
On August 17, 1998, facing a severe shortage of hard currency, the Russian government (led by Prime Minister Sergei Kiriyenko) and the central bank announced a devaluation of the ruble, a suspension of payments on short-term Treasury bills (GKOs), and a moratorium on commercial banks' foreign debt payments. "In 1997 it issued state bonds to balance the budget. The terms were hopelessly disadvantageous to the government if ever the rouble fell under severe pressure... the dreaded run on the rouble duly occurred in August 1998. Russia unilaterally defaulted on its international loan repayments" (line 3684). "Industrial production continued to fall. Output in the same year was down by eighteen per cent on 1991" (earlier data illustrating trajectory). This sudden default triggered a complete collapse of the Russian financial sector. Private banks failed, imports dried up, and hyperinflation returned, wiping out the savings of the emerging middle class. In the political fallout, Yeltsin dismissed Kiriyenko and, after his proposal to reinstate Viktor Chernomyrdin was twice rejected by the Duma, appointed Evgeny Primakov as Prime Minister. "The devaluation of the rouble increased the costs of imported goods and inadvertently provided a stimulus to domestic manufacturing and agriculture... By 1999 the beginnings of economic recovery were unmistakable" (lines 3686–3687).

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` **systemic failure to restructure the economy**, relying instead on a "pyramid scheme" of short-term state borrowing (GKOs) and international loans to stay afloat (Line 7371); the **decline in world prices for oil and natural gas**, which made up over 50% of Russian export revenues; and the **contagion of the 1997 Asian financial crisis**, which led foreign investors to panic and withdraw capital from emerging markets.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the **devaluation of the ruble** (which lost over 60% of its value within weeks) and the return of rapid inflation.
- `produced:` the **collapse of major private banks** and the **wipeout of savings** of the urban middle class and entrepreneurs who had prospered under privatization.
- `produced:` the **ouster of the Kiriyenko government** and the appointment of **Evgeny Primakov** (Line 7375), marking a political shift toward a compromise cabinet backed by the communist-dominated Duma.
- `preceded_by:` the **withdrawal of foreign investors** and a decline in international loans, with the IMF refusing further assistance.
- `followed_by:` a temporary **de-dollarization** and a boost to local manufacturing, which became more price-competitive due to the devalued ruble.

## Actors
- **Boris Yeltsin**, who dismissed the reformist cabinet; **Sergei Kiriyenko**, the prime minister during the default; and **Evgeny Primakov**, who stabilized the government. See [[yeltsin-boris]].

## Historiography
Riasanovsky notes that the 1998 default was a devastating blow to the post-Soviet market reforms, demonstrating the unsustainability of a financial system built on high interest rates, unpaid wages, tax evasion, and foreign debt (Line 7371).
