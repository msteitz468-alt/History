# Taiwan Expedition (1874)

The Taiwan Expedition (*Taiwan Shuppei*, also called the *Taiwan Incident*) was Japan's first overseas military deployment as a modern state — a punitive expedition dispatched in May 1874 to southern Taiwan following the killing of 54 shipwrecked Ryūkyū sailors by indigenous Paiwan people in 1871. It was the first overt act of Japanese imperialism and a direct outgrowth of the factional struggles that followed the **[[seikanron-1873|seikanron of 1873]]**.

## Narrative

In 1871, a Ryūkyū (Okinawan) vessel was wrecked off the southern tip of Taiwan. Fifty-four of the survivors were killed by indigenous Taiwanese. When Japan demanded that China punish the perpetrators, Chinese officials replied that the indigenous peoples of southern Taiwan were "outside the bounds of civilization" and beyond Chinese jurisdiction — a statement Japanese diplomats immediately weaponized, arguing that if China could not control the territory, Japan had the right to do so.

The Ryūkyū islands were at this time in an ambiguous position: traditionally a tributary of both China and the Satsuma domain of Japan, they had not been formally incorporated into the Japanese state. The Taiwan incident gave Japan an opportunity to assert that Ryūkyū islanders were *Japanese subjects*, making China's failure to protect them Japan's diplomatic grievance.

Japanese forces under Admiral Saigō Tsugumichi landed in southern Taiwan in May 1874 and conducted a military campaign against indigenous villages. The expedition was poorly organized and suffered more casualties from disease than from fighting. Nevertheless, it succeeded diplomatically: China, under pressure and wishing to avoid escalation, agreed to pay an indemnity of 500,000 taels — acknowledging, at least implicitly, that the Ryūkyūans were Japanese subjects. Japan withdrew from Taiwan.

**[[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]]** traveled personally to Beijing to negotiate the settlement, in what was his most prominent display of personal diplomatic leadership. The successful outcome strengthened his political position at home.

## Causal Analysis

The expedition was **enabled** by the seikanron crisis of 1873: with the Korea invasion blocked, a Taiwan expedition offered a more limited, lower-risk outlet for expansionist pressure. The legal pretext — China's own statement that the indigenous peoples were beyond its jurisdiction — was a genuine diplomatic coup, turning Chinese evasion into a Japanese opening. The expedition was thus both a response to domestic pressure and an opportunistic use of international law as an instrument of state expansion.

Keene's [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] mentions the expedition several times as background context: "members of the Ryūkyū domain had been killed by Taiwanese savages" is how Kido Takayoshi summarizes the precipitating incident when comparing it (unfavorably) to the Kanghwa Island incident of 1875, calling Taiwan "much less serious" because, unlike Korea, no permanent Japanese residents lived there.

## Consequence Analysis

The expedition's most important consequences were **legal and diplomatic** rather than military:

1. **The Ryūkyū question resolved**: China's payment of the indemnity effectively conceded that Ryūkyū islanders were Japanese subjects. Japan used this legal foundation to pressure the Ryūkyū kingdom to break ties with China (1875), and ultimately to abolish the kingdom and annex Okinawa as a Japanese prefecture (1879). The text documents this process in detail: the orders to King Shō Tai, the deployment of Matsuda Michiyuki, and the eventual forced removal of the king to Tokyo in 1879.

2. **Template for imperial expansion**: The Taiwan expedition established the pattern Japan would use again: a local incident involving Japanese subjects, a legal claim based on the weakness or non-jurisdiction of an Asian empire, military deployment, and diplomatic settlement that advanced territorial or political control. The Sino-Japanese War of 1894–95 would follow similar logic, at vastly larger scale.

3. **Ōkubo's authority reinforced**: Personal management of the Beijing negotiations enhanced Ōkubo's standing. The expedition demonstrated that the internalist-developmentalist faction that had blocked the Korea invasion could nonetheless deliver military and diplomatic success when the opportunity was right.

## Actors

- **[[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]]**: Led the diplomatic settlement in Beijing; the expedition was a strategic success for his faction despite tactical difficulties.
- **Saigō Tsugumichi**: Military commander of the expedition (younger brother of Saigō Takamori).
- **[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]**: Formally authorized the expedition; it occurred during his period of close collaboration with Ōkubo, Kido, and Iwakura.
- **Ryūkyū Kingdom / King Shō Tai**: The victims whose deaths provided the pretext; the kingdom's fate was sealed by the legal consequences of the indemnity.

## Historiography

Western historians have generally treated the Taiwan expedition as the inaugural act of Japanese imperialism — the first moment Japan deployed military force to coerce a neighboring power. Japanese historiography has debated how much the expedition represented deliberate imperial strategy versus opportunistic response to domestic political pressure. The latter interpretation is supported by the direct connection to the seikanron crisis: the expedition was partly a pressure valve. The legal mechanism — turning China's disavowal of sovereignty into a Japanese territorial claim — is seen as an early example of the sophisticated use of international law that characterized Meiji foreign policy more broadly.
