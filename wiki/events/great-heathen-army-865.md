# The Great Heathen Army (865–878)

## Narrative

In 865 a large Viking force — the **"Great Army"** (*micel here*) — landed in England,
marking the shift from sporadic coastal raiding to sustained territorial conquest. Over the
following years it overran and destroyed the kingdoms of **Northumbria** and **East Anglia**
and the eastern half of **[[mercia|Mercia]]**, wintering in fortified camps rather than
returning home. Only **Wessex** survived: after years of pressure and tribute, **Alfred of
Wessex** won a decisive victory at **Edington (878)**, after which the Viking leader Guthrum
accepted baptism and the army turned to settlement. The conquered territories of eastern and
northern England became the **Danelaw**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[viking-age|the escalation of Viking activity]] from raiding into conquest by
  semi-permanent armies that overwintered in England
- `contributed_to:` the political fragmentation of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms and the wealth of
  their churches and trading places
- `enabled:` the demonstration effect of earlier successful, loot-laden raids (NCMH II, Coupland)

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Danelaw** — the Scandinavian-settled and -governed zone of eastern and
  northern England
- `produced:` the survival of **Wessex** as the sole surviving English kingdom and the nucleus of
  a future unified England; Alfred's **burh** fortress network and military reforms
- `enabled:` the long-term Scandinavianisation of English law, language, and place-names in the
  Danelaw

## Historiography

The principal source is the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, compiled from a late-ninth-century West Saxon
viewpoint — which both centres Alfred and gives England a far thinner record than the Continent's
annals and charters (NCMH II, Coupland, Ch. 7). The term "Great Army" is itself problematic,
sometimes denoting a specific force rather than a single unified host. The size of the army and the
historicity of figures such as the sons of Ragnar are debated.
