# Fourth Syrian War (219–217 BCE)

Fourth in the recurring series of Ptolemaic-Seleucid wars over Coele-Syria, fought between [[actors/antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III of the Seleucid Empire]] and Ptolemy IV Philopator of Egypt, and one of the best-documented Hellenistic wars thanks to [[actors/polybius|Polybius's]] detailed surviving narrative in his *Histories*.

## Narrative

Antiochus III opened the war with early gains, overrunning much of Coele-Syria while the Ptolemaic court, dominated by the minister Sosibius, delayed confrontation to build and train an army — notably including large numbers of native Egyptian phalangites armed and drilled in Macedonian style, a departure from the traditionally Greco-Macedonian composition of Ptolemaic armies. The two armies met at the Battle of Raphia in June 217 BCE; Ptolemaic forces, aided by the performance of the newly-trained Egyptian troops, won a decisive victory, and Antiochus was forced to withdraw and cede back his gains, restoring the prewar Ptolemaic-Seleucid border.

## Causal Analysis

The war was `caused_by` Antiochus III's calculated bid to exploit what he judged Ptolemaic weakness and reclaim long-contested Seleucid territorial claims to Coele-Syria, `contributed_to` by the broader unresolved rivalry running through the earlier Syrian Wars.

## Consequence Analysis

Raphia `produced` a Ptolemaic victory that preserved the strategic status quo for another generation, but per Polybius it also `enabled` an unintended consequence within Egypt: the Egyptian soldiers who had proven themselves at Raphia became newly conscious of their own military value, contributing to native unrest and revolt against Ptolemaic Greek rule in the decades that followed.

## Historiography

Polybius's account, largely preserved, makes the Fourth Syrian War unusually well-documented compared to the earlier Syrian Wars, though his focus on Antiochus III (a favorite subject) and on the Hellenistic "great power" system means the internal Egyptian dimension — especially the significance of arming native troops — is treated somewhat in passing relative to how later historians of Ptolemaic Egypt read its long-term importance for the dynasty's stability.
