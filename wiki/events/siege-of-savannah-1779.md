# The Siege of Savannah (1779)

> **⚔️ Staff-college tactical analysis:** see **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/siege-of-savannah|Savannah — Tactical Analysis]]** in the warfare hub for the full order of battle, the phased conduct of the action, and the principles-of-war assessment.

The first major test — and failure — of **Franco-American cooperation** in the southern theater. After
Britain opened its **"southern strategy"** by taking Savannah (December 1778) and Augusta, the French
fleet of the **comte d'Estaing** descended on the Georgia coast, and with 5,000 French troops joined
**Benjamin Lincoln**'s Americans to besiege the city.

## Narrative

Heavy rain delayed the French guns, giving **Augustine Prevost** time to strengthen his works. With his
storm-battered ships pressing him to leave, d'Estaing abandoned a methodical siege for a frontal
**assault on 9 October** — which "failed disastrously," the columns "cut down by … grape shot … and
musketry." The allies lost ~900 killed and wounded to a "mere fifty-four" British. Lincoln withdrew;
d'Estaing sailed for the Caribbean.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the British **southern strategy**, premised on the South's commercial value to the West
  Indies and on (overestimated) Loyalist support.
- `contributed_to:` weather, d'Estaing's **impatience**, and the strength of Prevost's defenses.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` a damaging allied defeat — "as at Newport … the potential of Franco-American cooperation
  had not been realized."
- `enabled:` the lesson, finally applied at **[[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]]**, of what coordinated
  land–sea operations required; and it deepened [[clinton-henry|Clinton]]'s fear of exactly such a trap.

## Historiography

Documented through French and American official reports and British garrison accounts, the siege is a central case study in the historiography of Franco-American military cooperation's early failures, contrasted explicitly (as in this entry) with the coordination eventually achieved at Yorktown. Historians debate how much responsibility for the failed frontal assault belongs to d'Estaing's own impatience (driven by real hurricane-season and supply pressures on his fleet) versus Lincoln's deference to French command prerogatives in a coalition where the junior partner had to defer to naval and troop-numbers realities. The battle also receives attention in the historiography of Black military participation, since a Haitian volunteer contingent (the Chasseurs-Volontaires) fought here, a detail sometimes underexplored relative to the battle's broader place in Revolutionary War narratives.
