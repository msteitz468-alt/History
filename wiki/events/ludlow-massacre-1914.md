# Ludlow Massacre

On April 20, 1914, Colorado National Guardsmen and company guards attacked a tent colony of striking coal miners and their families at Ludlow, Colorado, during a strike against the Rockefeller-controlled Colorado Fuel & Iron Company; the resulting fire and gunfire killed some two dozen people, including women and children. Although [[actors/rockefeller-john-d-jr|John D. Rockefeller, Jr.]] was distant from operations, the family's controlling ownership made the massacre a defining reputational crisis. Chernow's *Titan* treats it as the catalyst that pushed Junior toward modern public relations (Ivy Lee), labor conciliation (the Rockefeller Plan, advised by W. L. Mackenzie King), and a deeper personal reorientation of the family's public role.

## Historiography

The event is documented through the 1914-15 Congressional (Commission on Industrial Relations) hearings, contemporary labor and company press, and the surviving correspondence and testimony gathered in Ron Chernow's *Titan* (1998), the source underlying this page. Exact casualty figures (particularly the number of women and children killed in the tent-colony fire) vary slightly across contemporary and later accounts. Historiographical debate centers less on the facts of the massacre than on causal responsibility and characterization: labor historians have generally read it as the direct, foreseeable product of Rockefeller-controlled company-town policy, while accounts more sympathetic to the family (including Chernow's) emphasize John D. Rockefeller Jr.'s physical and managerial distance from Colorado operations and treat the massacre chiefly as the catalyst for his later turn to industrial-relations reform.
