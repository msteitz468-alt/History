## Narrative

At sixty-seven years old, the poet-general [[actors/hosokawa-yusai|Hosokawa Yusai]] (Fujitaka) held his castle at Tanabe in Tango province against a Western besieging army reported at some 15,000 men, with a garrison of only about 500. The siege proceeded unusually half-heartedly: many of the besieging captains had studied poetry under Yusai and, by tradition, fought without enthusiasm against their former teacher.

Concerned above all for the survival of rare manuscripts in his keeping — including a valuable edition of the *Kokinshū* and the secret transmission of its interpretive tradition (*Kokin Denju*) — Yusai wrote to Prince Tomohito, the emperor's brother, asking that the volumes be conveyed to the imperial court. Alarmed at the prospect of losing so eminent a scholar-poet, the court issued an imperial edict ordering the siege lifted and the garrison allowed to march out unharmed; the Western force withdrew roughly two months after the siege began, too late to take part in the Battle of Sekigahara.

## Causal Analysis

The siege was directly caused_by the outbreak of the Sekigahara campaign and Yusai's alignment with [[actors/tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu's]] Eastern coalition; its unusual resolution was contributed_to by Yusai's personal renown as a poet-scholar, which activated direct imperial intervention independent of the military situation.

## Consequence Analysis

The prolonged diversion of a large Western force at Tanabe — together with comparable delays at Fushimi and Otsu — meant that tens of thousands of Western troops never reached the decisive field at Sekigahara, materially affecting the campaign's balance of forces in the Eastern coalition's favor.

## Historiography

The episode is recorded chiefly through A.L. Sadler's narrative of the Sekigahara campaign, which treats it as illustrating both the strategic value of stubborn garrison defense in delaying an army and the singular status accorded classical literary learning in Sengoku-era Japan, sufficient to override military logic at the imperial court's insistence.
