# The Peasants' Revolt (1381)

The **Peasants' Revolt** — the English Rising of 1381 — was the great popular insurrection of
later-medieval England, the most dramatic of the social explosions that the
**[[late-medieval-crisis|post-plague crisis]]** produced across Europe.
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Caroline M. Barron, Ch. 11b) sets it at the centre of the reign of
**[[richard-ii-of-england|Richard II]]**.

## Narrative

In late spring 1381 the commons of **Essex and Kent** rose, marched on **London**, and — with the
city's gates opened to them — burned John of Gaunt's Savoy palace, opened the prisons, and killed
the chancellor and treasurer (Archbishop Sudbury and Robert Hales). The fourteen-year-old **Richard
II** met the rebels, first at Mile End (where he conceded the abolition of serfdom) and then at
**Smithfield**, where their leader **Wat Tyler** was struck down; the king's personal intervention
helped disperse the rising, which was then suppressed in the localities and its concessions revoked.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **poll taxes** of 1377–1381 — the immediate trigger — levied to fund the
  failing French war.
- `contributed_to:` the **post-plague labour legislation** (the Ordinance and Statute of Labourers)
  that tried to hold wages at pre-plague levels against the market created by demographic collapse,
  and the resentment of **seigneurial dues and royal officials**. The preaching of **John Ball**
  ("When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?") gave the grievances an egalitarian
  ideology.

## Consequence Analysis

Though crushed within weeks, the revolt was a symptom of the deep social shift the
**[[black-death|Black Death]]** had set in motion: a shrunken population whose **labour scarcity
raised real wages** and emboldened the commons against lordship and the labour laws. In the longer
run the attempt to freeze wages and bind labour failed; **serfdom in England continued its
decline**, and landlords could not restore the old order. The revolt left the propertied classes
with a lasting fear of popular rising — and stands with the **Jacquerie (1358)** in France and the
**Ciompi (1378)** in Florence as part of the Europe-wide cluster of late-fourteenth-century revolts.
