# Hitler at Pasewalk Hospital (October–November 1918)

## Narrative

On 14 October 1918, near Comines on the Ypres sector, Corporal Adolf Hitler of the List Regiment was caught in a British mustard-gas attack. Temporarily blinded, he was evacuated to the military hospital at Pasewalk in Pomerania. While recovering, he learned of the armistice (11 November) and the German Revolution.

In his own later account: "I knew that all was lost... What was the pain in my eyes compared to this misery?" He resolved that he had become a politician with a mission against the "November criminals."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the physical gas injury combined with the sudden, total military and political collapse of the German Empire (Kiel mutiny, Kaiser's abdication 9 Nov, Wittelsbach fall in Bavaria 7 Nov, armistice). The "stab in the back" legend (Jews, Marxists, home-front betrayal) was already circulating; Hitler internalized it personally.

`preceded_by:` four years of front service as Meldegänger, Iron Cross awards, "community of the front" identity.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the explicit decision "I had become a politician." Returned to Munich 21 November 1918 "without education, career or prospects," intending to remain in the army as long as possible. This placed him in the chaotic revolutionary city where he would be retained as informant/educator and discover his oratorical talent.

`enabled:` the pivot from anonymous veteran to political agitator in 1919. Without the Pasewalk trauma coinciding with defeat and revolution, the specific radicalization path is altered.

`followed_by:` [[events/munich-revolution-1918-1919]], Reichswehr employment, [[events/hitler-dap-entry-1919]].

## Historiography

Kershaw (Range 01, Ch. 3) notes the decisive personal caesura. Hitler's *Mein Kampf* account of the hospital moment is treated as broadly credible in emotional impact even if details are stylized. The "stab in the back" was not unique to him but became for Hitler a lifetime mission. The event marks the transition from the "failure" of the pre-war years and the "elation" of 1914–18 to the embitterment that fueled his politics.

Cross-ref [[sources/kershaw-hitler-hubris-1998]], [[actors/adolf-hitler]], [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/adolf-hitler]], [[concepts/augusterlebnis]] (for 1914 contrast).

Evans (2003, [[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) concurs on the caesura:
gassing was "a frequent occurrence on both sides in the later stages of the
war"; at Pasewalk Hitler learned of defeat, Armistice, and Revolution — in
*Mein Kampf* "the greatest villainy of the century"; "everything went black
before my eyes," and he wept. Evans: "The memory of 1918 was to play a
central role in all his subsequent thought and action." He also traces
Hitler's propaganda doctrine to this trauma — propaganda "must be limited to
a very few points," pitched to "the most limited intelligence," aimed at
emotion because "the people... are so feminine by nature." See
[[stab-in-the-back-myth]].
