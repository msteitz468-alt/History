## Narrative

The colonization of the Americas was the last major continental peopling event
in human prehistory. It required crossing Beringia — the land bridge exposed
between Siberia and Alaska when sea levels were ~60–90 metres below present
during the Last Glacial Maximum — or navigating the Pacific coastline.

**Preconditions: Siberia first**
The Americas could only be colonized after Siberia was colonized. Despite
possible temporary human presence in Siberia 40,000–50,000 BP, permanent
settlement of Arctic Siberia is only attested from ~18,000 BP. The harsh
subarctic environments required developed cold-weather technologies.

**Entry routes (two models)**
1. **Beringia interior corridor**: After the coldest period of the Last Glacial
   Maximum, a corridor opened between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets
   along the Yukon and Mackenzie River valleys, allowing movement southward. This
   was the dominant model through the 20th century.
2. **Pacific coastal route**: Colonists followed the Pacific coastline of
   Beringia and North America, exploiting marine resources. This route would
   have been available earlier (before the interior corridor opened) but
   evidence is largely submerged under risen sea levels.

**The Clovis tradition (~13,500–12,800 BP)**
The Clovis tradition — named for a distinctive fluted stone point first found
near the Blackwater Draw site, Clovis, New Mexico — was the first clearly
documented, continent-wide culture in the Americas. See [[clovis-culture]] for
the full actor page. Up to 4,000 Clovis points have been found from Canada to
Mexico, often with remains of mammoths and other megafauna at kill sites.
Clovis peoples were highly mobile (absence of large grindstones; stone quarried
hundreds of kilometres from use sites). Radiocarbon dates place all Clovis
sites within a span of only ~600 years, implying a dispersed and possibly
fast-growing population. The Clovis tradition was the dominant framework for
thinking about first Americans from the 1930s to the 1960s — the **"Clovis-first"
model**, which held that Clovis hunters were the literal founding population,
entering via the ice-free corridor and spreading southward in pursuit of game.

**The Beringian sequence and the Nenana complex**
A stable human presence in Siberia is attested only after ~18,000 BP (the LGM);
Beringia's two great ice masses, the Laurentide and Cordilleran, coalesced
during the LGM and blocked southward movement, diverging afterward to open the
Yukon/Mackenzie ice-free corridor. Notably, the **earliest assemblages from
Alaska — where colonists necessarily entered — show no clear cultural affinity
to Clovis**: the **Nenana complex** (~13,400–13,900 BP) is a flake-and-blade
industry with teardrop "Chindadn" points whose relationship to Clovis remains
unclear. Blade technology links Nenana to Upper Paleolithic Siberia (Western
Beringia), suggesting Clovis fluting may have developed *within* the Americas
rather than being carried in.

**The Solutrean hypothesis (minority/rejected)**
Clovis knappers used a distinctive *outré passé* (overshot) flaking technique
also seen in the Upper Paleolithic **Solutrean** culture of Ice Age Europe.
A few archaeologists (Stanford & Bradley) read this as evidence of an Atlantic
crossing from Iberia. Waguespack and most specialists treat the resemblance as
**convergent adaptation to bifacial tool production**, not shared ancestry; the
genetic evidence consistently points to Siberia as the source.

**Pre-Clovis sites (contested)**
Several sites claim to document human presence before the Clovis tradition:
- **Monte Verde, Chile**: radiocarbon dates ~14,500–14,800 BP; the most widely
  accepted pre-Clovis site; implies entry well before 14,500 BP
- **Pedra Furada, Brazil**: claimed dates of 40,000+ BP; disputed; most scholars
  skeptical
- **Meadowcroft Rockshelter, Pennsylvania**: dates possibly 16,000 BP; debated
- **Buttermilk Creek, Texas**: ~15,500 BP; pre-Clovis stone tools

- **Paisley Cave, Oregon**: a human coprolite dated ~14,000 BP is currently
  the **earliest widely accepted direct evidence of human occupation** in the
  Americas; not associated with diagnostic artifacts, so it may represent
  pre-Clovis colonists, an earlier non-colonizing exploration, or early Clovis
- **Quebrada Jaguay, Peru**: a coastal site showing Late Pleistocene use of
  marine resources, consistent with a Pacific coastal entry route

Pre-Clovis deposits typically lack material suitable for radiocarbon and rely
on optically stimulated luminescence (OSL), a less-preferred method for this
period — a recurring reason their dates are scrutinized. The Clovis-first model
is now considered untenable by most archaeologists due to Monte Verde and other
evidence: a pre-Clovis population, possibly arriving via coastal routes,
preceded the Clovis tradition. But *which* sites constitute definitive proof,
and how large or enduring those earliest populations were, remains genuinely
unresolved. See [[clovis-first-vs-pre-clovis]].

**Megafaunal extinctions (~13,000–12,000 BP)**
- Over thirty genera of large-bodied North American animals (mammoths,
  mastodons, horses, camels, giant ground sloths, short-faced bears,
  saber-toothed cats) disappeared ~13,000–12,000 BP
- The **Clovis-overkill hypothesis** attributes extinctions primarily to human
  hunting: megafauna had no experience of human hunters and were not
  sufficiently wary. Clovis kill sites (Colby, Dent, Naco, Lehner, Murray
  Springs) provide the clearest global archaeological evidence of human
  predation on proboscideans, and experiments confirm Clovis points penetrate
  elephant hide.
- **Counter-argument** (Grayson & Meltzer): mammoth remains may be
  *over-represented* because big bones are easier to find; only ~14 of the
  37 extinct genera have any kill/butchery association at all, suggesting
  megafauna made little caloric contribution to everyday Clovis diet. On this
  reading Clovis foragers ate a broad, regionally varied diet and the
  extinctions were driven mainly by **rapid climate change** (the Younger Dryas
  cold snap ~12,900–11,700 BP coincides with both the extinctions and the
  Clovis→Folsom transition).
- Both factors likely combined; the debate is unresolved. See
  [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]].

**Paleoindian lifeways**
Paleoindian cultures were highly mobile. They produced distinctive stone tool
traditions with fluted points. Evidence for wide-range mobility: tools made of
stone quarried hundreds of kilometres away; absence of grinding stones
suggesting little plant food processing. Clovis and **Folsom** are the only two
populations in all of human prehistory known to have manufactured *fluted*
projectile points (the flute is a long channel flake removed from the base,
probably to ease hafting). Folsom (~12,800–11,000 BP), centered on the Rocky
Mountains and Great Plains, followed Clovis and had even longer, finer flutes;
it was first recognized at the Folsom site (1927) in association with extinct
*Bison antiquus*, the discovery that originally proved deep human antiquity in
the Americas. Beyond the grasslands, a variety of regional Paleoindian point
forms appear. The founding populations rapidly colonized a vast continent with
abundant naive megafauna.

**The Archaic transition (~8,000 BCE)**
Cultural, social, and technological continuities connect the Paleoindian period
to the Archaic. The transition is marked by new tool kits, signs of sedentism,
and more diverse subsistence strategies as megafauna disappear.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]]` — ultimately a late branch of the
global dispersal.

`caused_by: colonization of Siberia (~40,000–50,000 BP)` — the necessary stepping stone.

`contributed_to: lower sea levels` — exposed Beringia but did not create
the interior corridor south until ice sheet retreat ~14,500+ BP.

The critical enabling factor for the interior corridor route was the opening
of the ice-free passage after the Last Glacial Maximum, which occurred after
~14,500 BP. For the coastal route, the enabling factor was sea-going technology
and exploitation of marine resources.

## Consequence Analysis

The colonization of the Americas produced the entire pre-Columbian world:
Olmec, Maya, Aztec, Inca, and hundreds of other civilizations. It also
produced the megafaunal extinctions that radically transformed American
ecology — the absence of horses and large draft animals from the Americas
until European reintroduction in the 16th century CE shaped all subsequent
pre-Columbian technology and economy.

## Historiography

The Americas colonization debate is one of the most active in Paleolithic
archaeology. The field shifted decisively in the 1990s–2000s with the
acceptance of Monte Verde (~14,500 BP) by most scholars. The debate now
centers on:
- Whether a pre-Clovis coastal route population preceded Clovis peoples
- The early dates at Pedra Furada and what they imply (most scholars skeptical)
- The relative roles of human hunting vs. climate change in megafaunal extinction
- Genetic models of founding population size and entry timing

The paucity of skeletal remains (fewer than ten individuals represent early
Paleoindian cultures) means a single major discovery could transform the field.
