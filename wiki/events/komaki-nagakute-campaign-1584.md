## Narrative

### Strategic Background

By 1584 [[tokugawa-ieyasu]], lord of five provinces, was the only daimyo capable of checking [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]]'s bid for national supremacy. The immediate casus belli was Hideyoshi's subversion of three senior councilors of Oda Nobuo (son of Nobunaga) to undermine their allegiance. Nobuo had them executed and appealed to Ieyasu for support. Ieyasu "at once consented. No doubt he anticipated a collision sooner or later, for Hideyoshi's ambition was not compatible with any equal in the Empire."

Both sides mobilized coalitions. Hideyoshi had the support of Uesugi Kagekatsu (threatening Ieyasu's northern rear), Mori, and most of the western feudatories. Ieyasu arranged for Sasa Narimasa to attack Hideyoshi's allies in Kaga and Echizen, and for Chosokabe Motochika in Shikoku to create a second front. Hideyoshi's forces outnumbered the Tokugawa-Oda coalition nearly three to one.

### Opening Moves

The first significant action was Hideyoshi's ally Ikeda Nobuteru's seizure of Inuyama castle, a well-fortified position on the Kiso River in Owari. Ikeda used inside accomplices to ferry troops across and take the garrison by surprise. He then pushed out along the Owari plain toward Ieyasu's positions.

Ikeda's son-in-law Mori Nagayoshi made a forward probe toward Komaki with 5,000 men and was engaged and driven back in disorder by a Tokugawa force of similar size under Sakai Tadatsugu, losing some three hundred men. Ieyasu then seized the tactical initiative by occupying Komaki Hill — a 280-foot rise that commanded the surrounding plain, formerly used as a castle by Nobunaga — and spending a week entrenching its perimeter. Sakakibara Yasumasa's proclamation from Komaki framed the campaign as a righteous defense of Nobunaga's legitimate heir against a treacherous upstart.

### The Standoff

Hideyoshi arrived at Inuyama with approximately 80,000 men and constructed a rampart fifteen feet high between Nijubori and Iwasaki-yama. The two armies dug in opposite each other for weeks, "both fearing to repeat the mistake of Nagashino by making a frontal attack." Ieyasu held Komaki; Hideyoshi held Gakuden. Neither commander would risk a direct assault.

### The Nagakute Battle (May 1584)

Ikeda Nobuteru proposed a strategic encircling movement: take 20,000 men around Ieyasu's flank, strike at his undefended capital Okazaki in Mikawa, draw him out, then crush him. Hideyoshi approved but warned against over-extension. Ikeda divided his force: 6,000 under himself, 3,000 under Mori Nagayoshi, 3,000 under Hori Hidemasa, and 8,000 under Miyoshi Hidetsugu. They departed at midnight of the sixth of the fourth month.

The garrison of Iwasaki fort — 239 men under Niwa Ujishige — spotted the column and sallied to give battle, buying time with noise before being overwhelmed. Their sacrifice got word to Ieyasu.

Ieyasu left 5,000 men under Sakai Tadatsugu, Honda Tadakatsu, and Ishikawa Kazumasa at Komaki, then marched south with 6,300 men plus 4,500 under Mizuno Tadashige and 3,000 under Oda Nobuo. Moving by night, he circled to intercept. At dawn on the eighth day, his vanguard hit Miyoshi Hidetsugu's rearguard at Hakusan from both sides simultaneously: "the enemy, taken off their guard, were thrown into confusion immediately by the violent attack from both sides at once and badly cut up and routed, Miyoshi himself saving his life with difficulty."

Hori's division, hearing the fighting, swung back toward Nagakude and formed on a hill with the Kanegawa river in front. There they repulsed the victorious but disordered Tokugawa vanguard with disciplined volley fire. Ieyasu then arrived with the main body. Seeing his standard, Mori's men retired.

The decisive action came at nine o'clock. Ieyasu commanded the right wing (3,300), Ii Naomasa the left (3,000), with Nobuo in reserve (3,000). Against them: Ikeda's two sons Terumasa and Yukisuke with 4,000 on the right, Mori Nagayoshi with 3,000 on the left, Ikeda Nobuteru with 2,000 in reserve. The Tokugawa side opened with heavy matchlock fire, killing 200–300 of the enemy. Ikeda's sons charged Ii Naomasa's left but were stopped. Ieyasu on the right feigned inaction, waiting for Mori to commit. When Mori held back, Ieyasu suddenly divided his force and hurled it on Mori's position. A matchlock man in Ii's ranks shot Mori Nagayoshi through the head as he stood conspicuous in white costume rallying his men. He fell dead, aged twenty-seven. Nobuo's reserve then swept through the broken ranks and crashed into Ikeda Nobuteru's reserve. Nagai Dempachiro speared Nobuteru as he sat resigned on his campstool and took his head. Ikeda's son Yukisuke was also killed; his brother Terumasa escaped. "All was over by one o'clock, the action having occupied three hours. The loss on the side of the vanquished was 2,500 killed, and that of the Tokugawa and Oda forces 590."

### Aftermath and Honda Tadakatsu's Action

Hearing of the defeat, Hideyoshi moved from Gakuden toward Nagakute. Honda Tadakatsu, commanding at Komaki, refused to wait: he rode out with fewer than a thousand men to Ryusenji and began harassing Hideyoshi's flanks, theatrically daring the approaching host to battle. Hideyoshi recognized Honda and ordered his men not to cross the river to engage him: "He mustn't die. I need men like him and his master as my supporters in the future." Hideyoshi marched past without engaging while Honda watched from horseback.

Ieyasu slipped away from Kobata back to Komaki that night. Hideyoshi, finding his quarry had escaped, reportedly struck his hands together "partly in irritation and partly in admiration": "Here's a leader you can't catch either with net or line! He's smart and he's solid. You won't find another like him in the Empire, wide as it is. Well, I shall get him in Court dress at Kyoto sooner or later!"

### Peace and Stalemate

The campaign settled into further maneuvering and attrition without a decisive second battle. A peace was arranged through Oda Nobuo late in 1584. The Tokugawa-Oda alliance dissolved when Nobuo agreed terms with Hideyoshi independently — without consulting Ieyasu — and Ieyasu had no choice but to accept the resulting stalemate.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Hideyoshi's subversion of Nobuo's councilors created the immediate pretext; Nobuo's appeal gave Ieyasu a legitimate banner.
- `contributed_to:` Both sides' reluctance to risk a decisive frontal assault (the lesson of Nagashino) shaped the war's predominantly positional character.
- `caused_by: [[ieyasu-seizure-of-kai-and-shinano-1582]]` — Ieyasu's expanded domain gave him sufficient resources to contest Hideyoshi on roughly comparable terms.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[ieyasu-hideyoshi-relations]]` — The stalemate ended without decisive victory for either side; Hideyoshi accepted Ieyasu's continued autonomy for the time being.
- `enabled: [[ieyasu-submission-to-hideyoshi-1586]]` — Ieyasu's battlefield credibility, and specifically the Nagakute victory, gave him leverage to negotiate submission on favorable terms two years later.
- `produced:` Ieyasu's fame as a commander impervious to Hideyoshi's genius — a reputation that contributed to the eventual Tokugawa succession.

- Sakakibara Yasumasa — seized Komaki Hill; issued the propaganda proclamation justifying the campaign

## Historiography

Sadler draws on the Annals of the Sakakibara House for the proclamation, and on the Mikawa Go-Fudoki, Taikoki, and other Tokugawa-era chronicles for the battle narrative. The account is sympathetic to Ieyasu; Hideyoshi's strategic dominance despite the tactical defeat at Nagakute is somewhat underplayed. Modern historians generally assess the campaign as demonstrating Hideyoshi's superior strategic resources while conceding the tactical brilliance of Ieyasu's night interception. The episode of Honda Tadakatsu's defiance (which Sadler narrates with admiration) is a celebrated set piece in Japanese military historiography but may be embellished. The campaign established the terms under which the subsequent Ieyasu-Hideyoshi relationship evolved: mutual respect, acknowledged Tokugawa military potency, and ultimately Hideyoshi's need to secure Ieyasu through conciliation rather than conquest.
