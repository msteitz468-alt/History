# Granada Massacre of 1066

On **30–31 December 1066** (Ṣafar 459 AH), the Jewish vizier **Joseph ibn Naghrela** —
son of the celebrated poet-statesman-general **Samuel ha-Nagid ibn Naghrela** — was
assassinated by court enemies in the Zirid taifa of Granada, and a Muslim mob then
massacred much of the city's Jewish community and seized its property.

## Narrative

Samuel ha-Nagid had risen to be vizier and effective head of the Berber army of Muslim
Granada — an almost unheard-of height for a [[dhimmi|dhimmī]]. His son Joseph inherited
the position but was widely resented as arrogant. Amid factional intrigue, an inciting
Arabic poem by a contemporary Muslim (Abū Isḥāq of Elvira) denounced the Jews as having
"violated our covenant" and called the mob to slaughter Joseph. The principal
primary source is the memoir of **ʿAbdallāh**, grandson of the Zirid sultan Bādīs, who
describes the mob "killing every Jew in the city."

## Causal Analysis

Per [[cohen-under-crescent-cross-1994|Mark Cohen]] (following Bernard Lewis), the massacre
is best understood not as generic antisemitism but as a violent reaction to the perceived
**violation of the dhimma's "right order"** — a dhimmī "lording it over" Muslims. It thus
illustrates the structural *ceiling* on dhimmī empowerment: high office was possible but
precarious, and overreach could trigger lethal backlash. `contributed_to:` the anti-Jewish
incitement; `caused_by:` the factional politics of the Zirid court.

## Consequence Analysis

The massacre destroyed one of al-Andalus's leading Jewish communities. Cohen stresses a
striking historiographical afterlife: despite its severity, it left **"hardly a literary
trace"** among the Jews of the Islamic world — mentioned only briefly in Moses ibn Ezra's
Arabic prosody work and in Abraham ibn Daud's *Sefer ha-Qabbalah*, with contemporary
elegies for Joseph "lack[ing] the faintest allusion to the gruesome circumstances." This
near-silence is central to Cohen's argument about the comparative *absence* of a Jewish
literature of suffering under Islam. In modern debate the event is a favourite prooftext
of the "neo-lachrymose" [[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth|countermyth]] — which
Cohen counters is a real but **exceptional** episode against the classical norm.

## Historiography

The massacre is disputed less on facts than on framing: whether it evidences innate
Islamic persecution (countermyth) or an exceptional breach of an otherwise stable dhimma
order (Cohen, Lewis). The inciting poem survives only in OCR-degraded and much-quoted
form; its most-cited epithets ("apes," "outcast dogs") are reliably attested even where
the full text is corrupt. See [[jews-under-islam-myth-and-countermyth]].
