# The Korean War (1950–1953)

The **Korean War** was the first **hot war of the [[cold-war|Cold War]]** — a three-year conflict on the divided Korean peninsula that drew in the United States (under UN authority), the new People's Republic of **[[china|China]]**, and, behind the scenes, the Soviet Union. It transformed the Cold War from a primarily European, political-economic contest into a **global, militarized** confrontation. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. I]].

## Narrative

Korea had been divided at the **38th parallel** in 1945 (Soviet occupation north, American south), hardening into the rival regimes of **Kim Il Sung's** DPRK and **Syngman Rhee's** ROK. Until late 1949 Stalin restrained Kim from invading, warning it would bring US intervention. But after the Communist victory in China and the Soviet atomic test, **Stalin changed his mind in January 1950** — tempted, Leffler and Zubok suggest, to recoup the Soviet retreat in Europe, to gain a springboard against Japan, and to lock China against the US — and gave Kim a qualified green light, conditional on Mao's support.

North Korea **invaded on 25 June 1950**, nearly overrunning the South. The US (with the UN Security Council boycotted by the absent USSR) intervened; **MacArthur's** amphibious landing at **Inchon** (September 1950) routed the North and drove its army back. Truman and Acheson then made the fateful decision to **cross the 38th parallel** to unify the peninsula, betting China would not intervene. The bet failed: as UN forces neared the **Yalu**, **China entered massively** (October–November 1950), throwing them back. The front stabilized near the original border, and the war ground on for two more years of attrition until the **armistice of 27 July 1953** — leaving Korea **divided** along almost the same line, with no peace treaty.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[containment]]` policy and the logic of the emerging bipolar order. The division itself and Stalin's green light were proximate causes. The Chinese intervention was triggered by the UN crossing of the 38th parallel and approach to the Yalu.

`contributed_to:` acceleration of Western rearmament and formalization of alliances.

`preceded_by:` the Soviet atomic test of 1949 and the fall of Nationalist China (both noted in Freedman as heightening Western threat perceptions and contributing to NSC-68 momentum).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` militarization of containment via NSC-68; permanent division of Korea; transformation of NATO into an integrated military command with US ground forces committed to Europe; Japanese rearmament; a Sino-American freeze lasting decades.

`enabled:` the intellectual and policy turn toward massive retaliation as a cheaper alternative to matching communist conventional forces everywhere.

`concurrent_with:` the early phase of the nuclear arms race and the H-bomb decision.

## From Range 03 (Niu Jun and Stueck in Leffler & Westad Vol. I)

- Road to war (Niu Jun): "The international order in East Asia changed dramatically following the conclusion of the Sino-Japanese War in 1945; the two most consequential events of this period were the birth of the People’s Republic of China (PRC) on October 1, 1949, and, one year later, the PRC’s entry into a three-year military contest against the United States in the Korean War, 1950–53." "For China, the decision to ally with the USSR and enter the Korean War meant that there was no alternative but to man Asia’s Cold War frontier against US encroachment." US-Soviet coordination pre-1945 on "peace under Jiang Jieshi"; Stalin viewed CCP lower priority, told Americans/USSR would support US China policy and "there could only be one government in China, led by the GMD." CCP "lean to one side" policy formulated after civil war victories; solidified by Mikoian Xibaipo visit (Jan 1949) and Liu Shaoqi Moscow (June 1949); new treaty signed 14 Feb 1950 after Mao insistence in Moscow Dec 1949–Jan 1950. "The CCP lived in constant dread of various forms of US intervention, including a direct military intervention, schemes to sow division..., an embargo..., or the obstruction of the final reunification of Taiwan..." US Taiwan Strait blockade after June 1950 "directly contradicted the final goals of the CCP" and "forced the PRC to abandon a campaign to take over Taiwan and... facilitated the redeployment of several PLA army corps to the Korean border". Mao Politburo 4 Aug 1950: "if the US imperialists were to succeed, they would be complacent, and would threaten us."
- Origins of division (Stueck): Cairo Conference fall 1943 agreed Korea “in due course … become free and independent.” Roosevelt favored trusteeship (US, USSR, China, possibly Britain) fearing Korean incapacity and great-power struggle. At end of Pacific war mid-Aug 1945, "the United States and the Soviet Union alone sent troops to Korea". Truman proposed 38th parallel "be designated the demarcation line between Soviet and American occupation forces. Stalin agreed." "The Americans and the Soviets bypassed the Koreans in deciding to occupy their country and in choosing a boundary between their occupation forces..."
- South zone: Japanese authorities sought "native elite"; Yo Un-hyong formed Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence with affiliates nationwide. Sept 6 1945 some declared "Korean People’s Republic". US forces under Lt Gen John R. Hodge "refused to work with the People’s Republic, discouraged the people’s committees, and favored contacts with members of the colonial apparatus or Korean conservatives."
- North zone: Soviets "treated their zone as a sphere of influence." Kim Il Sung (born 1912 near Pyongyang, Manchuria guerrilla fighter affiliated with CCP, fled to USSR 1941) "emerged as the Soviets’ favorite." "Kim returned to Korea in a Soviet army uniform in September 1945"; became secretary-in-chief of NK Communist branch Dec 1945; chairman of new Korean Provisional People’s Committee (NKPPC, "in effect a provisional government under the Soviet occupation") Feb 1946.
- Trusteeship controversy and polarization: Dec 1945 US-USSR agreed "four-power trusteeship for a period of up to five years" via joint commission. Outrage in south led by Kim Ku and Syngman Rhee. Soviets persuaded Communists to support; US suggested trusteeship might be avoided. Joint Commission convened Seoul March 1946 "in an acrimonious atmosphere. It adjourned in May without agreement." "By summer, politics in Korea had become polarized. The Soviets had turned toward consolidating Communist factions under Kim Il Sung’s leadership rather than establishing a coalition... The Americans strove for a coalition of Korean rightist and centrist forces while adopting repressive measures against the Communists."
- Economic and social unrest south: "From the start, the American occupation mishandled rice distribution and, unlike the Soviets in the North, it held back on implementing a broad land reform program." Influx of over a million Koreans from north/Manchuria/Japan created shortages. "Brutal acts of the national police, manned primarily by Koreans who had served under the Japanese, magnified resentments..." Autumn Harvest Uprising fall 1946 began with "strikes among railroad workers in Seoul, Pusan, and Taegu". "Mobs of industrial workers, farmers, students, and the unemployed appeared in cities throughout the zone. Disturbances lasted into November and were suppressed by the national police only with the assistance of US troops and a new South Korean constabulary. Deaths and casualties on both sides numbered well into the hundreds. Although the role of Communists from within or outside the American zone remains uncertain, the makeup, timing, and location of the riots suggest coordination rather than spontaneity."

## Nuclear Strategy Implications (from Freedman 1981 Range 02)

In terms of nuclear strategy, the Korean War demonstrated that, "in a period when the world’s two greatest powers were nervous about getting into an all-out war with each other, any conflict would be subjected to major political constraints. The experience suggested that when it came to the crunch, atomic bombs were not perceived as ordinary weapons but as something special to be handled with care, almost as if a taboo prevented their use."

President Truman’s distaste for atomic weapons was shared by America’s Allies, particularly the British. When Truman hinted in a press conference that atom-bomb use might be considered, "Prime Minister Clement Attlee hurried over the Atlantic to urge restraint." The US State Department suspected even fiercer reactions from countries far less friendly than Britain.

The atomic stockpile "was still small and many felt it had to be kept in reserve for a clash in Europe. It was widely assumed that Korea was merely a diversion from a big Soviet push being prepared for Europe."

Finally, "it was not clear to local commanders how atomic weapons could be profitably employed. Targets of strategic value in North Korea could be dealt with readily by conventional bombardment, and those North of the Yalu were out of bounds. Targets of tactical value, such as the position of enemy troops, could rarely be reported accurately and sufficiently quickly to permit destruction with atomic weapons. Furthermore, they were often too close to friendly troops to be attacked with atomic weapons safely."

However, nuclear use was not entirely unthinkable. "Plans for a number of contingencies may well have involved nuclear weapons. One of the most likely was large-scale attacks on United Nations forces from communist aircraft based in China and the Soviet Far East. In the event of such attacks, the US might have retaliated by destroying the relevant bases, a task that could well have required atomic bombs." (All quotes and analysis: Freedman 1981: "Korea").

The war thus both illustrated the practical and political limits of nuclear weapons in limited conflicts and reinforced the perceived need for conventional rearmament (NSC-68 logic) while simultaneously feeding the later Eisenhower-era desire to escape those costs through greater reliance on nuclear threats ("the New Look").

## The War as Cold War Pivot (McCoy)

[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|McCoy (2024)]] notes that the 38th-parallel
division was sketched by Colonel Dean Rusk on a National Geographic map in August
1945, and frames the war (following Ronald Steel) as the pivot that "transformed the
vague rhetoric of the Truman doctrine into a blueprint for interventions against
communism," setting the template for four decades of proxy conflict. He also records
that South Korean police and military massacred ~100,000 suspected leftists in the
war's opening months (McCoy's figure), and totals over 3 million Korean dead. See
[[cold-war-proxy-wars|Cold War proxy wars]].
