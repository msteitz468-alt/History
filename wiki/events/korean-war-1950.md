# The Korean War (1950–1953)

The **Korean War** was the first **hot war of the [[cold-war|Cold War]]** — a three-year conflict on the divided Korean peninsula that drew in the United States (under UN authority), the new People's Republic of **[[china|China]]**, and, behind the scenes, the Soviet Union. It transformed the Cold War from a primarily European, political-economic contest into a **global, militarized** confrontation. From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. I]].

## Narrative

Korea had been divided at the **38th parallel** in 1945 (Soviet occupation north, American south), hardening into the rival regimes of **Kim Il Sung's** DPRK and **Syngman Rhee's** ROK. Until late 1949 Stalin restrained Kim from invading, warning it would bring US intervention. But after the Communist victory in China and the Soviet atomic test, **Stalin changed his mind in January 1950** — tempted, Leffler and Zubok suggest, to recoup the Soviet retreat in Europe, to gain a springboard against Japan, and to lock China against the US — and gave Kim a qualified green light, conditional on Mao's support.

North Korea **invaded on 25 June 1950**, nearly overrunning the South. The US (with the UN Security Council boycotted by the absent USSR) intervened; **MacArthur's** amphibious landing at **Inchon** (September 1950) routed the North and drove its army back. Truman and Acheson then made the fateful decision to **cross the 38th parallel** to unify the peninsula, betting China would not intervene. The bet failed: as UN forces neared the **Yalu**, **China entered massively** (October–November 1950), throwing them back. The front stabilized near the original border, and the war ground on for two more years of attrition until the **armistice of 27 July 1953** — leaving Korea **divided** along almost the same line, with no peace treaty.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[containment]]` policy and the logic of the emerging bipolar order. The division itself and Stalin's green light were proximate causes. The Chinese intervention was triggered by the UN crossing of the 38th parallel and approach to the Yalu.

`contributed_to:` acceleration of Western rearmament and formalization of alliances.

`preceded_by:` the Soviet atomic test of 1949 and the fall of Nationalist China (both noted in Freedman as heightening Western threat perceptions and contributing to NSC-68 momentum).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` militarization of containment via NSC-68; permanent division of Korea; transformation of NATO into an integrated military command with US ground forces committed to Europe; Japanese rearmament; a Sino-American freeze lasting decades.

`enabled:` the intellectual and policy turn toward massive retaliation as a cheaper alternative to matching communist conventional forces everywhere.

`concurrent_with:` the early phase of the nuclear arms race and the H-bomb decision.

## Nuclear Strategy Implications (from Freedman 1981 Range 02)

In terms of nuclear strategy, the Korean War demonstrated that, "in a period when the world’s two greatest powers were nervous about getting into an all-out war with each other, any conflict would be subjected to major political constraints. The experience suggested that when it came to the crunch, atomic bombs were not perceived as ordinary weapons but as something special to be handled with care, almost as if a taboo prevented their use."

President Truman’s distaste for atomic weapons was shared by America’s Allies, particularly the British. When Truman hinted in a press conference that atom-bomb use might be considered, "Prime Minister Clement Attlee hurried over the Atlantic to urge restraint." The US State Department suspected even fiercer reactions from countries far less friendly than Britain.

The atomic stockpile "was still small and many felt it had to be kept in reserve for a clash in Europe. It was widely assumed that Korea was merely a diversion from a big Soviet push being prepared for Europe."

Finally, "it was not clear to local commanders how atomic weapons could be profitably employed. Targets of strategic value in North Korea could be dealt with readily by conventional bombardment, and those North of the Yalu were out of bounds. Targets of tactical value, such as the position of enemy troops, could rarely be reported accurately and sufficiently quickly to permit destruction with atomic weapons. Furthermore, they were often too close to friendly troops to be attacked with atomic weapons safely."

However, nuclear use was not entirely unthinkable. "Plans for a number of contingencies may well have involved nuclear weapons. One of the most likely was large-scale attacks on United Nations forces from communist aircraft based in China and the Soviet Far East. In the event of such attacks, the US might have retaliated by destroying the relevant bases, a task that could well have required atomic bombs." (All quotes and analysis: Freedman 1981: "Korea").

The war thus both illustrated the practical and political limits of nuclear weapons in limited conflicts and reinforced the perceived need for conventional rearmament (NSC-68 logic) while simultaneously feeding the later Eisenhower-era desire to escape those costs through greater reliance on nuclear threats ("the New Look").
