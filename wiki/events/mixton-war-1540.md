# Mixtón War (1540–1541)

## Narrative

The **Mixtón War (1540–41)** was, in Eric Van Young’s judgment (CHNPA II.2 Ch. 15), among the most spectacular large-scale Indian resistance episodes in the Mexican Center-West — coming within a decade of effective Spanish presence in the more northerly parts of the region. The **Caxcan (Cazcán) uprising** threatened for a time to **extinguish Spanish influence in western Mexico**.

Unpacified tribal peoples north of the Río Grande de Santiago — especially **Cazcanes** northwest of Juchipila — raided from fortified hilltops of the **Sierra del Mixtón**, wounded or drove out encomenderos, destroyed mission centers, put Spanish forces to flight, and **threatened Guadalajara**. Major leaders included baptized chieftains **don Diego el Zacateco (Tenamaxtle)** and **don Francisco de Aguilar**, cacique of Nochistlán.

The rising was occasioned in part by the **draw-down of Nueva Galicia military strength** for the **Coronado** expedition north (1540–42). Motives included resistance to encomendero and missionary demands, generalized anti-Spanish resentments, and festering bitterness over the career of **Nuño Beltrán de Guzmán** in Nueva Galicia. The rebels **failed to ally with sedentary peoples to the south** — a structural limit that helped confine the war to the northern fringe rather than detonating a region-wide reconquest.

Suppression cost thousands of Indian lives and enslavements, plus the death of **Pedro de Alvarado** and scores of encomenderos, settlers, soldiers, and missionaries. The rising was crushed by Spanish force with large **Indian auxiliaries** under **Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza**. Savage repression followed: **branding and enslavement of hundreds** of captured natives under the Spanish politico-theological doctrine of **“just war”** — a doctrine then under attack from Las Casas.

The Mixtón War sits within a wider continuum of northern resistance: it is related to the larger **Chichimeca War** that racked the near north until nearly 1600, and to other risings (Guachichiles/Guamares Guanajuato–Zacatecas 1550 and 1607; Zacatecos/Guachichiles 1561; Guaynamota/Nayarit 1584; Aguascalientes 1541, 1575, 1593).

## Causal Analysis

- *caused_by:* encomendero and missionary extractive and cultural demands on Caxcan and neighboring northern peoples of Nueva Galicia
- *contributed_to:* (enabling context) military draw-down for Coronado’s northern expedition, 1540–42
- *contributed_to:* residual bitterness from Nuño de Guzmán’s Nueva Galicia conquest and enslavements (late 1520s–1530s)
- *preceded_by:* Spanish foundation of Guadalajara and other Nueva Galicia towns; early Franciscan mission penetration of the plateau and cañones
- *concurrent_with:* first generation of colonial rule in the Center-West after [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]]; ongoing [[virgin-soil-epidemics]] and [[encomienda]] reorganization
- Ideology (poorly documented in detail but, per Van Young, definite): **millenarian** elements — war of extermination against Spaniards, rejection of Christianity, return of the old native gods — continuous with the Franciscan millenarian stamp on regional religious discourse described under [[western-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]] and [[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]

## Consequence Analysis

- *produced:* temporary near-collapse then reconsolidation of Spanish military-political control in western Mexico
- *produced:* mass branding and enslavement of captives under “just war” doctrine
- *contributed_to:* death of Pedro de Alvarado and heavy Spanish-side losses among encomenderos, settlers, soldiers, and missionaries
- *followed_by* / *contributed_to:* prolonged northern frontier war (Chichimeca War to ~1600) and later discrete risings across Guanajuato–Zacatecas–Nayarit–Aguascalientes
- *enabled:* (for the colonial regime) harsher security and settlement patterns on the silver-corridor frontier; (for indigenous history) a load-bearing early case of millenarian anti-colonial ideology in the Center-West, later echoed in other religiously framed rural movements through Independence and the Cristero era
- *part_of:* the resistance and adaptation arc of [[western-mexico-indigenous-colonial-modern]] and [[colonial-mesoamerica-native-peoples]]

Sedentary southern peoples of the Center-West (including the recently conquered [[purepecha|Purépecha]] zone around [[tzintzuntzan|Tzintzuntzan]]) did **not** join the rising — a failure of cross-frontier alliance Van Young flags as critical to the war’s containment.

## Historiography

**Source base and gaps.** Van Young notes that colonial rebellions involving indigenous peoples in the Center-West are **“still hardly studied.”** Ideology of the Mixtón rising is “poorly known” beyond its millenarian outline. His synthesis privileges older narrative and documentary treatments (López Portillo y Weber; Ahumada on Mixtón and Zacateco–Guachichil aftermath) within a chapter better served overall by anthropologists/archaeologists than by ethnohistorians for the longer indigenous past.

**Framing.** Van Young places Mixtón as: (1) the standout early resistance episode of the Center-West; (2) continuous with the **Chichimeca** northern war system rather than a one-off; (3) an early instance of the **religious discourse of rural conflict** that he traces through El Indio Mariano, Independence-era village insurgency, “Religión y Tierras!,” Lozada, and the Cristero War. He does not adjudicate casualty totals beyond “thousands” of Indian lives/enslavements and “scores” of Spanish-side deaths among the named categories.

**Relation to conquest literature.** The war is post-[[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519]] but still within the first colonial generation of Nueva Galicia; it should not be collapsed into the fall of Tenochtitlan. Tarascan conquest chronology (Olid 1522; *cazonci* executed 1530) is a separate track — see [[purepecha]] and Warren’s *Conquest of Michoacán* (privileged by Van Young for Tarascan conquest, not for Mixtón detail).

**Attribution.** Narrative and causal claims on this page follow Van Young, CHNPA II.2 Ch. 15 (cache range R04), unless otherwise noted.
