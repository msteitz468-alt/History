## Narrative

On Easter Monday, 24 April 1916, an Irish Republican Brotherhood minority within the Irish Volunteers — acting with the support of James Connolly's socialist Irish Citizen Army — occupied the General Post Office and several other strategic buildings in Dublin and declared an Irish republic. After a week of fighting, the rising was suppressed by British forces who shelled the city centre with artillery and imposed martial law; the rebel leaders surrendered unconditionally. Fifteen leaders were subsequently executed by firing squad; others, including [[eamon-de-valera]], were imprisoned.

The rising was a small, localized event — perhaps 1,500 combatants, centered on Dublin — but its consequences were transformative. The executions converted public opinion that had been largely indifferent or hostile to the rising into sympathy, accelerated the collapse of the Irish Parliamentary Party's home rule politics, and established the precedent of armed separatist republicanism that shaped the [[irish-war-of-independence-1919]].

## Context: Dublin Lockout 1913

The immediate social context of the rising lay in the catastrophic labour dispute of 1913, when Dublin's employers — led by William Martin Murphy — locked out members of the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union. James Connolly organized the Irish Citizen Army as a workers' militia during the lockout; the dispute's violence 'discredited the Dublin police, the army, the courts, and the Irish administration more generally, weakening the state's legitimacy and giving impetus to separatism' (Whelehan in [[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], ch. 31). Witness statements later confirmed that 'some 1916 rebels were politicized by clashes with the police during the lockout.' However, 1913's labour defeat also showed the limits of syndicalist direct action, and the question of how the lockout's failure shaped attitudes toward revolutionary violence remains contested.

## The First World War as Catalyst

The [[first-world-war-1914]] was the decisive triggering context. It created conditions more favourable to rebellion than IRB activist Tom Clarke had experienced in his lifetime: the prospect of German military assistance, the possibility of a stake in post-war peace negotiations, and the influence of martial values on the rebels' own thinking. The shelling of Dublin's city centre with artillery and the imposition of martial law 'befitted a war more than an insurrection. This disproportionate response was also suggestive of the colonial dimensions of Ireland's status and it would be "difficult to envision the same tactics being so readily deployed in a British city"' (Whelehan, quoting McGarry). The war also temporarily divided the Irish Volunteers: Redmond's Woodenbridge speech committed the main body (180,000 strong) to the British war effort; only 10,000 separatists under Eoin MacNeill refused, and it was an IRB minority within that minority — with Connolly's Citizen Army — that staged the rising.

## The Rising Itself

The rebels occupied the GPO (Parnell Street), the Four Courts, Boland's Mill, and other strategic positions. The Proclamation of the Irish Republic, read by Patrick Pearse outside the GPO, declared Ireland a sovereign independent state and guaranteed equal rights and opportunities to all citizens regardless of religion — a document less secular and less social in outlook than the Fenian republic of 1867, reflecting what Owen McGee describes as the eclipse of nineteenth-century progressive Fenianism by the more Catholic, Irish-Ireland outlook of Pearse's generation.

Revisionist interpretations viewed the rising primarily as a 'romantic gesture, designed by poets for symbolic but not pragmatic reasons.' Fearghal McGarry's study of the rising 'from within and below' (using Bureau of Military History witness statements opened in 2003) reveals a more complex picture: many rebels 'simply preferred insurrection to inaction, as "an act of propaganda", and were well aware of the minimal possibilities for military success. They believed themselves to be participating in a regular military enterprise, legitimized by adherence to the rules of war, exercise of restraint, and codes of chivalry.'

The choice of conventional rather than guerrilla tactics was deliberate and contested: volunteer leaders Bulmer Hobson and Eoin MacNeill advocated 'hedge-fighting' or guerrilla tactics as more realistic, but the rebels chose a conventional style of warfare 'that appealed to gendered ideas of military honour and masculine identities that were shaped by the wartime context.'

Women organized in Cumann na mBan played supporting but subordinate roles in most locations; the Irish Citizen Army was somewhat different, assigning female members combatant roles at St Stephen's Green, though few were armed.

## Key Actors

- **Tom Clarke** (1858–1916): the senior IRB figure; had spent 15 years in British prisons for conspiring to bomb British cities in the 1880s; returned to Ireland in 1907 after years in Brooklyn; the organizational architect of the rising alongside Seán MacDermott
- **Patrick Pearse** (1879–1916): the public face; schoolteacher and poet; President of the Provisional Government; his rhetoric of blood sacrifice has generated the most historiographical debate
- **James Connolly** (1868–1916): syndicalist and socialist; commanded the Citizen Army; had organized the 1913 lockout; his inclusion gave the rising a labour dimension, though social radicalism was subsequently subordinated to nationalist narratives
- **[[eamon-de-valera]]**: commanded the Boland's Mill garrison; his American citizenship may have saved him from execution; went on to dominate Irish politics for the following half-century
- **[[michael-collins]]**: fought in the GPO; his analysis of the rising's military failures shaped the guerrilla strategy of the subsequent War of Independence

## Aftermath and Consequences

The executions — fifteen leaders shot between 3 and 12 May 1916, along with Roger Casement hanged in August — transformed public opinion. The mass arrests and internment without trial of thousands, many with no involvement in the rising, created a generation of radicalized prisoners in British internment camps (notably Frongoch in Wales). The released internees formed the organizational core of Sinn Féin's electoral machine in 1917–18. The December 1918 general election, inflamed by the 1918 conscription crisis, gave Sinn Féin 73 seats; the party abstained from Westminster and established Dáil Éireann in Dublin on 21 January 1919 — the same day an ambush at Soloheadbeg, County Tipperary, killed two policemen, conventionally marking the start of the [[irish-war-of-independence-1919]].

## Historiography

The rising's interpretation has been intensely contested. Earlier nationalist historiography treated it as the founding act of the Irish nation; revisionist historians of the 1970s–90s emphasized its romantic irrationalism, the IRB's manipulation of the Volunteers, and the limited public support before British repression. The 2003 opening of the Bureau of Military History witness statements transformed the field, enabling 'from below' social history of the kind Fearghal McGarry (2010) and others have pursued. The rising's relationship to the 1913 lockout, the First World War, and the international cycle of post-war violence has been the focus of newer transnational approaches.
