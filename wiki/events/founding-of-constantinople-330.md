# The Founding of Constantinople

The **founding of Constantinople** — Constantine's refoundation of **Byzantium** as **"New Rome,"**
dedicated on **11 May 330** — created the eastern imperial capital that would anchor the Roman world
for over a thousand years. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 4 (**Averil Cameron**).

## Narrative

After defeating Licinius at nearby **Chrysopolis** (324), [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]
renamed **Byzantium** — superbly sited on the Bosphorus controlling the straits between Europe and Asia
— as the **"city of Constantine."** He endowed it with the apparatus of an imperial centre: a great
oval **forum** with his statue on a porphyry column, the **Augusteum** square, a **senate house**, a
**hippodrome** adjoining the **palace**, walls, and a **bread distribution** like Rome's to attract
settlers. He also built churches and a **mausoleum** ringed with twelve empty sarcophagi for the
apostles — and the (later completed) S. Sophia and Holy Apostles. The city was dedicated on 11 May 330.

## Causal Analysis

Cameron stresses that Constantinople was, in essence, a **tetrarchic capital** in the mould of
Thessalonica or Serdica — the natural product of the [[roman-tetrarchy|tetrarchic]] system of mobile
emperors ruling from frontier "sub-capitals," now given permanent and monumental form at a strategic
site near the eastern and Danubian frontiers.

## Consequence Analysis

Cameron is careful to **deflate the myth** of a city "founded as a Christian capital": its
Christianization was **gradual** (pagan temples remained, even new ones per Zosimus), and Constantine
was at least as concerned to provide the **secular accoutrements of an imperial centre** as to build
churches. Paradoxically it was **Rome** — which Constantine never visited after 326 — that received the
great Constantinian churches, while at Constantinople building concentrated on the **secular and
imperial**. In the long run, however, "New Rome" became the enduring capital of the Eastern Roman
([[byzantine-empire|Byzantine]]) Empire and a Christian metropolis without rival.

## Significance

The foundation shifted the empire's centre of gravity decisively **eastward** and created the city
that would outlast the Western empire by a millennium — a permanent imperial residence that completed
the late-Roman eclipse of Rome and the senate.

## Historiography

Constantine’s New Rome is central to any account of the late empire’s eastward shift. Debates include how “Christian” the foundation was from the start (Gibbon’s “never profaned by idols” is overstated by modern standards), how far the city was an artificial colony at the expense of older cities, and how much the foundation contributed to western decline versus eastern survival. CAH and Byzantine scholarship stress gradual Christianization of urban cult; Gibbon (below) makes the foundation a pillar of his “new capital, new policy, new religion” package and later credits it with preserving the East more than ruining the West.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]] makes the foundation a pillar of Constantine’s “new capital, a new policy, and a new religion.” **Gibbon’s views** (often at odds with modern caution that Christianization of the city was gradual).

- **Bequest after Licinius:** Constantine left the empire “a new capital, a new policy, and a new religion” — innovations consecrated by later ages.
- **Site:** preferred the confines of Europe and Asia to curb Danube barbarians and watch Persia; “the incomparable position of Byzantium… guarded by nature against a hostile attack, whilst it was accessible on every side to the benefits of commerce”; “formed by nature for the centre and capital of a great monarchy.”
- **Divine guide and lance:** God commanded the foundations; on foot with a lance Constantine traced the boundary — “I shall still advance… till He, the invisible guide who marches before me, thinks proper to stop.”
- **Art by spoliation:** in the “decline of the arts,” “the cities of Greece and Asia were despoiled of their most valuable ornaments.”
- **Artificial colony:** “this artificial colony was raised at the expense of the ancient cities of the empire”; senators virtually commanded with palaces, lands, pensions.
- **Egyptian corn:** annual grain fed “a lazy and insolent populace, at the expense of the husbandmen of an industrious province.”
- **Title and name:** dedication as Second or New Rome; “the name of Constantinople” prevailed; Gibbon asserts the capital “was never profaned by the worship of idols.”

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

After defeating Licinius at Adrianople (July **324**), besieging Byzantium, and winning at Chrysopolis on the Bosporus, Constantine proclaimed foundation of a new city at Byzantium bearing his name before year’s end; by spring **325** Licinius and his son had been put to death at Thessalonica. Mitchell’s narrative thus anchors the foundation proclamation in the **324** campaign aftermath (dedication/formal foundation conventionally **330**).
