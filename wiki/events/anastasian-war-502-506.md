# The Anastasian War, 502–506

The first major **Roman–Sasanian war** of the sixth century, fought between the emperor **Anastasius I**
and the shah **Kavadh I** over the Mesopotamian frontier — the opening round of the century-long contest
that would run through the reigns of Justinian and Khusrau down to Heraclius.

## Narrative

When **Kavadh I**, needing money after his restoration to the Persian throne, demanded the customary
**subsidies** and was refused by **Anastasius**, he invaded Roman territory in **502**. He took
Theodosiopolis in Armenia, then besieged **Amida**, which fell in **January 503** after a long and
brutal siege. The Roman response in 503 was large but poorly coordinated — its divided generals failed
to retake Amida and suffered reverses — but in 504–505 Roman forces recovered the initiative, blockaded
Amida into surrender, and raided Persian territory. With both sides distracted (the Persians by Hunnic
pressure in the Caucasus), a **truce was agreed in 506**, restoring the pre-war frontier.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

caused_by: Anastasius's **refusal of the subsidies** Persia regarded as owed for the joint defence of
the Caucasus passes, and Kavadh's fiscal need. produced: the traumatic **sack of Amida** and heavy
devastation of Roman Mesopotamia; and, in its aftermath, Anastasius's decision to **fortify Dara**
(Anastasiopolis) close to the frontier as a forward base — a provocative strongpoint that became a
flashpoint of later wars. enabled: the **sixth-century cycle of Roman–Persian conflict** that
**[[justinian|Justinian]]** inherited; the war is the immediate background to the long frontier struggle
narrated by Procopius.

## Historiography

The outstanding contemporary source is the **[[watt-pseudo-joshua-stylite|Chronicle of Pseudo-Joshua the
Stylite]]**, written at Edessa within a year or two of the war — uniquely detailed on the siege of Amida,
the conduct of the campaign, and the famine and plague that preceded it. It can be set beside Procopius
and the Syriac chronicle tradition (Pseudo-Zachariah).
