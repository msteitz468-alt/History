# Pueblo Revolt of 1680

Coordinated rising of [[pueblo-peoples|Pueblo]] communities in New Mexico that expelled Spanish colonial authorities for roughly twelve years—the most successful Indigenous overthrow of European rule in North America in the colonial era. Seeds in sixteenth-century entrada and [[onate-new-mexico-1598|Oñate colonization]]; full Southwest colonial narrative in Lamar & Truett, [[sources/cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v1-2|CHNPA I.2]] ch. 10 (also light treatment in Trigger & Swagerty, CHNPA I.1 ch. 6).

## Narrative

### Path to revolt (1598–1680)

From the [[onate-new-mexico-1598|Oñate]] colony onward, Indian–European and Indian–Indian relations “went from bad to worse.” Successive governors demanded tributes of woven cloth, cattle, labor, and other items as taxes the Pueblos had never paid; missions demanded tribute and labor to farm mission lands; encomienda grantees expected Indian labor. Spanish authorities sent captured [[apache|Apache]], [[navajo-dine|Navajo]], and Ute Indians south for sale as mining laborers, escalating Athapaskan raiding that forced abandonment of three southern Tiwa villages in the 1670s.

Franciscans, aided by Spanish soldiers, raided the kivas and publicly burned masks and sacred objects (katsina paraphernalia). They also hanged some Indians as witches. These religious assaults, piled on extraction and multi-directional raiding, led many Puebloans to organize for resistance.

### August 1680 rising

In August 1680 the Pueblos rose in a general revolt under leaders including **Popé of San Juan Pueblo** and **Francisco El Ollita of San Ildefonso**. The revolt killed **over 400 Spanish colonists and twenty-one Franciscan missionaries** and expelled the rest. Survivors fled toward El Paso del Norte.

With the Spaniards gone, Pueblo communities set about removing vestiges of alien rule: European livestock was slaughtered, fruit trees pulled up, and people waded into the river to wash away the taint of baptism.

### Reconquest and renegotiated order (1692–96)

Inter-Pueblo unity dissolved once the common enemy was gone. When Diego de Vargas led an army to reoccupy the region between **1692 and 1694**, he reconquered the separate villages one by one. Reconquest was brutal but did **not** restore the antebellum status quo:

- Catholic priests largely **gave up eradicating Pueblo religious ceremonies** (though witchcraft trials continued)
- The **encomienda system was abolished**
- Use of Indians for **tribute labor declined**
- Spanish and Pueblos were forced into **greater cooperation** against increased [[apache|Apache]], [[comanches|Comanche]], and [[navajo-dine|Navajo]] raids—especially necessary given Pueblo demographic collapse (estimated **60,000 in 1600 → ~15,000 in the 1700s**, largely from Old World diseases; only **nineteen of ~sixty** original villages remained by 1700)

After de Vargas reconquest (1692–96), Pueblo refugees also joined Navajo communities in the San Juan country—transferring herding, weaving, pottery, and stone-hogan skills that reshaped later Diné society.

## Causal Analysis

Multi-causal pathway in Lamar & Truett (not monocausal missionary or drought models alone):

- `caused_by:` Franciscan religious suppression—kiva raids, public burning of sacred objects, hangings as witches
- `caused_by:` colonial extraction—gubernatorial tribute, mission labor, encomienda
- `contributed_to:` by Athapaskan slave export intensifying Apache/Navajo/Ute raiding and southern Tiwa abandonments
- `preceded_by:` [[onate-new-mexico-1598]] violence pattern (food seizure, rape, murder; Acoma siege, killings, and maiming of captives) that “planted seeds” for revolt
- `preceded_by:` [[coronado-expedition-1540]] as first Spanish military imprint on Pueblo country (Tiguex, etc.) without permanent colony
- `contributed_to:` by drought and multi-directional raiding pressures on an already narrowed subsistence margin (Spanish food/labor demands strained the “narrow margin between plenty and starvation”)
- `part_of:` [[greater-southwest-california-colonial-1880s]] Spanish New Mexico arc

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` temporary end of Spanish civil and religious control in the Rio Grande Pueblos; expulsion to El Paso; deaths of 400+ colonists and 21 Franciscans
- `produced:` deliberate cultural decolonization ritual (livestock slaughter, orchard destruction, river washing of baptism)
- `followed_by:` fragmentation of inter-Pueblo military unity; de Vargas reconquest village-by-village 1692–94
- `produced:` post-reconquest colonial bargain—encomienda abolished, tribute labor declined, ceremonial life less aggressively suppressed
- `enabled:` Spanish–Pueblo military cooperation against Apache, Comanche, and Navajo raiders in the eighteenth-century “island” of New Mexico enclaves
- `contributed_to:` Navajo ethnogenesis after reconquest—Pueblo refugees (Jemez especially noted) modified Navajo skills and worldviews
- `concurrent_with:` broader Athapaskan horse/raiding transformation and later Comanche ascent on the eastern flank
- Scale: immediate regional (New Mexico colony); consequential at civilizational scale as proof that coordinated Indigenous revolt could erase a European province for a generation and force structural renegotiation of colonial rule

## Actors

- **Pueblo leadership:** Popé (San Juan); Francisco El Ollita (San Ildefonso); multi-village coalition spanning independent linguistic communities that normally lacked intervillage political tradition
- **Spanish:** civil governors and encomenderos; Franciscan missionaries; Diego de Vargas (reconquest)
- **Neighboring Athapaskans and later Comanches:** simultaneously enemies (raiders, slave targets) and, for some Pueblos post-reconquest, destinations for refugee flight ([[navajo-dine]])
- See also [[pueblo-peoples]], [[hopi]] (Western Pueblos’ different long-run trajectory of non-submission)

## Historiography

**CHNPA treatment.** Trigger & Swagerty (I.1 ch. 6) document the revolt lightly as an outcome path of sixteenth-century contact and Oñate-era atrocity. Lamar & Truett (I.2 ch. 10) supply the load-bearing narrative: extraction cascade, kiva desecration and witch hangings, August 1680 numbers, decolonization rites, reconquest without restoration of encomienda or full ceremonial eradication, and the post-1690s Spanish–Pueblo defensive partnership.

**Scholarly frame.** Standard Spanish Borderlands and ethnohistorical literature (Spicer *Cycles of Conquest*; Weber; John; Forbes among works the bibliographic essay privileges) treats 1680 as multi-causal. Debates center on: relative weight of religious persecution vs labor/tribute vs ecological/raiding crisis; degree of pan-Pueblo coordination vs opportunistic village alliances; Popé’s role in oral and documentary tradition; how much the post-1692 order represents Indigenous victory versus Spanish strategic learning. Atrocity and sacred-object destruction are first-class content—not softened.

**Collection note.** Strong primary and secondary base for New Mexico; Western Pueblo (Hopi/Zuni) trajectories and Athapaskan perspectives require separate specialist layers beyond this synthesis chapter.
