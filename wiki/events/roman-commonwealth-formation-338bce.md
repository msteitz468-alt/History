# Formation of the Roman Commonwealth

## Overview

The settlement that Rome imposed after the **[[latin-war-340-338bce|Latin War]]**
in **338 BCE** — worked out in detail through c. 329 BCE — was the most consequential
institutional innovation in the history of the early Republic. It dissolved the
[[latin-league|Latin League]] as a political entity and organized the peoples of
Latium and Campania into a structured hierarchy of statuses, creating what Arnold
Toynbee called the **Roman commonwealth** (*nomen Romanum*). This institution
provided Rome with the manpower and political template to conquer peninsular Italy
over the following three-quarters of a century.

Cornell (CAH VII.2, Ch. 8) endorses De Sanctis's judgment that the settlement of
338 BCE was the **critical turning-point** (*momento critico*) of Roman history.

## The Two Principles

The settlement operated on **two organizing principles**:

1. **Individual treatment**: Rome dealt with each defeated community separately
   rather than as a group. Leagues and confederations were dissolved; the Latin
   League ceased to exist as a political entity. Each community was bound to Rome
   directly, with no lateral ties to other communities.

2. **Hierarchy of statuses**: A set of distinct juridical categories was established,
   so that Rome's subjects were divided into communities with specific, defined rights
   and obligations. This created a hierarchy within the Roman commonwealth.

## The Settlement Terms (Livy VIII.14)

### (a) Incorporated Latin Communities — Full Citizenship

Several Latin cities were incorporated into the Roman state and their inhabitants
made **full Roman citizens** (*optimo iure*):
- **Lanuvium**: full citizenship
- **Aricia**: full citizenship
- **Nomentum**: full citizenship
- **Pedum**: full citizenship
- **Tusculum**: citizenship restored (it had joined the revolt but was pardoned
  after the ringleaders were executed — an early and significant example of Roman
  clemency to a formerly loyal community)

Additionally:
- **Velitrae**: harsh treatment — walls razed, ruling class banished, land
  distributed to Roman settlers; remaining Veliterni given Roman citizenship
- **Antium**: citizenship granted; fleet surrendered; ships' rams (*rostra*) displayed
  on the speakers' platform in the Roman Forum (hence the name *Rostra*); a
  Roman citizen coastal garrison established

These communities became self-governing **municipia** on the Tusculum model.
The censors of 332 BCE registered the new citizens, assigning most to existing
tribes and creating two new ones (Maecia and Scaptia) for Lanuvium and Velitrae.

### (b) Latin Communities Not Incorporated

- **Tibur** (Tivoli) and **Praeneste**: retained independent allied status but
  forced to cede some territory — they were too powerful to absorb directly and
  had shown relative restraint
- The remaining Latin cities were freed from the League but permitted to exist
  as **sovereign communities**, retaining the rights of *conubium* and *commercium*
  with Roman citizens — though initially forbidden to exercise these rights with
  each other (breaking lateral Latin bonds)

**Latin status redefined.** This was the crucial conceptual transformation: from
this point, **Latin status was no longer an ethnic category** (membership in the
Latin League of Latium) but a **portable set of legally defined rights** (*ius
Latii*) — conubium, commercium, and ius migrationis — that Rome could bestow on
any community by legislative act. New Latin communities could be created simply
by founding a colony with Latin status. This redefinition enabled the subsequent
colonization program that planted Latin colonies throughout Italy.

### (c) Communities Outside Latium — Civitas sine Suffragio

For the non-Latin peoples whom Rome had defeated — primarily the Campanians and
Volscians — the settlement created the category of **civitas sine suffragio**
(citizenship without suffrage):
- **Capua, Suessula, Cumae** (Campanian cities): civitas sine suffragio
- **Acerrae**: added 332 BCE
- **Fundi, Formiae** (Volscian towns): civitas sine suffragio
- **Privernum**: added 329 BCE

The *cives sine suffragio* bore all the obligations of Roman citizenship — especially
military service — but held no political rights: they could not vote in Roman assemblies
or hold office at Rome. As communities they retained their native institutions and
became self-governing *municipia*. Their status was similar in practice to the Latin
allies (who also had *conubium* and *commercium* with Rome) but juridically distinct:
Latins were technically foreigners (*peregrini*), while the Campanians and Volscians
were technically citizens (*cives*).

## Colonization as the Fourth Instrument

Alongside enfranchisement, allied status, and civitas sine suffragio, **Latin
colonization** was the fourth instrument of the settlement. Starting with Cales
(334 BCE), Rome planted a series of autonomous communities with Latin status
at strategic nodes throughout the peninsula:

- Colonies of 2,500–6,000 adult males and their families received land allotments
  and were constituted as self-governing communities with Latin rights
- The colonists (Roman and allied) were drawn from the proletariat; they received
  land and became a property-owning class with Latin status
- The colonies were simultaneously military strongholds, Romanized cultural enclaves
  (Latin-speaking, Roman in institutions), and demographic relief valves

Toynbee's assessment remains apt: the constitutional innovations of 338 BCE gave
the Roman commonwealth "the maximum capacity for expansion, combined with the
maximum solidity of structure."

## The Commonwealth Structure

The resulting Roman commonwealth comprised four distinct categories of community,
each in a bilateral relationship with Rome:
1. **Full citizens** (in the ager Romanus proper and municipia optimo iure)
2. **Citizens sine suffragio** (in the municipia sine suffragio)
3. **Latin colonists and Latin allies** (with the portable ius Latii)
4. **Italian allies** (with individual bilateral treaties)

No lateral ties existed among these categories; each was bound only to Rome.
The commonwealth was explicitly **not a federation**: there was no federal assembly,
no mechanism for allied consultation on policy, no allied participation in
Roman decision-making. All strategic decisions rested with the Roman Senate
and people.

**Scale by 338 BCE** (Afzelius's estimates): ager Romanus c. 5,525 km², population
c. 347,300 free persons; total commonwealth c. 484,000 persons — already the most
powerful military state in peninsular Italy.

**Scale by 264 BCE**: ager Romanus c. 26,850 km², population c. 900,000; total
commonwealth over 3 million persons — larger than any single opponent could match.

## Significance

The formation of the Roman Commonwealth was not the result of philosophical
deliberation but of institutional improvisation: Rome needed to manage conquered
peoples without transforming its city-state structure beyond recognition. The solution
— portable Latin status, individual bilateral treaties, the municipium model, and
colonization — turned out to be an almost infinitely scalable system.

The commonwealth's **dynamic character** was its most remarkable feature. As Cornell
notes: "The institution of the self-governing municipium enabled the Roman state to
go on extending its territory and incorporating new communities without having to make
any radical changes to its rudimentary system of centralized administration."

## Historiography

The settlement is primarily attested in Livy VIII.14, a systematic analytical chapter
that Cornell regards as among the most valuable in the entire tradition. Its basis
may be the genuine record of the settlement; though elaborated, it reflects a real
institutional transformation visible in subsequent Roman history. The exact terms for
each community are sometimes disputed; Velitrae's and Antium's status (full citizenship
vs. *sine suffragio*) was debated in antiquity and by modern scholars. Cornell defends
full citizenship for the incorporated communities.
