## Narrative

The **Mihna** (Arabic: "inquisition" or "trial") was a caliphal examination (*imtihan*) by which the 'Abbasid caliphs al-Ma'mun (813–833), al-Mu'tasim (833–842), and al-Wathiq (842–847) compelled Islamic scholars to affirm the Mu'tazili doctrinal position that the [[quran|Qur'an]] was a created entity, not God's uncreated eternal speech. The inquisition ended when al-Mutawakkil (847–861) abandoned it in 848 and restored favor to the Hadith folk.

The immediate trigger was the Mu'tazili theologians' access to caliphal patronage and their campaign against what they regarded as the quasi-idolatrous "association" of the Qur'an with God's own being — treating the Qur'an as uncreated seemed to them to compromise divine unity (tawhid) by positing a second eternal entity alongside God. With caliphal backing, they tested the 'ulama on this doctrinal point, imprisoning or flogging those who refused to comply.

The most prominent resister was **[[ibn-hanbal-ahmad|Ahmad ibn Hanbal]]** (780–855), whom al-Ma'mun specifically selected as the chief example. Ibn Hanbal refused to recant under threat of death. Al-Ma'mun's own timely death spared him execution, but he underwent long imprisonment and became a hero to the Hadith folk and their popular following in Baghdad.

## Causal Analysis

### Proximate Causes

The Mihna was enabled by:
- The Mu'tazilis' intellectual confidence that their rationalist theology was superior to the textualism of the Hadith folk.
- Al-Ma'mun's ambition to assert caliphal authority over the 'ulama — reversing the structural autonomy they had gained under the [[abbasid-caliphate|'Abbasids]].
- The prior period (under al-Rashid, 786–809) when the Hadith folk had themselves used popular pressure to persecute dissidents, making the Mu'tazili counter-attack possible.

### Structural Causes

Deeper than the doctrinal dispute was a contest over who held authority in Islamic religious life. The [[ulama]] had built their position on the claim that legal and doctrinal authority resided in the community of scholars recognizing each other's reliability — not in the caliph. The Mihna was a direct challenge to this claim. "Even though they recognized his position, the Sunnis remained as autonomous as the Shi'is; so far as possible, they vested legal authority in all the possessors of knowledge, 'ilm, literally the 'ulama'" (Hodgson). The Mihna was the one major attempt by the 'Abbasids to reverse this arrangement.

## Consequence Analysis

The Mihna's failure had consequences of civilizational scale:

1. **Doctrinal**: The doctrine of the uncreated Qur'an became entrenched in Sunni theology; the Mu'tazili position — that the Qur'an was created — was permanently discredited in mainstream Sunni circles.

2. **Institutional**: The principle that caliphal authority stopped at the boundaries of 'ulama-defined doctrine was definitively established. No subsequent 'Abbasid caliph attempted a comparable doctrinal inquisition. The [[ulama]] emerged as a structurally independent institution.

3. **Popular**: Ibn Hanbal's martyrdom-status gave the Hadith folk, and through them the broader populistic Shari'ah-minded tradition, a charismatic legitimacy they had not previously possessed. "The viewpoint of the Hadith folk was launched toward general success with its victory in Baghdad" (Hodgson).

4. **Scholastic**: The Hanbali madhhab — founded on Ibn Hanbal's principles — became one of the four canonical Sunni legal schools, uniquely committed to the exclusive authority of hadith and the Qur'an with minimal use of analogy.

## Historiography

The Mihna has been extensively studied as a case of state-church conflict in pre-modern Islam. Walter M. Patton's *Ahmad ibn Hanbal and the Mihna* (1897) provided an early English-language account but, Hodgson notes, "presupposes without examination some assumptions of the time when it was written — that the Mu'tazilis were 'rationalists' and probably licentious and that the Hadith folk were 'traditionalists' in the sense of holding to unaltered community tradition. The consequence is that the book misses all the problems."

More recent scholarship (Nawas, Hurvitz on Ibn Hanbal; Van Ess on Mu'tazilism) has refined the picture. The Mu'tazilis were not simply "rationalists" but represented a specific approach to divine transcendence; the Hadith folk were not simply "traditionalists" but were committed to a textualist and populistic program of Shar'i social order.
