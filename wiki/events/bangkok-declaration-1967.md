## Narrative

The Bangkok Declaration, signed in August 1967 by the deputy prime minister of Malaysia and the foreign ministers of Indonesia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand, created the Association of South-East Asian Nations. It replaced the earlier Association of Southeast Asia (ASA, 1961), which had been destroyed by the Malaysia-Philippines dispute over Sabah and Indonesia's armed confrontation against Malaysia. The Declaration was the product of a changed regional conjuncture: Suharto's New Order had ended confrontation, Ferdinand Marcos had restored Philippines-Malaysia diplomatic relations, and Singapore had become an independent republic anxious about its survival. Against the threatening background of the escalating Vietnam War, these states had for the first time genuine grounds "to bury their differences and seek regional co-operation."

ASEAN's founding text explicitly avoided military alliance language. Thanat Khoman, Thailand's foreign minister and the chief architect of reconciliation among the founding members, coined the term "collective political defence" to describe ASEAN's security rationale. The stated primary objective was "to accelerate the economic growth, social progress and cultural development in the region." Foreign military bases were acknowledged as temporary; ASEAN was to be open to any Southeast Asian state that accepted its principles. Burma and Cambodia declined to join.

The highest policy-making body was the Meeting of Foreign Ministers, convening annually. Economic ministers and subordinate committees met regularly. An ASEAN Secretariat was established in Jakarta following the 1976 Bali summit.

## Causal Analysis

The Bangkok Declaration was made possible by the resolution of the Indonesia-Malaysia confrontation (1963–6) — the immediate structural precondition. Beyond that, it reflected a convergence of threat perceptions: all five founding states were anti-communist, feared internal subversion supported by Hanoi or Beijing, and saw economic development as requiring regional stability. The shift in US policy — the beginning of the Vietnam drawdown, which was signalled by the Guam Doctrine in 1969 — reinforced ASEAN's importance as a non-American-led framework for regional order.

The failure of earlier regional attempts (ASA, Maphilindo) taught specific lessons: Indonesia had to be included; the organization could not have an explicitly anti-communist security mandate that would alienate non-aligned members; and bilateral disputes had to be managed within the framework rather than allowed to destroy it.

## Consequence Analysis

ASEAN grew slowly in its first decade. "For many years ASEAN developed slowly. Its member states were a motley collection of diverse cultures and competing economies, paying only lip service to the association's ideals." Bilateral tensions over Sabah, Singapore-Indonesia relations, and the Malaysia-Thailand border persisted. ASEAN's value was unproven until the communist victories in Indochina in 1975 and Vietnam's invasion of Cambodia in 1978, which finally produced unified ASEAN action and international recognition of the organization as a "body with political and diplomatic influence."

The Bangkok Declaration established the norms — non-interference in internal affairs, consensus, no military commitments — that would define ASEAN's character through the Cold War period and beyond.

## Historiography

There is no significant scholarly controversy over the founding facts. The interpretive question is whether ASEAN was primarily a security organization masquerading as an economic one, or vice versa. Lau Teik Soon and Leo Suryadinata (CHSEA Ch. 10) treat the security rationale as primary, with economic development as the means of achieving internal stability sufficient to resist communist subversion. This is consistent with the founding members' own framing.
