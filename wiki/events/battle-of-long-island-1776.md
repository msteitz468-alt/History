# The Battle of Long Island (27 August 1776)

> **⚔️ Staff-college tactical analysis:** see **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/battle-of-long-island|Long Island — Tactical Analysis]]** in the warfare hub for the full order of battle, the phased conduct of the action, and the principles-of-war assessment.

The largest battle of the war and a near-catastrophe for the **[[continental-army|Continental
Army]]**. Having evacuated [[siege-of-boston-1775|Boston]], the British returned in overwhelming
force — a "vast armada" of **427 vessels, 24,000 soldiers, and 10,000 sailors** — to take **New
York**. Washington, ordered by Congress to hold a city he and Charles Lee both judged untenable,
divided his army across Manhattan and Long Island.

## Narrative

[[howe-william|Howe]] repeated his Bunker Hill template in reverse: demonstrating against the
American center and right while marching **10,000 men overnight through the unguarded Jamaica
Pass** — watched by only five American officers, who were captured without raising the alarm. At
9 a.m. on 27 August the British rolled up the American rear; Putnam's line collapsed, General
Sullivan was captured, and only the stand of **Lord Stirling's** Maryland and Virginia Continentals
(driven into Gowanus Creek) bought time. Howe, again wary of storming fortifications and hoping for
reconciliation, halted before the Brooklyn Heights lines instead of finishing the army — letting
Washington **evacuate 9,000 men across the East River under fog** on the night of 29–30 August.
American losses ran to ~300 killed and over 1,000 captured.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the strategic imperative to contest **New York's great harbor**, and Congress's
  insistence on holding the city against military logic.
- `contributed_to:` the **failure to guard the Jamaica Pass**, enabling Howe's envelopment.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **loss of New York City** (held by the British until 1783) and a chain of defeats —
  Kips Bay, Fort Washington — and the demoralizing **retreat across New Jersey**.
- `enabled:` paradoxically, the army's survival, since Howe's restraint and the foggy evacuation
  preserved the force that would strike back at [[battle-of-trenton-1776|Trenton]].

## Historiography

Well documented through Howe's and Washington's own correspondence and the accounts of participants like Lord Stirling, the battle is a standard case study in the historiography of the Revolution's early "near-disasters," particularly Washington's contested decision (under congressional pressure) to divide his army to hold an indefensible city. Historians debate how much responsibility for the Jamaica Pass failure rests with Washington, subordinate generals, or simple bad luck, and there is a long-running discussion of Howe's "restraint" — whether his failure to press the advantage reflects genuine hope for reconciliation, caution born of Bunker Hill's casualties, or a missed opportunity to end the war outright. The successful night evacuation across the East River is celebrated in national memory in ways historians increasingly try to contextualize against the scale of the preceding defeat.
