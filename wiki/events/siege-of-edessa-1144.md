# Fall of Edessa (1144)

## Narrative

In late 1144 **[[imad-al-din-zangi|Imad al-Din Zangi]]** captured **[[edessa|Edessa]]**, capital of the first [[crusader-states|crusader state]] founded during the First Crusade. Count **Joscelin II** was absent; Zangi, campaigning against Artuqid and other local rivals as much as against Franks, seized the opportunity. After a siege, Muslim troops stormed the city (**24 December 1144**); the citadel fell shortly after. The loss of the oldest Frankish principality shocked Latin Christendom and became the immediate cause of the **[[second-crusade-1147|Second Crusade]]**.

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] details the city’s condition and the campaign’s mechanics. Edessa held a large ancient Christian population (Cobb: “perhaps forty thousand” — hedge) but was the poorest Latin state, a northern outlier worn by raids. Zangi benefited from an Artuqid-Hisn Kayfa succession and from Joscelin’s treaty with the new Artuqid; Joscelin left a small Armenian garrison and resided at **Tall Bashir**, while an open break with Raymond of Antioch reduced the chance of nearby relief — “walls, towers, and ramparts avail but little if there are none to man them.”

Zangi cast the campaign as *jihad* and summoned Turcoman tribes; a vast army with archers and siege gear, including sappers (some from Khurasan), undermined the walls. When a breach opened on **24 December 1144**, Muslim troops poured in; after heavy Muslim casualties came a sack — pillaging, slaying, capturing, ravishing, looting — until Zangi ordered a halt, promised rule of law, lightened taxes, and rebuilt fortifications. Aftermath: Zangi took **Saruj** and Frankish lands east of the Euphrates save **al-Bira**; Tall Bashir remained Joscelin’s exile court. The conquest sent regional shockwaves; in **September 1146**, while besieging an Arab rival at **Qalʿat Jaʿbar**, Zangi was murdered in his sleep.

After Zangi’s death Armenians rebelled; Joscelin recaptured the town but not the citadel; **[[nur-al-din|Nur al-Din]]** force-marched from Aleppo, Joscelin abandoned the city, and Nur al-Din sacked Edessa with free hand to his troops. **Ibn al-Athir** insists this second sack — not Zangi’s 1144 conquest — was when the city was truly plundered and depopulated: “Many people think that it was plundered when [Zangi] conquered it but this is not so.”

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Zangi’s military expansion and the temporary vulnerability of Edessa (Joscelin’s absence, thin garrison, rupture with Antioch).
`contributed_to:` chronic disunity among Franks and among Muslim amirs in the Jazira and northern Syria; Artuqid succession politics that freed Zangi’s hand.
`preceded_by:` decades of Zangid consolidation at Mosul and Aleppo after 1127.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` destruction of the County of Edessa as a major Frankish power (final expunging of residual Frankish territory followed under Nur al-Din by ~1150).
`enabled:` papal and Cistercian mobilization of the Second Crusade (1147–49).
`contributed_to:` the mid-century Muslim counter-offensive continued by **[[nur-al-din]]**; regional shock that energized Muslim audiences and demanded Frankish response.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

Cobb’s Islamic-side narrative stresses *jihad* packaging, Turcoman mobilization, sapper techniques, Zangi’s post-sack order to restore law (contrasting later Nur al-Din depopulation), and the interpretive frame that Zangi used *jihad* against Franks partly as cover for ambitions against Muslim rivals — a moral coda later sources attach to his murder. The 1146–47 second sack under Nur al-Din is essential for Muslim memory of the city’s ruin.

## Historiography

Latin writers framed Edessa as martyrdom of the first crusader conquest; Arabic sources place the campaign in Zangi’s regional power struggle and debate which sack ruined the city (Ibn al-Athir vs popular attribution to Zangi alone). [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] stresses Artuqid rivalries in the operational season, hedges population figures, and treats Edessa’s “crusade-defining” status as partly retrospective.
