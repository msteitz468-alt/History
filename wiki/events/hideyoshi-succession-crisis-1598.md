# Hideyoshi Succession Crisis (1598–1600)

The political crisis triggered by the death of [[actors/toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi Hideyoshi]] in September 1598, which left rule of Japan nominally to his five-year-old son Hideyori under a collective regency Hideyoshi had designed to prevent any single powerful daimyo from seizing power in the interim.

## Narrative

Hideyoshi's arrangement placed authority in a "Council of Five Elders" (the most powerful daimyo, including [[actors/tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]]) balanced against a "Council of Five Commissioners" of administrators (led by Ishida Mitsunari) intended to check the elders and safeguard Hideyori's eventual succession. The arrangement began unraveling almost immediately: Ieyasu, the most powerful of the Five Elders, moved to build his own alliances and political marriages among other daimyo in violation of Hideyoshi's restrictions, while Ishida Mitsunari organized opposition among daimyo suspicious of Ieyasu's ambitions. By 1600 the rival blocs had hardened into the Eastern Army (Ieyasu and his allies) and Western Army (nominally loyal to the Toyotomi house, organized by Ishida and other commanders), leading directly to the decisive [[events/battle-of-sekigahara-1600|Battle of Sekigahara]] in October 1600.

## Causal Analysis

The crisis was `caused_by` Hideyoshi's death before his heir reached majority, combined with the structural weakness of a regency arrangement that depended on cooperation among rival power-holders with no enforceable authority above them; it was `contributed_to` by long-standing personal and factional rivalries (particularly between Ieyasu and Ishida Mitsunari) that predated Hideyoshi's death.

## Consequence Analysis

The crisis `produced` the Battle of Sekigahara and, through Ieyasu's victory there, `enabled` his establishment of the Tokugawa shogunate in 1603 — ending over a century of civil warfare (the Sengoku period) and beginning over two and a half centuries of Tokugawa rule.

## Historiography

The crisis and Sekigahara are central events in Japanese historiography of the transition from the Sengoku to Edo periods; sources are relatively rich (contemporary letters, later Tokugawa-sponsored histories favorable to Ieyasu, and modern revisionist work questioning the inevitability of Ieyasu's rise) though much surviving narrative was shaped after the fact by the victorious Tokugawa regime's interest in presenting Ieyasu's actions as restrained and justified rather than opportunistic.
