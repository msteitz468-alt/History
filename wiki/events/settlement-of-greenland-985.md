# Norse Settlement of Greenland

## Narrative

Greenland was colonized c. 985 by settlers following Erik the Red. The settlement was divided into a large eastern settlement in the south and the much smaller western settlement farther north in modern Nuuk district. Radiocarbon dates suggest the landscape filled rapidly, with the eastern settlement probably settled a generation before the western.

The Greenlanders set up a chiefly society with assemblies as in Iceland and adopted Christianity around A.D. 1000. The economy was based partly on domestic stock but with considerable supplement from hunted caribou and seals. Walrus hide and ivory, polar bear and fox skins provided key export products. In 1127 the Greenlandic chieftains traded a live polar bear to the king of Norway to obtain their own bishop. By the fourteenth century Greenland boasted a monastery and nunnery as well as some of the largest stone churches in the North Atlantic.

Around A.D. 1200 the Norse and surviving Dorset Paleo-Eskimo were contacted by the Thule Inuit. Around A.D. 1350 the smaller Norse western settlement became extinct, and by around 1450 the larger eastern settlement followed suit.

## Causal Analysis

**Environmental drivers.** West Greenland is affected by a side stream of the North Atlantic Drift but is fundamentally arctic in climate, afflicted by summer drift ice. Settlers left the main channel and entered environments critically different from homelands. The flora was deceptively similar (dwarf willow, birch, grasses), but plants were closer to biological limits. Climate during initial landnám was warmer and more stable. Soils in Greenland are generally less prone to wind erosion than in Iceland, but deforestation and soil impacts occurred.

**Economic and social drivers.** The "ideal farm" of the Nordic homelands (high cattle and pig representation) exercised a strong hold on first settlers. Zooarchaeological evidence from early phases at chieftain's farm W 51 shows rich cattle and pig bones, more similar to landnám Iceland than contemporary Icelandic sites. Pigs prospered poorly; later samples show reduction in cattle and few or no pig bones. Fishing played a minor role; marine mammals (seals) and caribou were critical. Isotopic studies of human bones show a steady increase in marine foods consumed in later periods.

Contact with Dorset Paleo-Eskimo resulted in Norse settlements along the southwest coast and Dorset farther north. Thule Inuit (ancestors of modern Inuit) migrated from Alaska with sophisticated arctic hunting technology (including baleen whale hunting). Dynamics of Norse-Thule contact involved a mixture of friendly and hostile encounters, resulting in Thule migration into Norse settlement areas.

## Consequence Analysis

The colonies survived approximately 450–500 years before extinction. They established the westernmost outposts of Scandinavian culture in the North Atlantic. Factors in demise included climate cooling (Little Ice Age), Thule contact and competition, declining European connections, and vulnerabilities created by initial landnám settlement decisions and environmental impacts (deforestation, overgrazing by imported stock). The bishop's manor became a center of stratification (housing for over 100 cattle vs. 2-3 on most farms).

After the short-lived Vinland attempt (c. A.D. 1000), Iceland and Greenland remained the westernmost outposts until the wind went out of the Viking Age Atlantic voyages with integration into European Christendom.

## Historiography

Greenland certainly had its own sagas, annals, and written records, but these were lost when the settlements became extinct; only fragments remain. Documentary sources are a closed body. Modern research relies heavily on archaeology, zooarchaeology, paleoenvironmental studies, and radiocarbon. Isotopic analysis of bones has demonstrated dietary shifts. Debates continue on the relative weight of climate change, Inuit contact, and internal social/environmental factors. Recent work emphasizes contingency, resilience, and the long-term consequences of landnám choices rather than single-cause collapse.
