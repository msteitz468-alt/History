# The Corinthian War, 395–386 BCE

## Narrative

The **Corinthian War** was the coalition war in which **Thebes, [[places/athens|Athens]], Corinth and
Argos**, financed by [[achaemenid-empire|Persia]], tried to break the
[[spartan-hegemony|Spartan hegemony]]. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 4, Robin
Seager) shows the causes to be **resentment of Sparta's ruthless expansionism**, not
social or ideological division: Corinth and Thebes had been denied a share of the spoils
of 404 and alarmed by Spartan pressure in central Greece. The trigger was **Persian
gold**: in 396 the King's emissary **Timocrates** distributed subsidies to win a war in
Greece that would force Sparta to recall [[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]] from Asia. The
Theban leaders Ismenias and Androcleidas then "**with consummate skill**" engineered a
border quarrel between Phocis and Locris, giving Sparta a "morally plausible excuse" to
attack — and Boeotia the necessity of defending itself.

## The course of the war

- **395 — Haliartus.** The Spartans invaded Boeotia in two prongs; at **Haliartus**
  **[[lysander]] was killed** before [[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]] could arrive — a
  shocking blow that emboldened the coalition (Athens, Corinth and Argos now joined
  Thebes).
- **394 — Nemea and Coronea.** Sparta won the great land battle of **Nemea (Corinth)**
  and, after Agesilaus marched home from Asia "by the roads once taken by Xerxes," the
  hard-fought **Coronea**, where the wounded king beat a Theban-led army. But these
  victories settled nothing: "there was to be no further major land battle between the
  complete forces of the two sides."
- **394 — Cnidus.** The decisive stroke came at sea: the Athenian **[[conon]]**,
  commanding a Persian fleet with the satrap **Pharnabazus**, **annihilated the Spartan
  navy off Cnidus** (Agesilaus, encamped at Coronea, lied to his troops that Sparta had
  won). Conon and Pharnabazus then toured the Aegean expelling Spartan harmosts.
- **393 — the Long Walls rebuilt.** Conon came to Athens with Persian gold and his
  crews and **rebuilt the Long Walls and the fortifications of the Piraeus** — Persia
  knowingly strengthening Athens against Sparta. Conon was hailed as "liberator of the
  allies," and Athens at once began reviving imperial claims.
- **391–388 — stalemate and Lechaeum.** The land war narrowed to the Corinthia. At
  **Lechaeum (390)**, the Athenian general **Iphicrates** used light-armed **peltasts**
  to cut to pieces a Spartan *mora* (regiment) of hoplites — a famous demonstration that
  the citizen phalanx was no longer invincible. **Thrasybulus** revived Athenian power
  in the Hellespont and the north until he was killed at Aspendus (389).

## The diplomatic resolution

Sparta's diplomat **Antalcidas** repeatedly sought to detach Persia by offering to
abandon the Greeks of Asia. When in 388 he cut Athens' Black Sea **corn supply** (as
[[lysander]] had in 405), and Persia tired of Athenian provocations (aid to Evagoras and
to rebel Egypt), [[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]] imposed the **[[kings-peace-386bce|
King's Peace]]** (386). Though Sparta "had done no more than hold her own" in the war,
she emerged its great beneficiary: as enforcer of the peace's **autonomy clause** she
broke up Theban control of Boeotia and Argive control of Corinth.

## Enforcing the King's Peace (Osborne)

[[osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed.), *Classical Greece 500–323 BC* (2000)]]
(the fourth-century period narrative) confirms the CAH picture of the **King's Peace of
387/6** and sharpens its political logic: the Peace formally recognized **Persian
overlordship of all Asia Minor and Cyprus**, but in the short term what mattered most in
mainland Greece was its **autonomy clause** — and it was **[[agesilaus-ii|Agesilaus]]**,
not the Persian King, who decided how that clause would be applied, using it to break up
both the union of **Corinth and Argos** and the **Boeotian confederacy**. Osborne treats
this as proof that Spartan, not Persian, interests actually shaped the Peace's
consequences.

Sparta then used the Peace era to coerce former allies further, in ways contemporaries
recognized as flagrant abuses of the "autonomy" the Peace itself proclaimed:
- **385 — Mantinea.** Sparta accused her ally **Mantinea** of aiding Argos, besieged the
  city, demolished its walls, and broke it up into four or five separate villages.
- **c. 380 — Phlius.** Sparta besieged **Phlius** over a property dispute involving
  restored exiles.
- **382 — the Theban Cadmea.** The Spartan commander **Phoebidas** seized the Theban
  acropolis in a "grotesque breach of the King's Peace" that split Spartan domestic
  opinion — Phoebidas was tried but only fined, while the pro-Athenian Theban leader
  **Ismenias** was tried and executed. This seizure is the proximate grievance behind
  Thebes's later refusal (371) to accept a renewed Common Peace on Spartan terms — the
  chain of escalation that runs directly to [[battle-of-leuctra-371bce|Leuctra]].

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: resentment of [[spartan-hegemony|Spartan]] expansionism; **Persian subsidy**;
the Theban contrivance of the Phocis–Locris quarrel. produced: the loss of the Spartan
fleet at **Cnidus** and the rebuilt Athenian **Long Walls**; a land stalemate;
ultimately the **[[kings-peace-386bce|King's Peace]]**, which "within a decade" Sparta
would see "turned against her" — but which Sparta first used, via Agesilaus's control of
the autonomy clause, to break up rival leagues and coerce Mantinea, Phlius, and Thebes.
The war is the textbook case of **Persia as arbiter** of Greek affairs — "the only power
to have derived unmitigated gain."
