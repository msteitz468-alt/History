# Indian Reorganization Act

Wheeler–Howard / Indian Reorganization Act (June 1934): ended the [[dawes-act-1887]] allotment regime, offered tribal self-government under federal reorganization, and became the structural template for mid–late twentieth-century tribal governments—even where tribes rejected the Act itself.

## Narrative

After FDR and Interior Secretary Harold Ickes appointed **John Collier** Commissioner of Indian Affairs (**1933**), first-year actions implemented much of the 1920s Indian Defense Association agenda as federal policy: religious freedom at all agencies; abolition of **mandatory Christian services at boarding schools**; cancellation of nearly all debts the government claimed Indian groups owed from past appropriations; halt to sales of allotted lands; intervention settling remaining Pueblo land disputes in favor of Indians.

Collier’s **1934** proposal package: (1) self-governing units that could act as business corporations; (2) promote Indian traditions; (3) abolish allotment and consolidate lands; (4) national court of Indian affairs. When the IRA became law in **June 1934** it had been stripped of its most controversial provisions—**no Indian court, no statement of support for Indian culture, no requirement that previously allotted land return under Indian jurisdiction**. Nevertheless, Hoxie stresses the “stunning prospect” of the U.S. government acting to restore and reorganize indigenous governments it had previously sought to destroy. Extended to **Alaska and Oklahoma in 1936**.

**Referenda:** **258** local votes; **164** communities organized under the Act; **94** rejected it. Collier was stunned by opposition; critics charged heavy-handed campaigning and threats to cut appropriations. Opposition included allottees fearing landless majorities, defenders of informal councils, and those who saw “**self-administration rather than self-government**” without an economic development program.

**Navajo rejection:** Pre-IRA investigations claimed Navajos grazed ~3× sustainable livestock; voluntary reduction 1933–34 failed; stock seized and destroyed in owners’ view. June **1935** IRA referendum (**98%** turnout) defeated by **384 votes**—stock reduction crucial; also factional contest between Chee Dodge (supported Collier reforms) and Jacob Morgan (anti–New Deal). After defeat, Indian Office still pressed a “tribal” charter; 1938 election under imposed regulations made Morgan chairman. Later 1950s council development (courts, police, power authority, newspaper; Navajo-Hopi Rehabilitation Act mineral revenues) became modern nation-building path *outside* formal IRA acceptance (Iverson: decade “witnessed the birth of the Navajo nation”).

**Other receptions:** New York Iroquois traditionals rejected IRA as threat to treaty-based sovereignty (only Wisconsin Oneidas among Iroquois accepted). Klamaths and Crows rejected despite Collier’s view of their “unity” and “progress.” Rosebud vote pitted boarding-school “progressives”/landless corporation supporters against Black Hills Treaty Council advocates who feared a new tribal organization would undermine treaty-based dealings.

- caused_by (as reform climate): Meriam Report (1928) documenting health, income, and boarding-school food crisis and naming allotment as origin of bleakness.
- preceded_by: Collier’s pre-statute first-year reforms; Pueblo All Pueblo Council / Bursum-bill fight coalition (Collier, Stella Atwood).
- contributed_to: Depression-era New Deal willingness to reverse Progressive-era individualization.
- contrasts_with prior: [[us-civilization-program]] assimilation instruments (boarding Christianity; severally).

## Consequence Analysis

- **Land:** of **2.75 million acres** acquired by Indian groups **1934–1940**, over 1 million from public domain, another 1 million submarginal Resettlement Administration/unclaimed homestead lands; only **20%** via purchase/exchange with allottees—most desirable lands lost over previous half-century remained beyond grasp. IRA loans small, mostly to already-successful individuals (default rate <1% of $5.5M commitments by 1944).
- **Education:** day schools 132→226; boarding enrollment 22,000→<15,000; Johnson-O’Malley public-school contracts.
- **Governments:** representative tribal structure largely established under IRA constitutions (Washburn); BIA prepared constitutions with anthropological advice allowing preexisting structures (e.g. Hopi village religious leaders); Collier’s tribal-unity model subsumed autonomous village/band/clan units—generating later Hopi and Sioux legitimacy challenges; critics charged “boiler plate” governments, energy-company control, corruption, puppetry.
- **Not overnight rebirth** (Washburn): many tribes—most notably Navajo—refused; liberal feature required tribal acceptance before Act applied; long adverse history led many to infer anything government wanted was not in Indian interest.
- Forward: Reagan-era “government-to-government” built on IRA tribal governments; template for [[tribal-self-determination]] even as [[termination-policy]] later tried to dismantle trust.

## Historiography

Hoxie emphasizes stripped statute still revolutionary in federal intent, contested local reception, and modest material restoration. Washburn stresses that tribal governments were not “reborn overnight,” that subtribal units were often subordinated to federally recognized “tribes,” and later claims Collier was “in effect vindicated” when radicals returned to working through tribal governments. Contested: self-government vs self-administration; whether IRA constitutions were authentic or BIA impositions; Navajo stock-reduction as environmental necessity vs colonial violence. Do not treat 164 acceptances as consensual endorsement without noting campaign pressure claims and 94 rejections.
