# Battle of the Little Bighorn (1876)

**Narrative**

On 25–26 June 1876, five companies of the 7th U.S. Cavalry under Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer were destroyed by a coalition of Lakota Sioux, Northern Cheyenne, and Arapaho warriors in the valley of the Little Bighorn River, Montana Territory. It was the largest and most decisive victory by Plains Indian forces against the U.S. Army during the Indian wars.

Custer's regiment formed the strike force of Brigadier General Alfred Terry's Dakota Column, part of a three-pronged summer campaign (with columns under Gibbon and Crook) to force "non-treaty" bands onto the Great Sioux Reservation. After locating an enormous village (estimates 1,000–2,000 lodges) on 25 June, Custer divided his regiment into three battalions: Reno (3 companies) to attack the southern end, Custer (5 companies) to move north along the bluffs for an envelopment, and Benteen (3 companies) in reserve with the pack train. Reno's attack was repulsed with heavy loss; his survivors reached the bluffs and later linked with Benteen. Custer's force was cut off, surrounded on high ground, and overwhelmed. No survivors from the five companies lived to report the sequence.

The village was one of the largest ever assembled on the northern plains; leaders included Sitting Bull (spiritual/political), Crazy Horse, and Gall. The fight lasted roughly one to two hours for Custer's command.

**Causal Analysis**

- **caused_by:** U.S. decision to open the Black Hills to miners after Custer's 1874 expedition publicized gold, in direct violation of the 1868 treaty.
- **contributed_to:** Failed diplomacy and the January 1876 ultimatum that labeled non-compliant bands "hostile."
- **preceded_by:** Crook's fight on the Rosebud (17 June) that bloodied the village but did not deter its movement.

**Consequence Analysis**

- **produced:** The iconic defeat that dominated headlines and fixed Custer in American memory.
- **contributed_to:** Renewed political will and resources for the final conquest of the northern plains tribes (1876–77 campaigns).
- **part_of:** The broader process of U.S. continental consolidation and Native dispossession.

**Historiography**

Immediate accounts emphasized heroism or blame. The 1879 Reno Court of Inquiry largely exonerated Reno and Benteen while leaving Custer's decisions open to interpretation. 20th-century military historians (Utley) reconstructed the fight from archaeology, survivor testimony, and Native accounts. Revisionist scholarship situates the battle within the larger pattern of treaty violation and genocidal pressure. Archaeological work in the 1980s–2000s refined casualty and movement models. Stiles emphasizes the battle as the logical endpoint of Custer's mismatch with the modernizing army and nation rather than a singular aberration.
