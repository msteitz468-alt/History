## Narrative

On 3 October 1990, the five newly-constituted eastern German states (Länder) acceded to the Federal Republic of Germany under Article 23 of the Basic Law. The German Democratic Republic ceased to exist. The accession was the culmination of a diplomatic and political process that had moved with remarkable speed from the fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 to full political unification less than eleven months later.

The unification process had two interlocking tracks: domestic German politics and international diplomacy. Chancellor [[helmut-kohl]] dominated both.

## Domestic Track

**Volkskammer Elections (18 March 1990).** The first free East German parliamentary elections were the critical domestic turning point. Kohl threw West German CDU organizational resources and his personal campaign presence into support for the Alliance for Germany (a coalition of East-CDU, Democratic Awakening, and the CSU-backed German Social Union). The Alliance won decisively — CDU alone received over 40 percent — on a platform of rapid unification via Article 23 and immediate currency union. The SPD finished a distant second (21.9%); civic groups (Alliance '90, Greens) who opposed or qualified unification received minimal support (2–3%). The result made Lothar de Maizière (CDU) the GDR's first democratically elected Prime Minister and secured a negotiating partner for Bonn committed to rapid, westward-extending unification.

**Currency, Economic, and Social Union (1 July 1990).** The first State Treaty, establishing monetary, economic, and social union, took effect 1 July 1990. The DM replaced the GDR mark. The exchange rate — 1:1 for wages, pensions, and small savings; 2:1 for larger savings amounts — was more favorable to East Germans than the Bundesbank recommended, reflecting political calculation (Kohl's campaign promise) over economic prudence. Critics including Bundesbank president Karl Otto Pöhl warned of inflationary pressure and rising eastern unemployment as labor costs outpaced productivity. Their concerns proved well-founded: East German industry, unable to compete, collapsed rapidly after currency union.

**Unification Treaty (August 1990).** The second State Treaty, the Unification Treaty negotiated by West German Interior Minister Wolfgang Schäuble, regulated GDR accession: property restitution (principle of "return over compensation" for property seized after 1949), treatment of Stasi-compromised public servants, and institutional transfer. Schäuble was explicit: "It is about the accession of the GDR to the Federal Republic, not the reverse operation." This meant the FRG's institutions, legal code, and administrative model were extended eastward rather than a new constitution being drafted — a decision with lasting social and psychological consequences for East Germans who felt passive recipients rather than co-creators of the new state.

## Diplomatic Track

The **2+4 process** (two German states plus four wartime powers: USA, USSR, UK, France) provided the international framework for unification's external dimension.

The key obstacles were: (1) Soviet opposition to a unified Germany in NATO; (2) French and British hesitations about German power; (3) the unresolved Polish border question.

**Poland and the Oder-Neisse line.** Kohl's initial hesitation to guarantee the Oder-Neisse border explicitly — he was concerned about alienating refugee associations and nationalist voters before the federal election — generated international anxiety. Eventually, on 21 June 1990, the Bundestag and Volkskammer passed a joint resolution recognizing the eastern frontier, and the formal border treaty was concluded after unification.

**Caucasus Breakthrough (July 1990).** The decisive moment was Kohl's meeting with Gorbachev in the Caucasus (Stavropol region). The USSR accepted: unified Germany's membership in NATO; full German sovereignty; German troop limits of 370,000; no extension of non-German NATO forces or nuclear weapons to former GDR territory; and Soviet troop withdrawal from eastern Germany within four years. In exchange, Germany provided financial support for Soviet troop withdrawal and offered a bilateral friendship treaty. The question of whether Kohl explicitly or implicitly promised that NATO would not subsequently expand eastward remains historiographically contested (Sarotte's research suggests he cultivated deliberate ambiguity).

**2+4 Treaty (12 September 1990).** The Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany, signed by the two German states and the four powers, suspended Allied reserved rights over Germany as a whole upon unification — definitively closing the "German question" opened in 1871 and reactivated catastrophically in 1933.

**CSCE endorsement.** The Paris CSCE summit praised unification as "an important contribution to a just and lasting order of peace for a united, democratic Europe."

## Rival Conceptions of Unification

The process was contested along two dimensions:
- **National dimension:** Article 23 (rapid accession) vs. Article 146 (new constitution negotiated by two equal states). The SPD, churches, trade unions, East German civic groups, and reform communists favored a more gradual process. Kohl and the Alliance for Germany pushed Article 23.
- **Social dimension:** How would the costs be distributed? Kohl minimized discussion of costs; Lafontaine demanded frank accounting; trade unions and churches warned about the economically vulnerable.

## Consequences

Immediate: The eastern German economy collapsed under currency union and market exposure. The Treuhandanstalt (Trusteeship Agency) tasked with privatizing eastern state-owned firms struggled to find buyers; unemployment soared. The "blossoming landscapes" Kohl promised took far longer and more public investment to materialize than initially projected.

Long-term: Reunification anchored a unified Germany deeply in Western institutions. France insisted on the Maastricht Treaty and European monetary union as the price of French acceptance — unified Germany's economic weight made monetary union essential for maintaining Franco-German balance. See [[german-european-integration]]. The eastern German transition produced persistent east-west disparities in income, employment, and political trust that remained visible decades later.

## Historiography

Unification historiography centers on: (1) Was rapid unification the right choice? Critics (Lafontaine, civic groups, SPD left) argued it constituted annexation and imposed unnecessary social costs; defenders (Kohl, Görtemaker) saw it as historically necessary and pragmatically appropriate. (2) Kohl's role — the literature largely credits him with masterly management of both domestic coalition-building and international diplomacy ("chancellor democracy" at its most potent). (3) The NATO expansion question — whether Western promises to the USSR were broken remains a live historiographical and geopolitical debate. (4) East German agency — historians increasingly attend to what East Germans wanted, contrasting the civic groups' "third way" aspirations with the working-class pragmatism that demanded the DM and unification.

Key texts: Görtemaker (1994), Jarausch (1994), Maier (1997), Sarotte (various works on 2+4 negotiations).
