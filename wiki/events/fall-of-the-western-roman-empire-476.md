# The Fall of the Western Roman Empire (476)

The deposition of the last western emperor, the boy **Romulus Augustulus**, by the general **Odoacer**
in **September 476** — the conventional end of the Roman empire in western Europe, when Odoacer "sent an
embassy to Constantinople which did no more than state the obvious: there was no longer any need for an
emperor in the west." Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 1 (**Peter Heather**). One of the
wiki's standing **transition pages**.

## Narrative: the road to 476

Heather frames the fall as the working-out of a structural crisis after the death of **Attila** (453) and
the **collapse of Hunnic power**. The western general **Aetius** had used Hunnic troops to *contain* the
barbarian groups settled inside the empire (the Visigoths, Burgundians, Vandals); after Attila's empire
dissolved and Aetius and Valentinian III were murdered (454–455), that containment strategy ended. The new
order had to **include** the barbarian leaders in imperial politics — each demanding rewards (land, tax
revenues, titles) from an **already shrinking fiscal base** (Africa lost to the **Vandals** since 439,
Britain and parts of Gaul and Spain gone). The result was a **"vicious circle"**: too many players
squabbling over a bankrupting empire, every regime change requiring fresh gifts that shrank the base
further.

A succession of short-lived western emperors — most of them frontmen for the generalissimo **Ricimer**
(Avitus, **Majorian**, Libius Severus, **Anthemius**) — tried and failed to break the cycle. The one viable
escape was to **reconquer Vandal Africa** and restore its revenues; both Majorian's (460) and Anthemius'
great expeditions (468, with a vast eastern fleet) were **destroyed by the Vandals**, and with them died
the last hope of revival. Thereafter "one group after another came to the realization that the western
empire was no longer a prize worth fighting for": **Euric the Visigoth** seized Gaul and Spain "on his own
authority," the eastern empire made peace with the Vandals (c. 474), the Burgundians turned homeward.
Finally the **army of Italy** under Orestes raised his son **Romulus** (476); when it mutinied over money,
the officer **Odoacer** deposed Romulus and **declined to appoint a successor**, returning the western
imperial insignia to the emperor **Zeno** at Constantinople.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **collapse of Hunnic power** after Attila, which ended Aetius' containment strategy and
  forced the barbarians *into* the body politic.
- `caused_by:` the **loss of Africa to the [[vandal-kingdom|Vandals]]** (439) and the failure of the
  reconquest expeditions — depriving the West of its richest revenues.
- `contributed_to:` Heather's **"vicious circle"** — too many armed players over a shrinking fiscal base,
  so that "the centre no longer controlled anything anyone wanted" and the imperial idea "became
  meaningless."

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **end of the western imperial line** and the open emergence of the
  [[germanic-peoples|barbarian successor kingdoms]] (Odoacer's, then Ostrogothic Italy under
  [[theoderic-the-great|Theoderic]]; Visigothic Spain; Frankish Gaul; Vandal Africa).
- `enabled:` the survival and later resurgence of the empire **in the East** — whence
  [[justinian|Justinian]] would briefly "reconquer" Africa and Italy in the sixth century.
- The event was, Heather stresses, less a single catastrophe than the **last stage of a long dissolution**
  — Roman landowning élites and barbarian leaders alike gradually transferring their loyalties to regional
  powers.

## Historiography

Heather rejects the "single dramatic fall," presenting 476 as the **anticlimactic endpoint** of a process
already complete in substance — a reading central to the modern late-antiquity school (and to CAH XIV's
preface, which notes that a western political terminus could as well be placed in the 470s or c. 540).
476's symbolic weight derives from Odoacer's *not* replacing the emperor; the deeper causes feed the
standing [[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|controversy on the fall of the West]].

The longer historiographical arc runs from **Gibbon**, who made 476 the archetype of "decline and
fall," through the **"barbarian invasions"** school (external conquest) and **Pirenne**, who argued
that Roman Mediterranean unity actually survived until the Arab conquests broke it (see
[[pirenne-thesis|the Pirenne thesis]]), to the modern **Late Antiquity / "transformation"** school
(Peter Brown, Walter Goffart) that recasts the fifth century as a gradual accommodation in which the
"barbarians" were absorbed rather than victorious. On this longer view the process is better dated
from **376** (the Gothic crossing of the Danube and [[battle-of-adrianople-378|Adrianople, 378]]) than
from 476 — the choice of 476 itself is a historiographical artefact, since contemporaries did not
regard it as the end of Rome.

[[sources/heather-fall-roman-empire-2005|Heather's 2005 monograph]] sharpens the externalist
pole of this arc: the West was a structurally sound state destroyed by the [[huns|Hunnic]] shock
and the **fiscal spiral** of provincial loss (above all [[vandal-kingdom|Vandal Africa]], 439),
not by internal decline — his closing line reversing Gibbon, that "Roman imperialism was
ultimately responsible for its own destruction." See the
[[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire|fall controversy]] for the opposing positions.
