# Transatlantic Flight (1927)

## Narrative
On May 20, 1927, Charles Lindbergh took off from Roosevelt Field, Long Island, in the *Spirit of St. Louis*, a custom-built single-engine monoplane (Ryan NYP). After 33.5 hours of solo flight, during which he battled extreme sleep deprivation, icing conditions, and dense fog, he landed at Le Bourget Field near Paris on May 21, greeted by an ecstatic crowd of 150,000 people.

## Causal Analysis
- **The Orteig Prize:** The $25,000 prize offered by Raymond Orteig in 1919 acted as a major financial driver, prompting multiple teams to attempt the flight.
- **Funding:** Lindbergh secured the backing of St. Louis businessmen, establishing a fund of $15,000 to construct the aircraft.
- **Engineering Innovations:** Donald Hall and the Ryan Aeronautical team designed the Ryan NYP with a blind, aerodynamically clean cockpit to maximize fuel capacity. The flight relied on the new Wright Whirlwind J-5C radial engine, renowned for its mechanical reliability.

## Consequence Analysis
- **The "Lindbergh Boom":** The flight catalyzed public interest in aviation, leading to a massive increase in commercial passenger bookings, airport construction, and aviation stocks.
- **Global Celebrity:** Lindbergh became an international icon, using his influence to promote global commercial routes and secure funding for Robert Goddard's rocketry research.
- **Transregional Integration:** The flight bridged the Atlantic, accelerating the development of international air travel and passenger transport.

## Historiography
The flight is unusually well documented through Lindbergh's own detailed account (*We*, 1927, and the later Pulitzer Prize-winning *The Spirit of St. Louis*, 1953), contemporary press coverage of the record attempt, and Ryan Aeronautical's engineering records. Because Lindbergh's own retrospective writing is the dominant primary source, historians have noted the need to read his self-presentation critically, particularly regarding his later political reputation; the flight itself, however, is not a contested episode factually. Aviation historians debate primarily the flight's causal weight in the "Lindbergh Boom" versus broader technological and economic trends already pushing commercial aviation forward in the late 1920s.
