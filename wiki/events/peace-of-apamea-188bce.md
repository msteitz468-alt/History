# The Peace of Apamea (188 BCE)

The settlement that ended the [[roman-syrian-war-192-188bce|Roman–Syrian War]] and
redrew the political map of Asia Minor. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]]
Ch. 8 (Errington).

## Terms

[[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]] was required to:
- **evacuate all Asia Minor north and west of the Taurus** (the "Taurus line,"
  defined by Cape Sarpedon at sea and the River Tanais/Calycadnus inland);
- pay a **15,000-talent** indemnity (500 immediately, 2,500 on ratification, the
  rest over twelve years) — plus 400 talents and grain owed to Eumenes;
- surrender his war fleet (keeping ten small ships) and elephants, and not sail west
  of Cape Sarpedon;
- hand over enemies of Rome (**Hannibal**, Thoas, others — Hannibal escaped) and
  twenty hostages, including his son Antiochus.

## The reordering of Asia Minor

Rome took **no territory**. The lands Antiochus vacated were divided:
- **[[pergamon|Eumenes II of Pergamon]]** received the lion's share — most of western
  Asia Minor, the Chersonese, and (with exceptions) the Greek cities north of the
  Maeander that had not joined Rome in time;
- **[[rhodes]]** received **Lycia and Caria south of the Maeander**;
- Greek cities that had **joined Rome before Magnesia** were declared **free**.

## Significance

Apamea did **not** destroy the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]] — they kept an enormous
Asiatic empire from the Taurus to Iran — but it **confined them to Asia**, barring
them permanently from the west. It applied to Asia Minor the principle already used
in the Balkans (196): strategically important coasts placed in the hands of
**Roman friends**, neutralised from rival influence without a Roman presence. Eumenes,
Rhodes, and the free cities "owed the advantages of their status to Rome alone — and
they knew it." [[flamininus|Flamininus' doctrine]] of gratitude-as-policy was thus
extended eastward; it would not long survive the
[[third-macedonian-war-171-168bce|defeat of Perseus]], when Rome turned on both
Rhodes and Eumenes.

## Historiography

The treaty's terms are known chiefly through **Polybius** (writing within living memory
of the events, and drawing on documentary access as the son of an Achaean statesman
active in this period) and **Livy** (Book 38, largely dependent on Polybius but
supplemented by the Roman annalistic tradition). The close agreement between the two on
the treaty's specific clauses suggests both ultimately draw on the actual diplomatic
record rather than independent, divergent traditions, giving the terms of Apamea
unusual documentary solidity compared to many Hellenistic-era settlements. Polybius's
generally pro-Achaean, pro-Roman perspective shapes his framing of the settlement as a
just reordering rather than naked opportunism, a reading modern historians have
qualified by pointing to how quickly Rome abandoned its client Eumenes and Rhodes after
Pydna (168) — suggesting the "gratitude" framework Polybius and Flamininus articulated
was always contingent on Roman interest rather than principle.
