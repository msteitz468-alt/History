# Four-Year Plan (1936)

## Narrative

By spring 1936 the German economy faced an acute crisis: raw-material supplies for armaments sufficient for only two months; fuel for the armed forces critically short. Economics Minister Schacht pressed for brakes on rearmament. The military demanded acceleration. Hitler remained inactive for months.

Göring emerged as the key figure. Appointed Plenipotentiary for the Securing of the Raw Materials and Foreign Exchange Demands of the Reich (April 1936), he assembled experts (Löb, Krauch of IG Farben). By midsummer a programme for synthetic fuels, rubber, and industrial fats, plus maximum autarky, was ready.

In late August 1936, while at Bayreuth, Hitler dictated a lengthy memorandum on the economy (one of the rare occasions he put views in writing). It fell into two parts: 'the political situation' (pure Hitler: social-Darwinist, racially determinist; Bolshevism as the coming crisis; Germany as focus of showdown) and 'Germany's economic situation' (Göring-influenced raw-materials programme). Cost was irrelevant. Objections brushed aside: 'The nation does not live for the economy; rather, finance and the economy... must all exclusively serve this struggle for self-assertion.' Private industry had to fulfil national tasks or the state would do it itself. The ultimate solution was extension of 'living space'. A 'Several Years Plan' (soon called Four-Year Plan) would make the army operational and the economy ready for war within four years.

At the Nuremberg Party Rally (September 1936) Hitler announced the 'new Four-Year Programme'. The decree for implementation followed on 18 October. Göring was the driving force; he rapidly improvised a panoply of special commissioners with overlapping bureaucracies answerable only to him — 'a recipe for administrative and economic anarchy'.

Schacht recognized defeat; his influence waned. Goerdeler's critique was dismissed. The plan locked Germany into an armaments-tilted economy that could not be sustained indefinitely without expansion.

## Analysis (Kershaw)

The decision was ultimately ideological, not economic. Economic options were still open in principle but had narrowed. Hitler's broad, inflexible ideological premises made outright contest impossible without rejecting him personally. The memorandum accommodated Göring's and Blomberg's pragmatic expansionism within Hitler's racial vision. Both implied war at some point.

The momentum created was immense. Pressures on the economy fed directly into Hitler's ideological imperative. 'Working towards the Führer' operated here too: technocrats and IG Farben leaders pursued priorities that made territorial expansion necessary for both economic and ideological reasons. Aryanization offered 'easy pickings'.

By the end of 1937 the economic drive had created its own dynamic. Expansion became not just desirable but necessary.

## Verbatim (Kershaw range)

- 'The nation does not live for the economy; rather, finance and the economy, economic leaders and theories must all exclusively serve this struggle for self-assertion in which our people are engaged.'
- 'The German economy will either grasp the new economic tasks or else it will prove itself quite incompetent to survive in this modern age when a Soviet State is setting up a gigantic plan. But in that case it will not be Germany that will go under, but, at most, a few industrialists.'
- On ultimate solution: extension of 'living space' as 'the task of the political leadership one day to solve this problem'.
- 'In the next four years, the German army had to be made operational, the economy made ready for war.'

## Historiography (Kershaw)

Kershaw emphasizes that economics and ideology were thoroughly interwoven. The plan was programmatic rather than detailed. Hitler's way of arguing (inflexible premises + broad dogmatics) made it unassailable on its own terms. The result was not coherent planning but a powerful dynamic pushing toward expansion and, ultimately, war.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich in Power* (2005)

Balance sheet and quantities (ingested 2026-07-02): Göring read Hitler's
memorandum to cabinet 4 September 1936 ("The German armed forces must be
operational within four years"; "The German economy must be fit for war
within four years"; recalcitrant businessmen "punished by death"), was made
supreme by decree 18 October; [[schacht-hjalmar|Schacht]] was sent on leave
the day it reached cabinet. **Achievements:** aluminium +70% (world's largest
producer), textile self-sufficiency 5%→43% (1932–39), synthetic fuel +69%
(1937–39). **Failures:** buna at ~5,000 of a 29,000-tonne target; half of
mineral oil still imported in 1939; iron-ore imports *rose* from 4.5m to
~21m tonnes 1933–38; 1937 steel allocations gave the air force a third and
the army half of requirements. Rearmament reached 21% of national income by
1938 (deficit ~9.5bn RM); the Hermann Göring Works (July 1937) muscled state
steel past private industry (Thyssen's bitter prophecy that industrialists
would be shot "just as their equivalents were shot in Soviet Russia"). Evans
names the structural contradiction — autarky (long-war preparation) vs.
headlong rearmament (imminent war) — as the matrix of *Blitzkrieg*: the
economy could not sustain prolonged conflict, so conquest had to be fast and
sequential. On the "IG Farben Plan" thesis he is skeptical: Farben took no
more than a quarter of Plan expenditure and "neither was the Plan mainly
dependent on I.G. Farben, nor I.G. Farben on the Plan" — the "primacy of
politics" over business interest set the parameters.
