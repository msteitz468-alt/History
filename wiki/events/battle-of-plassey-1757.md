# The Battle of Plassey (1757)

**Plassey** (Palashi, 23 June 1757) was the decisive turning point by which the
**[[english-east-india-company|English East India Company]]** became a territorial power in India —
less a battle than a **conspiracy with a cannonade**, in Percival Spear's reading
([[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]], 1965). It opened "the financial
bleeding of Bengal" and laid the foundation of the British Raj.

## Narrative

When the young Nawab **Siraj-ud-daula** seized the Company's Calcutta (1756) — the episode that
produced the disputed "Black Hole" story — the Company sent **Robert Clive** with a force from Madras.
Rather than fight, Clive exploited Siraj's "mingled rashness and timidity" and the disaffection of the
Nawab's own nobles and the great Bengali financiers, conspiring to replace him with the elderly
general **Mir Jafar**. At **Plassey** the actual fighting "was slight"; Mir Jafar's defection decided
the day. Clive's qualities here, Spear notes, "were diplomatic rather than military." Mir Jafar was
installed as Nawab — and Clive, now "the kingmaker and plunderer," took a personal fortune
(£234,000 plus a £30,000-a-year land grant).

## Causal & consequence analysis

- `caused_by:` friction over the Company's **abused trade privileges** and Calcutta's fortification;
  Siraj's seizure of Calcutta; and Clive's **conspiracy** with Mir Jafar and the financiers.
- `produced:` a **sponsored state turned plundered state** — the Company held the military power, the
  Nawab the nominal throne; Company servants, exempted from duties, undercut Bengali merchants and
  "browbeat the Nawabi officers and intimidate the villagers" in Bengal's unwarlike countryside. In a
  few years Bengal was ruined and the Company near bankruptcy.
- The escalation ran through the displacement of Mir Jafar, **Mir Kasim's** resistance and defeat,
  and the battle of **Buxar (1764)** to the decisive step: the Mughal grant of the **[[english-east-india-company|diwani]]
  (revenue authority) of Bengal and Bihar to the Company (1765)** — Clive limiting commitments rather
  than marching on Delhi — which made the Company the **Mughal revenue agent**, and so the virtual
  ruler, of the richest province of India.

## Significance & historiography

Spear treats Plassey + the diwani as the moment "an adventurer's *tour de force* became a solid
political fact": the Company-state of Bengal, given coherent administrative shape by **Warren
Hastings** (the Board of Revenue, English district collectors — "the real beginning of British
administration in India"), became the **basis for the later all-India hegemony**. He is unsparing
about its character — a regime that "creamed off" a larger share of the national income than any
Mughal *subah* and returned less — locating the **drain of Bengal's wealth** at the very origin of
British rule, a theme later nationalist and economic historiography would develop much further.
