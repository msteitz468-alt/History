# Sennacherib's Siege of Jerusalem (701 BCE)

In **701 BCE [[sennacherib|Sennacherib]]** devastated [[kingdom-of-judah|Judah]]
and besieged **Jerusalem**, but the city was **not taken** — an outcome the Hebrew
Bible transfigures into the miraculous deliverance of Zion. It is one of the most
intensively debated episodes in ancient history, precisely because the **Assyrian
and biblical accounts diverge**. [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]]
(Ch. 23, Grayson; Ch. 30, Mitchell) treats it.

## Narrative

**Hezekiah of Judah**, drawn into the western arm of
**[[neo-babylonian-empire|Merodach-baladan's]]** anti-Assyrian revolt and
encouraged by Egypt and [[kush-meroe|Kush]], renounced Assyrian allegiance.
Sennacherib's **third campaign** took Sidon and Ashkelon, defeated an
**Egyptian–Kushite army at Eltekeh**, stormed the Judaean fortress towns (the
**siege of Lachish** is the subject of his famous palace reliefs), and shut
Hezekiah up, in his own words, **"like a bird in a cage"** in Jerusalem.

Here the sources part. **Sennacherib's annals** record no capture — only an
enormous **tribute** sent from Jerusalem to Nineveh. The **Bible** (II Kings
18–19; Isaiah 36–37) tells of the Assyrian *rab-šāqēh* haranguing the walls, the
prophet **Isaiah's** assurance of deliverance, the approach of **Taharqa**, and a
night when "the angel of the Lord" slew 185,000 in the Assyrian camp, after which
Sennacherib withdrew and was later murdered at Nineveh.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Hezekiah's revolt and his alliances with Egypt/Kush and
[[neo-babylonian-empire|Babylon]] — a coordinated challenge Sennacherib could not
ignore once he had dealt with Merodach-baladan.
`contributed_to:` by Hezekiah's deliberate **fortification of Jerusalem** and his
water-tunnel (the Siloam works), which prepared the city to withstand a siege.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **devastation of Judah** (the Shephelah towns, Lachish) and a
crushing tribute — Judah survived only as a reduced, loyal vassal.
`produced:` the **survival of Jerusalem and the Davidic line**, which the biblical
tradition reads as proof of **Zion's divine inviolability** — a theology that
shaped later Judaism and the messianic hope, and that made the eventual
[[fall-of-jerusalem-587bce|fall of Jerusalem in 587]] all the more traumatic.

## Historiography

The crux is the **one-campaign vs. two-campaign** problem. Grayson argues the
Assyrian and biblical narratives describe **essentially different events** and
posits a **second western campaign late in the reign (c. 688–681)** — to which the
Lachish reliefs, the Taharqa episode, and the deliverance-tradition belong — since
Taharqa was too young to command in 701. **Mitchell (Ch. 29)** by contrast holds to a
**single 701 campaign** (arguing Taharqa, c. 20, could have held titular command), with
the deliverance a theological gloss on the withdrawal — which he attributes to plague,
to a rumour of trouble in Babylonia, or to a textual crux ("185,000" perhaps for "185
picked men"). Others read the deliverance as a gloss on the tribute. Herodotus preserves
a rationalizing variant (mice gnawing the Assyrian bowstrings). The debate is a model
case of reconciling royal annals with a hostile foreign source. See [[hezekiah]] for
the Judaean preparations (the Siloam tunnel, the *lmlk* jars, the walls).

## Dever (2017): forty-six cities, a dozen destructions

[[sources/dever-beyond-the-texts-2017|Dever (2017)]] tests Sennacherib's annals against the
ground. The king claims to have taken "forty-six of his strong walled cities... 200,150
people... I counted as spoil," but Dever: "Sennacherib's claim... is greatly exaggerated.
There cannot have been any such number of fortified sites in the whole of Judah. Here we have
been able to cite no more than a dozen or so late eighth-century destructions" — concentrated
in the Shephelah ([[lachish|Lachish]] III, Beersheba II, Beth-Shemesh, Tell Beit Mirsim, Tel
'Eton, Tel 'Erani), while Jerusalem, Tell en-Nasbeh, Gibeon and others were spared. The
**[[lachish|fall of Lachish]]** — its 19,000-ton siege ramp, the Nineveh reliefs, and mass
graves of ~1,500 — is the campaign's dominant archaeological fact. Jerusalem survived through
Hezekiah's preparations (Siloam Tunnel, Broad Wall, lmlk provisioning) and tribute. Faust's
revisionist coda: the seventh century, "in the aftermath of the supposed disaster," was
actually Judah's demographic and economic peak.
