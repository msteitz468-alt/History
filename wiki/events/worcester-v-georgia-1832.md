# Worcester v. Georgia (1832)

## Narrative

U.S. Supreme Court decision (March 1832) holding that Georgia’s extension of state jurisdiction into the [[cherokee-nation]] was null and void under federal treaties and the structure of [[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america|Native–U.S. sovereignty]]. Unenforced. Michael D. Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8; also noted in Washburn & Trigger’s CHNPA introduction on Marshall’s Cherokee jurisprudence.

### Prelude: Corn Tassel and *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*

In 1830 Cherokee **Corn Tassel** killed another person in the Cherokee Nation; Georgia arrested, tried, and convicted him for murder and sentenced him to hang. Principal Chief John Ross retained counsel; **William Wirt** (former Monroe/Adams Attorney General, who had opined Indian nations were independent sovereigns) took appeals. December 1830 Chief Justice **John Marshall** ordered Georgia to appear; the legislature carried out Corn Tassel’s execution instead.

Wirt filed *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*: Cherokee Nation a sovereign foreign nation; Georgia no jurisdiction; treaties and Trade and Intercourse Acts recognized sovereignty; commerce clause sole federal channel. **March 1831** Marshall: Cherokee Nation not a foreign nation and cannot bring suit—case dismissed. Obiter dicta: Cherokee Nation a **“domestic dependent nation”** in a relationship with the United States resembling that between a **ward and a guardian**. Privately Marshall told Wirt he was sympathetic—Georgia had no jurisdictional rights if a proper case were brought.

### The Worcester test case

Georgia required non-Indians in the Cherokee Nation (except federal officials) to register and take an oath of allegiance. Missionaries **Samuel Worcester** and **Elizur Butler** (American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions) ignored the law for a test case; arrested, convicted, sentenced to **four years hard labor**.

### Holding (March 1832)

Marshall agreed Georgia’s law was unconstitutional. Analogy: large powerful state / small weak state—the small state may surrender specified sovereignty attributes for benefits but retains full sovereignty in unspecified areas. The Cherokee Nation had given up to the United States sovereignty in enumerated areas such as **foreign relations and trade with Americans** but retained sovereignty in all other areas; it received a U.S. protection pledge including protection from American citizens—treaty and Congressional guarantees superior to state law. Therefore Georgia’s legislation extending jurisdiction into the Cherokee Nation was **null and void**.

### Non-enforcement

Cherokees overjoyed then despondent: the Court had **no power to enforce** its rulings. Georgia rejected *Worcester* and continued using legal power to harass Cherokees from their land. Ross ran a publicity blitz; majority policy remained refuse to negotiate and rely on American sense of justice; hoped 1832 election would remove [[jackson-andrew]]. Failure of enforcement opened the path to the unauthorized Treaty of [[new-echota]] (1835) and [[trail-of-tears-1838]].

Washburn & Trigger note that Marshall’s earlier *Life of George Washington* (1804–07) already revealed the keen mind later summarized brilliantly in the Cherokee cases, most notably *Worcester v. Georgia*.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: Georgia’s registration/oath laws and conviction of missionaries Worcester and Butler
- contributed_to: by Cherokee legal strategy after Corn Tassel’s execution and *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* dismissal
- part_of: [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880]] and struggle over [[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]
- concurrent_with: Jackson Administration policy that states enjoy full sovereign powers over all land and people within their boundaries; federal non-enforcement of protective treaty provisions

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: landmark holding that state jurisdiction over the Cherokee Nation was void; enduring “domestic dependent nation” framing (from the prior case) plus residual tribal sovereignty doctrine
- enabled: temporary Cherokee legal and public-relations hope; then despondency when unenforced
- contributed_to: Treaty Party conclusion that only flight was safe → [[new-echota]] / [[trail-of-tears-1838]]
- contrasts_with: Jacksonian state-rights removal strategy that treated judicial victory as politically empty without executive force

## Actors

Samuel Worcester; Elizur Butler; Chief Justice John Marshall; William Wirt; John Ross and Cherokee Nation; State of Georgia; [[jackson-andrew]] administration (non-enforcement context).

## Historiography

Central case in federal Indian law and Cherokee removal scholarship. Debates: scope of “domestic dependent nation”; whether Marshall’s court was pro-Cherokee or merely federalist against states; Jackson’s alleged (often apocryphal) defiance phrase vs actual non-enforcement through state-rights theory. Green stresses the gap between legal victory and political power. Prucha, Wilkins, Norgren, and McLoughlin are standard further guides beyond Green’s synthesis.
