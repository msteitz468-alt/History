# Charter Oath in Five Articles (1868)

The founding programmatic statement of the Meiji government, sworn by Emperor Meiji before the gods of heaven and earth on April 7, 1868. Keene calls it "the young emperor's first act of major historical significance" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]). Its five articles combined language of democratic consultation with the goal of strengthening imperial rule.

## Narrative

### The Ceremony

The oath was sworn on April 7, 1868, the day before the scheduled imperial army assault on Edo Castle — a timing some scholars interpret as deliberate, designed to mobilize domestic support for the military campaign. The ceremony was entirely Shintō in form: salt water and rice were scattered for purification; the head of the Office of Shintō Worship sang god-songs for the descending deity; offerings were made; and the emperor, accompanied by Sanjō Sanetomi and Iwakura Tomomi, took his place on a throne facing south, the seat of the gods (*shinza*) diagonally to his right.

Sanjō read aloud a Shintō prayer invoking the gods of heaven and earth. The emperor then advanced to the altar, bowed in prayer, and made an offering. Sanjō then read aloud the oath in the emperor's name. Present were nobles, daimyos, and senior officials in full court robes. After the reading, 767 persons signed a document pledging their lives to the oath's implementation.

### The Text of the Five Articles

> 1. Deliberate assemblies shall be widely established and all matters decided by public discussion.
> 2. All classes, high and low, shall unite in vigorously carrying out the administration of affairs of state.
> 3. The common people, no less than the civil and military officials, shall each be allowed to pursue his own calling so that there may be no discontent.
> 4. Evil customs of the past shall be broken off and everything based on the just laws of nature.
> 5. Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundations of imperial rule.

The text was not composed by the emperor. The original draft was written by two samurai-scholars, **Yuri Kimimasa** (1829–1909) and **Fukuoka Takachika** (1835–1919), and revised by **Kido Takayoshi** ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]). How much the fifteen-year-old emperor knew of its contents before Sanjō read it aloud is uncertain.

### The Accompanying Imperial Letter

On the same day, a letter in the emperor's name was made public — a document Keene notes is "so unlike anything Kōmei or any earlier emperor would have written." The letter blamed the military class for creating an "aura of awe" around the throne that isolated the emperor from his people and promised that the emperor would personally govern, leave the palace, and consult his subjects. It concluded: "Cast away private thoughts and choose the general good."

### The Next Day: The Five Prohibitory Signboards

On April 8, signboards replacing the shogunate's public edicts were erected throughout the country. The first three injunctions (against murder, insubordination, Christianity) were traditional. The fourth explicitly ended the *jōi* (expel the barbarians) component of the sonnō jōi slogan: "the court, for good reasons, has opened relations with foreign countries and concluded treaties with them in accordance with international law. Accordingly, foreigners must not be harmed." Keene notes these signboards "were much better known to the mass of the population than the Charter Oath pronounced before nobles and daimyos."

## Causal Analysis

The oath's immediate cause was political: the new government needed to announce its principles, consolidate elite support before the Edo campaign, and address simultaneously both conservative domain lords and those who had hoped for liberal reform. Its intellectual origins lay in multiple streams — Sakamoto Ryōma's *Senchū hassaku* (Eight Proposals Aboard Ship), the Satsuma-Tosa pact of 1867, and Western constitutional ideas filtered through samurai-scholars like Kido and Yuri.

The Shintō ceremonial form was not incidental but ideologically significant: the oath was framed as a commitment made before the gods, in the context of the reestablishment of the Jingikan and the separation of [[shinto|Shintō]] from Buddhism — giving the whole event a dual character as both modernizing statement and religious restoration.

## Consequence Analysis

The Charter Oath's long-term significance was contested from the start. Its critics within the Meiji government saw it as a tactical statement, not a genuine commitment to representative government. Its defenders cited it throughout the Meiji era as the founding charter of constitutional progress. "In later times the principles enunciated in the five articles were qualified, watered down, and sometimes ignored, but they were never repudiated, and they survived as ideals for those who hoped to make Japan into an enlightened modern state" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

The fifth article — "Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world" — provided the ideological license for the systematic Westernization of the 1870s: the missions abroad, the hiring of foreign experts (*oyatoi gaikokujin*), the adoption of Western legal, military, and educational systems.

The oath is linked causally to:
- The reorganization (announced in Chapter 17) establishing executive, legislative, and judicial branches "conceived in the expressed hope of building a modern state that would not be inferior to the advanced countries of the West"
- The later debates over the [[meiji-constitution-1889]] (1889)
- The [[meiji-modernization]] process as a whole

## Actors

- **[[emperor-meiji]]**: Performed the oath before the gods; almost certainly had little role in its composition. Its moderate, forward-looking character may have appealed to him precisely because of his youth.
- **Sanjō Sanetomi**: Read the oath aloud and the Shintō prayer. A central figure in the early Meiji government.
- **[[iwakura-tomomi]]**: Was present as assistant president; his political project required a legitimating statement.
- **Kido Takayoshi**: The most important intellectual contributor; his revision shaped the oath's final language.

## Historiography

The interpretation of the Charter Oath is a standing debate in Meiji studies. The "skeptical" position holds that it was a tactical document — its seemingly liberal provisions masked the real goal of winning support before the Edo offensive. The "progressive" position holds that the language was genuinely unprecedented and reflected real aspirations toward constitutional governance.

Keene's analysis in [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]] takes a nuanced middle position: "It would be a mistake to suppose that the five articles of the oath signified an intention by the leaders of the government to create a parliamentary democracy in the near future. But whether or not the oath was genuinely progressive, the language was unprecedented in Japan or, indeed, any other country in the orbit of Chinese civilization." He notes that "deciding 'all matters by public discussion' was definitely not traditional" and that the characterization of past customs as "evil" — rather than praiseworthy — was a genuine departure from political convention.

The oath's connection to the same-day establishment of the Jingikan — the Shintō ministry that promoted state Shintō and the separation of Buddhism — reveals the contradictions built into the Restoration from the start: modernizing language paired with nativist religious policy.
