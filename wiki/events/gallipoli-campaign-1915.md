# Gallipoli Campaign (1915)

## Narrative

By early 1915 the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps (ANZAC) had been incorporated under British General Sir William Birdwood as a major component of Sir Ian Hamilton's Mediterranean Expeditionary Force. The plan was to land on the Gallipoli peninsula in European Turkey, force open the Dardanelles to Allied battleships, and intimidate the Ottoman government in Constantinople into capitulation. "Though bold and probably feasible, the plan foundered."

The naval attack on 18 March 1915 failed by "simple mischance." The land invasion on 25 April 1915 failed through "poor planning and inept execution." The invasion force landed at two widely separated places; "an unexpectedly energetic Ottoman reaction defeated indecisive British and Australian commanders." The landings around Anzac Cove cost approximately 5,000 Australian casualties. Australian, New Zealand, British, and Indian troops "remained trapped in two footholds on the Gallipoli peninsula."

In August a major offensive — a complex plan involving attack from the Anzac area over the Chunuk Bair range — resulted in major loss of life with medical services again inadequate. The offensive failed, prolonging the stalemate.

In September 1915 press leaks precipitated a political crisis. Journalist Ellis Ashmead-Bartlett denounced command failure; the "Gallipoli letter" from Australian journalist Keith Murdoch reached British Prime Minister Herbert Asquith. General Hamilton was replaced by Sir Charles Monro, who planned a withdrawal. "Anzac Cove was evacuated on 19–20 December 1915, the British Helles front on 8 January 1916." The evacuation — the one element of the campaign to succeed — was propagandistically represented as "a triumph of Anzac ingenuity rather than efficient military staff work."

The "combination of 'nationhood, brotherhood and sacrifice', as Bill Gammage wrote, proved to be a powerful and enduring mix in creating an enduring Anzac legend."

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: Ottoman Turkey's entry into the war (29 October 1914) which made imperial troops needed to protect the Suez Canal
- caused_by: British strategic preference for an eastern alternative to Western Front stalemate
- contributed_to: Poor planning by Hamilton's headquarters for the land invasion
- contributed_to: Unexpectedly strong Ottoman defensive response

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: [[anzac-legend]] — the cultural mythology of Australian national character forged in war, drawn initially from Charles Bean's journalism representing the landings as "a triumph of colonial manhood"
- produced: Shift in Australian self-understanding — the slogan "Here Australia Became a Nation" appeared on a photograph of Gallipoli in 1915
- contributed_to: Further burst of volunteering in Australia following initial positive reports
- contributed_to: Erosion of Australian confidence in British high command — a pattern continued at Fromelles and Bullecourt
- enabled: Monash's later reputation, though the source notes his performance on Gallipoli involved "a shaky start"

## Actors

- **Sir William Birdwood**: British general commanding ANZAC Corps; planned the August offensive
- **Sir Ian Hamilton**: Commander, Mediterranean Expeditionary Force; replaced after press revelations of command failure
- **Sir Charles Monro**: Hamilton's replacement; planned the successful withdrawal
- **Ellis Ashmead-Bartlett**: British journalist; initial reports represented the landings as triumph of colonial manhood; later denounced command failure
- **Charles Bean**: Australian official correspondent; his words "endured as the basis of the influential 'Anzac legend'"
- **Keith Murdoch**: Australian journalist; "Gallipoli letter" to Asquith contributed to Hamilton's removal

## Historiography

The campaign's historiography divides sharply between nationalist commemoration and military-critical assessment. Initial propaganda — especially Ashmead-Bartlett's reports — "represented the landings as a triumph of colonial manhood." The source presents the campaign as a military failure redeemed narratively: the evacuation was the sole success, yet was mythologised as Anzac ingenuity (the "drip rifle" story, which "was in fact irrelevant to the evacuation's success"). C.E.W. Bean's official history and his journalistic work during the campaign form the textual foundation of the Anzac legend. Revisionist scholarship (Bill Gammage, Les Carlyon, Peter Stanley) has complicated the picture: L.L. Robson showed the Anzacs "far from being noble bushmen, were more likely to come from the city and to have been born in Britain"; Stanley explored mutiny and self-harm. The source (Garton and Stanley) treats the gap between mythologised and actual experience as central to understanding post-war Australian society. See [[anzac-legend]] for the commemorative tradition.
