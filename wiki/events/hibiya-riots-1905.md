## Narrative

The Hibiya Riots (September 5–7, 1905) were popular disturbances in Tokyo triggered by public fury at the terms of the [[treaty-of-portsmouth-1905|Treaty of Portsmouth]], which ended the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] without obtaining an indemnity from Russia or the full cession of Sakhalin. Approximately 30,000 protesters assembled at Hibiya Park on September 5 for a "people's mass meeting" to discuss rejection of the treaty and impeachment of cabinet ministers.

### The Outbreak

The police refused to allow demonstrators to enter Hibiya Park. The crowds broke through police barricades; outnumbered officers could not control them. Troops were called out to protect the palace, government ministries, and foreign legations. "The noise of the clashes in the park could be heard in the palace," Keene records, "and the emperor, unable to sit tranquilly in his chair, paced back and forth listening to the tumult. Suddenly there was a sound of rifle fire; the military police were firing pistols to intimidate the protesters. The emperor, normally so impassive, was extremely agitated by the noise outside. Soon afterward, Prime Minister Katsura rushed to the palace to report on the situation, and that night the emperor repeatedly sent chamberlains to discover the latest developments" (Keene, ch. 55).

The demonstrations continued for two more days. Protesters set fires, burned ten or more streetcars, and stormed from place to place burning police boxes. Martial law was declared for Tokyo and vicinity; it was not lifted until **November 29, 1905**. Heavy rains on the third day dispersed the crowds. Similar protests broke out at smaller scale in other Japanese cities.

### Public Expectations vs. Reality

The riots reflected a catastrophic mismatch between public expectations and diplomatic reality. Throughout the war, Japanese newspapers had encouraged the public to expect massive Russian indemnities comparable to what Germany had extracted from France in 1871. The government had never publicly acknowledged Japan's financial exhaustion or the limits it imposed on peace terms. President Roosevelt had warned Foreign Minister Komura that an indemnity was unrealistic without Japanese troops surrounding Moscow; the government knew this but did not say so. When the public learned that Japan had won the war but received no indemnity and only half of Sakhalin, the sense of betrayal was intense.

Roosevelt privately thought "that the Japanese government was to blame for having allowed the public to expect Russia would pay a large indemnity" (Keene, ch. 55). Komura and his colleagues, who had signed the treaty "quite against their own wishes" on orders from Tokyo, "could easily imagine the stormy reception they would receive after their return to Japan."

### Interpretations Abroad

Foreign observers in Tokyo quickly denied that the riots represented xenophobia or anti-Christian sentiment. Roosevelt remained sympathetic to Japan, writing to Minister Takahira: "You have crowned a great war by a great peace" (Keene, ch. 55).

## Causal Analysis

The riots were the product of a government that had mobilized public patriotism for war without preparing the public for the financial and strategic constraints that would shape peace. The popular expectation of a punitive peace — an indemnity, Sakhalin, Manchuria secured — was not irrational given Japan's military victories, but it was not grounded in realistic assessment of Japan's capacity to continue fighting. The riots thus represent the first serious rupture between the Meiji state's wartime narrative and popular political expectations.

## Consequence Analysis

Martial law suppressed the immediate disorder, but the riots fed into a broader post-war disillusionment. Keene documents "anxious pessimism" among young men and women after the war, and intellectual alienation that manifested as naturalist literature, socialism, and what one commentator called "the morose era." The riots foreshadowed later tensions between popular expectations and elite decision-making that would recur in Japanese politics through the 1920s–30s.

## Historiography

Keene's account emphasizes Emperor Meiji's personal agitation at the sounds of riot and gunfire from inside the palace — a rare moment of visible emotional disturbance. The riots are presented as a consequence of the government's failure of political communication rather than as evidence of Japanese militarism per se. Roosevelt's assessment that the government had mismanaged public expectations is treated as essentially correct.
