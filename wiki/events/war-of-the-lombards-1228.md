# War of the Lombards (1228–1233)

The **War of the Lombards** was the civil war in Cyprus (with a Syrian extension) between
the **[[ibelin-family|Ibelin]]** party — guardians of the young Henry I — and the imperial
officers of **[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick II]]**, whose Italian ("Lombard") troops
and baillis tried to supplant Ibelin regency. It is the central political crisis of
thirteenth-century Lusignan Cyprus and left the Ibelins supreme in the island nobility
until the later fourteenth century.

## Narrative

### Catalyst (July–September 1228)

Frederick's 1228–29 eastern intervention transformed existing Cypriot rivalries into
full-scale civil war. Structural conflict: western custom often gave the suzerain wardship
of a minor's lands; Latin East custom gave wardship to the next of kin. Frederick claimed
regency, homage, and minority revenues. He was enraged by the 1225 coronation of Henry
without prior notice and by unpaid minority profits; Cypriot opponents (Gauvain of Cheneche,
William of Rivet, Aimery Barlais) cultivated him.

At a Limassol banquet (soon after Frederick's arrival 21 July 1228) he surrounded John of
Beirut with men-at-arms and demanded surrender of Beirut and the minority profits. John
stood on High Court process. Frederick took hostages (two of John's sons) and twenty
pledges. John provisioned St Hilarion; mid-August Frederick occupied Nicosia. Early
September settlement: practically John had lost — sons released, but Frederick took Cypriot
fealty as suzerain, kept royal profits, and outmanoeuvred John on castle appointments.
Frederick "had ended Ibelin rule in Cyprus, at least for the time being."

### Five baillis and the first round (1229–30)

Frederick farmed the regency for three years to a consortium of **five baillis** — Aimery
Barlais, Amaury of Bethsan, Hugh of Jubail, William of Rivet, Gauvain of Cheneche — for
10,000 silver marks, with western mercenaries and (per Philip of Novara) an oath to
dispossess the Ibelins. Little over a month after Frederick left, the Ibelins landed at
Gastria, marched on Nicosia, and won the **Battle of Nicosia (14 July 1229)**. The baillis
held out in Kyrenia, St Hilarion, and Kantara; Kyrenia fell soon; the other two resisted
~ten months, capitulating April/May 1230. John offered generous terms — keeping fiefs in
return for oaths not to oppose him again — conscious he had overthrown the emperor's
officers and besieged a castle holding the king.

### Filangieri and the second round (1231–33)

In autumn 1231 imperial marshal **Richard Filangieri** arrived with a substantial army.
Blocked in Cyprus, he attacked John's lordship of Beirut. In Acre, Ibelin sympathizers
formed the **Commune of Acre** (within the Confraternity of St Andrew). John's early-1232
expedition to raise the Beirut siege met setbacks (Barlais desertion of ~80 knights, wrecks
at Botron, only 43 knights answering the Assise sur la ligece). Fortunes reversed when John
became mayor of the Commune, seized imperial ships, and enlisted the Genoese; Filangieri
raised the Beirut siege. A surprise reverse at **Casal Imbert** coincided with Henry I's
coming of age. War shifted to Cyprus: Barlais seized the island; Filangieri brought forces
from Tyre.

In May 1232 the Ibelins returned by sea (imperial ships seized at Acre + Genoese), seized
**Famagusta by night**, and on **15 June 1232** won the **Battle of Agridi** in the pass
toward St Hilarion. Kyrenia held until after Easter 1233 under full land-and-sea siege
(Genoese alliance, Dec 1232). Philip Chenard surrendered on terms. Ibelin victory was
complete.

### Aftermath

Henry convened the High Court: those who had taken arms against him since majority were
deprived of fiefs (list headed by Barlais, Bethsan, Jubail, Chenard). Frederick lost his
main pretext once Henry was of age; 1242 Ibelins expelled the imperial garrison from Tyre;
**1247 Innocent IV absolved Henry of any oaths to Frederick** — "No more is heard of Cyprus
as a client-kingdom of the Western Empire." Genoese commercial privileges (June and Dec
1232) became the lasting basis of Genoese rights in Cyprus — a long-term cost of victory.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Frederick's eastern suzerainty claims meeting Ibelin minority government
- `contributed_to:` pre-existing anti-Ibelin faction of second-generation Lusignan vassals
  who saw former Lusignan foes monopolize patronage; Alice of Champagne's loss of control
- `enabled:` Genoese naval support and the Commune of Acre's resistance to Filangieri

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Ibelin ascendancy (constableship 1240s–1290s; seneschalcy to the 1360s)
- `produced:` end of Hohenstaufen overlordship of Cyprus
- `contributed_to:` Genoese foothold that would culminate in
  [[genoese-invasion-of-cyprus-1373|1373–74]]
- Legal change: after the war, children already born to men sentenced to dispossession
  could no longer succeed to fiefs (Philip of Novara)

## Historiography

Philip of Novara's *Gestes des Chiprois* is the principal narrative — Ibelin vassal,
panegyric of John and Balian, romance style. Edbury insists it be treated with
circumspection; there is no anti-Ibelin chronicle. He balances Novara against the pattern
of Ibelin legalism cloaking high-handed violence (refusal of Alice's nominee, lynching of
Baldwin of Belleme, armed defence of St Hilarion, war against the suzerain's officers).
Force figures in the sources are uncertain and rhetorical.
