# The Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901)

The **Boxer Rebellion** (or Boxer Uprising) was an anti-foreign, anti-Christian peasant movement in
north China that the Qing court briefly endorsed, leading to a foreign military invasion, the sack
of Beijing, and a punitive settlement that fatally weakened the dynasty. From the
[[cambridge-history-of-china|Cambridge History of China]] (Late Qing volume).

## Narrative

The **Yihequan** ("Righteous and Harmonious Fists," dubbed "Boxers" for their martial-arts rituals)
arose in drought-stricken **Shandong** amid deep popular resentment of foreign missionaries,
Chinese Christian converts, railways, and the broader humiliation of [[opium-wars|imperialist]]
encroachment. Believing themselves invulnerable to bullets, Boxers attacked Christians and
foreigners and, in 1900, poured into **Beijing**, besieging the **foreign legations**. The
**Empress Dowager Cixi** threw the court's support behind them and declared war on the powers. An
**Eight-Nation Alliance** (Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, the US, Italy, Austria-Hungary)
sent an expeditionary force that relieved the legations, **captured and looted Beijing**, and sent
the court fleeing to Xi'an. (Southern governors, in the "Mutual Protection of Southeast China,"
declared the war illegitimate and stayed neutral — a sign of Qing disintegration.)

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` intense **anti-foreign and anti-Christian** feeling driven by imperialist pressure,
  missionary activity, and economic dislocation, sharpened by **natural disaster** in north China.
- `caused_by:` the **weakness and miscalculation of the Qing court**, which after the failure of
  [[self-strengthening-movement|self-strengthening]] and the 1898 reforms chose to harness rather
  than suppress the movement.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` foreign military **invasion, the sack of Beijing**, and widespread reprisals.
- `produced:` the **Boxer Protocol (1901)** — a colossal indemnity, the execution or punishment of
  officials, the stationing of foreign troops, and the razing of forts — a national humiliation.
- `produced:` the final **discrediting of the Qing**: the dynasty's belated "New Policies" reforms
  could not restore its legitimacy, and the road ran on to the **[[fall-of-the-qing-1911|1911
  Revolution]]**.

## Actors

The **Boxers** and the **Qing court** (Cixi) against the **Eight-Nation Alliance**, with Chinese
Christians and foreign missionaries as the principal victims. See [[qing-dynasty]].

## Historiography

The CHC and modern scholarship read the Boxers not as mere "xenophobic fanatics" (the contemporary
Western image) but as a popular movement rooted in **socioeconomic crisis and anti-imperial
grievance** — though the court's opportunistic embrace of it was a catastrophic blunder. It is a
key episode in the narrative of Qing collapse and of Chinese nationalism, and remains contested in
Chinese memory (patriotic resistance vs. destructive obscurantism).
