# Second Seminole War (1835–1842)

## Narrative

Longest and costliest of the U.S.–Seminole wars, fought to enforce Removal from Florida into [[indian-territory]] / western [[creek-muscogee|Creek]] country. Michael D. Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8.

### Seminole origins and First Seminole War

[[seminole|Seminoles]]: Lower Creek migrants to Spanish Florida from the early 18th century; augmented by escaped South Carolina/Georgia slaves (intermarried, enslaved, or autonomous allied communities) and defeated Creek civil-war Nativist refugees after 1814. Post-1812 Georgia planter raids for runaways; **1818** [[jackson-andrew]] led Army + Creek warriors into Florida (**First Seminole War**); **1819** Spain sold Florida to the U.S. **1823** treaty relocated Seminoles to a peninsula reservation—unproductive, could not support their numbers; settlers feared African-descent people among Seminoles.

### Contested removal treaty

**9 May 1832** removal treaty with western-tour proviso; March **Fort Gibson** document by seven land scouts interpreted by the U.S. as final despite Seminole belief the nation would decide after hearing reports. Treaty required move onto the western Creek Nation—alarmed civil-war refugees (fear of retribution) and black Seminoles (fear of enslavement by Creek slaveholders). Removal to begin **January 1836**.

### War

A month before the deadline, warriors **killed agent Wiley Thompson**, pro-removal headman **Charley Emathla**, and wiped out a U.S. troop column → **Second Seminole War**. Declared over in **1842**: cost the United States **1,500 lives** and **over twenty million dollars**. No one knows the number of dead Seminoles, but the war cost most of them their Florida homes. Captured/surrendered people loaded onto boats to New Orleans then rivers to Fort Gibson / Creek Nation. By the end, some **4,000** Seminoles and their allies of African descent had been relocated; about **500** remained in the swamps of southern Florida.

### Later phases

Mid-1850s **Third Seminole War** with surveyors → **200** more west; rest persisted in Florida. **1856** Seminoles purchased a tract from the Creeks and re-created a Nation (pattern parallel to Chickasaw–Choctaw separation).

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: disputed 1832 removal treaty / Fort Gibson “finalization” and order to settle among Creeks
- contributed_to: by [[indian-removal-act-1830]] regime and settler fear of African-descent Seminole communities
- contributed_to: by unproductive 1823 Florida reservation and First Seminole War aftermath
- part_of: [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880]]
- concurrent_with: Creek Alabama terror and 1836 forced march; Cherokee New Echota crisis

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: U.S. losses of 1,500 dead and >$20 million; unknown Seminole mortality; destruction of most Florida homes
- produced: forced relocation of ~4,000; persistent Florida remnant (~500 after 1842; further resistance mid-1850s)
- enabled: 1856 separate Seminole Nation tract purchased from Creeks in [[indian-territory]]
- contributed_to: Jacksonian claim of possible Seminole–Creek common cause used to justify Creek Army roundup in 1836

## Actors

Seminole warriors and communities (including African-descent allies); U.S. Army; agent Wiley Thompson (killed); Charley Emathla (pro-removal headman, killed); [[jackson-andrew]] / successive administrations; Creek Nation as unwanted relocation host; later surveyors of Third Seminole War.

## Historiography

Green flags Mahon on the Second Seminole War as a standard guide. Atrocity and cost figures (U.S. dead, dollars, unknown Seminole dead, forced boat removals) are load-bearing facts. Contested: legitimacy of Fort Gibson document; role of black Seminole freedom stakes in prolonging resistance. Do not reduce to a “Florida Indian problem” without perpetrators, victims, and numbers.
