# Trajan's Dacian Wars (101–106 CE)

The two campaigns by which **[[trajan|Trajan]]** destroyed the powerful Danubian kingdom of
**Dacia** under King **Decebalus** and annexed it as a Roman province — the empire's most
spectacular conquest since Augustus, and the last great permanent addition to its territory.
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 2.

## Narrative

Dacia (roughly modern Romania), unified and militarized under **Decebalus**, had inflicted
defeats on Roman forces and exacted an unsatisfactory peace from **[[domitian|Domitian]]** (subsidies
in return for nominal client status). Trajan, seeking both **security on the Danube** and the
glory of conquest, crossed the river (the famous bridge at Drobeta) in the **First Dacian War
(101–102)**, forcing Decebalus to terms. When Decebalus broke the settlement, the **Second Dacian
War (105–106)** ended in the storming of the capital **Sarmizegetusa**, the suicide of Decebalus,
and the **annexation of Dacia** as a province. The campaigns are commemorated in narrative relief
on **Trajan's Column** in Rome.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **threat and prestige** of the Dacian kingdom and the unresolved settlement
  Domitian had been forced to accept.
- `contributed_to:` **Trajan's drive for conquest** — part of the expansionism that took the
  empire to its [[trajans-parthian-war-114-117|greatest extent]].
- `produced:` the **province of Dacia** — a Latin-speaking enclave north of the Danube, heavily
  colonized (the *Colonia Ulpia Traiana*); and an enormous influx of **Dacian gold and silver**
  that funded Trajan's building programme (the Forum, the markets, the alimenta) and a great
  congiarium and games.
- `produced:` **Trajan's Column** — the prime monument and pictorial "commentary" of the wars,
  and a key source.

## Significance

The conquest of Dacia was the **high-water mark of Roman territorial expansion** in Europe and
the last province permanently added to the empire. It pushed Roman control **beyond the Danube**
frontier (a salient that would prove hard to hold and was abandoned in the third century), and
its wealth underwrote the prosperity and monuments of Trajan's reign. The thoroughness of the
Roman colonization left a lasting linguistic imprint (the Romance character of later Romanian).
