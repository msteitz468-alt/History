## Narrative

In the autumn of 634 (if [[edwin-of-northumbria|Eadwine]] was killed in 633) or 635 (if Eadwine was killed in 634), [[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]], son of [[æthelfrith-of-bernicia|Æthelfrith]], slew Cadwallon ap Cadfan, king of Gwynedd, "in the battle of 'Heavenfield' near Hexham (HE III, 1)" and "assumed the kingship of both the Deirans and the Bernicians." The battle's location — in the vicinity of Hexham, near Hadrian's Wall — shows that Cadwallon had advanced north against Oswald, "presumably intending to deal with the Bernician aethelings before they could consolidate their position further." This was "what lies behind Bede's statement that Cadwallon intended to destroy the whole nation (HE II, 20)."

According to Adomnán's Life of Columba, Oswald had a vision of St Columba on the eve of battle and "commended himself and his men to God." Despite Cadwallon's numerically superior forces — though "Bede or his sources may have exaggerated Cadwallon's numerical superiority for dramatic effect" — it was Cadwallon who proved vulnerable: "far from home and by now an isolated member of the alliance which had conspired to defeat Eadwine."

## Causal Analysis

Cadwallon's overreach is the key causal factor. After destroying Osric of Deira and Eanfrith of Bernicia, he "began to ravage more widely." The slaying of Eanfrith "may have been an important turning-point": until that moment the sons of Æthelfrith may have been in alliance with the British leader. When Cadwallon turned against the Bernician aethelings too, he created the conditions for a unified response.

Oswald's ability to mount a challenge was enabled by his years in exile in Scottish Dál Riata. "There is some evidence that Oswald, while an exile, may have fought in Ireland." The presence of Æthelfrith's sons near Bernicia at the critical moment probably reflects "military assistance for them from the Picts or the Scots or both."

## Consequence Analysis

Heavenfield ended the brief but devastating period of British military dominance in the north. Oswald's victory restored the Bernician-led overkingship of the northern Angles and did so on distinctly Bernician and Celtic-Christian terms: "as soon as he was established as king he sent a request to Columba's monastic foundation on Iona among the Dalriadic Scots for a bishop, receiving in due course Aidan (HE III, 3)." This brought the northern church within the familia of Iona, halting the Canterbury-oriented mission of Paulinus and setting up the ecclesiastical tensions resolved at the [[synod-of-whitby-664|Synod of Whitby]] in 664.

## Actors

- **[[oswald-of-northumbria|Oswald]]** — returned exile, Christian prince raised in Celtic lands; the victor.
- **Cadwallon ap Cadfan** — king of Gwynedd; killed in battle, ending his attempt at a pan-British reconquest of the north.

## Historiography

The battle's date depends entirely on whether Eadwine died in 633 or 634 (Kirby prefers 634, yielding Heavenfield in 635). Bede's account (HE III, 1–2) is the primary narrative source, supplemented by Adomnán's vision story, which Kirby treats as evidence of the Iona connection without endorsing its literal details. The site "Heavenfield" near Hexham is conventional but not archaeologically established.
