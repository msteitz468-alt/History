# The Licinian-Sextian Laws

## Narrative

The **Leges Liciniae Sextiae** (Licinian-Sextian Laws) of **367 BCE** were the most
significant constitutional settlement of the [[struggle-of-the-orders|Struggle of the
Orders]] — the laws that ended the patrician monopoly on the consulship and created the
institutional framework for the joint patrician-plebeian **nobilitas** that would
govern the Roman Republic for the next two centuries.

## The Rogations and the "Anarchy"

The laws were proposed by two tribunes of the plebs: **C. Licinius Stolo** and
**L. Sextius Lateranus**, who pursued their rogations for approximately **ten years**
(from c. 376 BCE). The Roman tradition (Livy VI.35; Diodorus) records a "five-year
anarchy" from 375 to 371 BCE during which the tribunes vetoed all magistrate elections,
leaving Rome without curule magistrates. Cornell's assessment: the five-year period
is exaggerated — Diodorus records only one year without magistrates, and the five-year
figure looks like another device (similar to the "Dictator Years") to extend the
fourth-century chronology. A **real constitutional standoff** occurred, but probably
not lasting five consecutive years.

## The Three Provisions

The passed legislation addressed three connected complaints:

### 1. Debt Relief
Interest already paid on debts was to be **deducted from the principal**; the
remaining principal was to be paid off in **three equal annual installments**.

This was a rescheduling measure, not debt cancellation — it gave heavily indebted
smallholders a structured path to clearance rather than the alternative of nexum
or outright default. Cornell notes that this provision is possibly a retrojection
of later debt-relief measures (the 326 BCE Lex Poetelia Papiria, which abolished
nexum, addressed similar grievances) and that its precise content may have been
reshaped by later annalists who knew the later crisis.

### 2. Ager Publicus Limitation
No citizen was to hold more than **500 iugera** (approximately 125 hectares) of
**ager publicus** (Roman public land, i.e., land nominally owned by the state but
in practice occupied by wealthy landholders).

This was a cap on the de facto privatization of the public domain that wealthier
families had been carrying out since the capture of Veii (and earlier). Cornell
treats this as the most historically problematic provision: the **Licinian land
law** appears again in the tradition in the context of the Gracchi (133 BCE) with
virtually the same terms, suggesting either that the fourth-century law was a
retrojection from the Gracchan crisis, or that the Gracchi were reviving a genuine
old measure. The latter is possible; the former cannot be excluded.

### 3. Plebeian Consulship
**One of the two annual consuls must be plebeian.**

This was the constitutional revolution. The first plebeian consul was **L. Sextius
Lateranus**, one of the law's own authors, elected for 366 BCE. The patricians
extracted a price: a third chief magistrate, the **praetor**, was created alongside
the two consuls, initially reserved for patricians and holding military command and
judicial functions. A new aedileship, the **curule aedileship**, was also created
— initially patrician, then alternating between patrician and plebeian annually.

The net effect: the formal patrician monopoly on the chief magistracies was broken,
but the patricians retained the new praetor and shared the curule aedileship.

## The End of the Consular Tribunate

The reform simultaneously **abolished the consular tribunate** (*tribuni militum
consulari potestate*) that had replaced the dual consulship intermittently from
444 to 367 BCE. The consular tribunate had been an administrative experiment that
failed to resolve the underlying constitutional conflict; the Licinian-Sextian
settlement made it redundant by restoring the dual consulship with built-in
plebeian access.

## Subsequent Settlements

The Licinian-Sextian laws were not the end of the Struggle of the Orders; they
were the pivotal point. Subsequent legislation continued to erode the remaining
patrician monopolies:

- **Lex Genucia (342 BCE)**: Both consuls could be plebeian (and later made it
  mandatory to have at least one plebeian); also prohibited holding two magistracies
  simultaneously or iteration of the same magistracy within ten years
- **Leges Publiliae (339 BCE)**: Senate must ratify popular decisions in advance
  (*patrum auctoritas* becomes a pre-vote formality, not post-vote veto)
- **Lex Ogulnia (300 BCE)**: Plebeians admitted to the pontificate and the
  augurship — opening the two most prestigious priesthoods
- **Lex Hortensia (287 BCE)**: Plebiscites binding on the whole people without
  senatorial ratification — the final settlement

## The Rise of the Nobilitas

Cornell (CAH VII.2, Ch. 7) emphasizes that the immediate political consequence of
367 BCE was **not the triumph of a homogeneous plebeian class but the emergence of
a joint patrician-plebeian ruling class** — the *nobilitas*. The key feature of
this ruling class was not patrician birth but **iteration of the consulship**: a
family that had held the consulship was *nobilis*, regardless of whether it was
patrician or plebeian.

The fourth century was the formative period of this nobilitas: a small number of
families — both old patrician houses (the Valerii, Cornelii, Fabii) and new plebeian
families (the Licinii, Sextii, Genucii, Publilii) — dominated the consular fasti.
The first generation after 367 was a meritocratic one: plebeian consuls from this
period were often men of genuine military achievement. By the third century, the
consulship had become the hereditary preserve of a closed class of *nobiles*, and
the open-competition character of the early fourth century was gone.

## Cornell's Analytical Point

Cornell distinguishes sharply between:
- **The fourth-century political structure**: competition among talented individuals
  of both orders, relatively open access to the consulship within the *nobiles*
  class, dominated by military achievement
- **The classical Republican oligarchy** (third–second century): closed oligarchy
  of interlocking families, dominated by the Senate, where the consulship was the
  hereditary expectation of a narrow group

The Licinian-Sextian settlement created the first; the second was a development of
the following century. Projecting the classical-Republican oligarchy backward onto
the immediate post-367 period is an anachronism.

## Historiography

The Licinian-Sextian laws are among the most-debated events in Roman constitutional
history. Three main controversies:

1. **The land and debt provisions**: Whether these are genuine fourth-century
   measures or later constructions (especially the land cap, which so closely
   mirrors the Gracchan situation) is unresolved. Cornell accepts them as genuine
   in outline while acknowledging the possibility of elaboration.

2. **The "anarchy"**: The five-year anarchy of 375–371 BCE is almost certainly
   exaggerated; one year is plausible, five is not. The Varronian chronology needed
   the extension to reconcile its arithmetic.

3. **The praetor**: The precise nature of the new praetor — was it a genuinely new
   office or a renamed existing one? — is disputed. The most likely answer is that
   the urban praetor was a new creation, specializing in civil jurisdiction, while
   "praetor" as a military title was older.
