## Narrative

The pre-Columbian contact between Polynesian voyagers and the Indigenous peoples of South America is among the most debated episodes in Pacific prehistory. The weight of evidence now supports at minimum one or more episodes of contact in which Polynesian canoes reached the South American coast between roughly 1000 and 1400 CE, with the most specific evidence centered on central-south Chile (Mapuche territory, roughly 32°–42°S).

The event arises directly from the logic of Polynesian eastward expansion: once Rapa Nui was settled (c. 1000–1200 CE), the nearest major landmass to the east was the South American continent. As Ramírez-Aliaga writes in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]], "contact with South America may have been accidental, but must be considered as an inevitable consequence of the process of exploration and colonization of the South Pacific, when the Polynesians were moving consistently to the east."

Bedford (ch. 19, same volume) provides the base context: Polynesian voyagers had "irrefutable evidence that there was contact, in the form of sweet potato and the bottle gourd making its way into the Pacific. The sweet potato was domesticated in South America and the bottle gourd has been present there for 10,000 years. It appears that Pacific Islanders made landfall on the South American coast and successfully returned with both crops." Archaeobotanical samples confirming these pre-contact introductions have been found "in early archaeological deposits across the Polynesian triangle, including Rapa Nui, Hawai'i, New Zealand, and the Cook Islands."

The more specific question — whether contact extended to southern Chile and involved cultural exchange with the Mapuche — is argued chiefly by Ramírez-Aliaga (ch. 21). His evidence:

1. **Chicken bones:** At El Arenal, Arauco Province (core Mapuche territory), chicken bones recovered in archaeological context yielded DNA "identical to samples from Tonga and Sāmoa." Three AMS datings confirmed a pre-Hispanic context: "1300–1450 CE." This constitutes "the first hard evidence for a Polynesian contact in southern Chile."

2. **Human skeletal morphology:** At Mocha Island (38°S), craniometric analysis of museum collections revealed that "three out of six complete skulls fell into the Southeast Asia and Pacific pattern." The most Polynesian-morphology skull was found "in a clear El Vergel archaeological context (1290 CE)"; two others from the Early Ceramic period (ending ~1000 CE). In 2011, excavations on Mocha Island recovered "a male skeleton with Polynesian traits, including a very special offering: a toki-kura made of polished green stone" — the first such artefact found in clear context. The same Polynesian morphological traits have been found in a cemetery from the Early Ceramic period at Tunquen, near Valparaíso, 600 km north of Mocha Island.

3. **Cultural parallels:** A dense cluster of parallels between Mapuche and Polynesian culture includes: the toki (stone adze; "a pan-Polynesian word for adze"); the toki-kura adze pendant (cf. Māori *kura pounamu*); stone clubs (clavas) resembling Māori wahaika and Chatham Island patu; the sewn-plank canoe (dalca of Chiloé — "the only particular item which could be still considered a Polynesian borrowing," with Polynesian parallels and its Polynesian name *tomolo'o* among the Chumash of California); lunar calendar; Pleiades New Year; a hockey-like game (*pai pai* in Rurutu / Austral Islands; *palin* in southern Chile); cord-knotting for data recording; and a dozen possible linguistic cognates.

## Causal Analysis

### Routes and Mechanism

The counterclockwise current system of the South Pacific, with Rapa Nui near its center, makes westward passage from South America to Polynesia very difficult ("it was virtually impossible to sail there from South America"). The logical Polynesian route to southern Chile uses the belt of westerly winds south of latitude 35°S: "separating from the axis of the counterclockwise movement of currents and winds, leaving Rapa Nui in the centre, down to the belt of westerly winds south of latitude 35, and then turning directly east, precisely in the direction of the Mapuche area." Computer simulations "revealed that all voyages from Polynesia would eventually reach South America, directly to Chile or farther to the north, according to seasonal changes."

El Niño events generated "anomalous westerlies" that could drive a canoe "directly from the heart of Polynesia to the shores of south-central Chile." Polynesian explorers' strategy of "slow sailing against the prevailing trade winds, so that they could return home quickly and safely with the wind at their backs" made exploration toward South America rational: they could always return downwind.

The linguistic affiliation of the sweet potato confirms Polynesian agency in the transfer: Peter Buck (Te Rangi Hīroa) observed that '*kumara*' was "brought back by Polynesian pioneers, and dispersed throughout Polynesia along with its Cañari (native people from the Gulf of Ecuador) name: *kumal*." introduced into the Cook Islands at about 1000 CE.

### Return Voyages

Ramírez-Aliaga proposes that return voyages would have followed the Humboldt Current north along Chile's coast, permitting acquisition of sweet potato in northern Chile and then a westward turn into eastern Polynesia. Recent computer simulations confirm this possibility.

## The Debate

This event is one of the more contentious in Pacific archaeology, with several distinct contested dimensions:

**The sweet potato and bottle gourd** are the strongest and most broadly accepted evidence: the archaeobotanical record across the Polynesian triangle makes a pre-Columbian transfer from South America incontrovertible. The primary dispute is *who made the contact* — most scholars now accept it was Polynesian voyagers reaching South America rather than South Americans reaching Polynesia (the Heyerdahl Kon-Tiki hypothesis, for which "no evidence has emerged to support"). The only question is where on the South American coast they landed.

**The chicken DNA evidence** (Storey et al. 2007) has been contested: critics raised concerns about contamination and dating. Ramírez-Aliaga states that the team's replies "consistently sustained the case as the first hard evidence for a Polynesian contact in southern Chile." The debate remains open in the literature.

**The skeletal evidence from Mocha Island** is the newest and most preliminary line. The craniometric analysis (Matisoo-Smith and Ramírez-Aliaga 2010) identified three skulls matching a Pacific/Southeast Asian morphological pattern; a toki-kura offering recovered in situ in 2011 was a significant contextual find. DNA analysis on the human bones is described as "the next step."

**The cultural parallels** (material culture, language, technology) are numerous but individually inconclusive — each could be independent invention. Cumulatively, Ramírez-Aliaga argues, "so many parallels are suggestive of some form of connection to say the least." Critics note that some parallels (e.g. patu clubs) are also found in North America, weakening the case for a specific Polynesian-Mapuche connection rather than a broader pattern.

**The alleged admixture DNA evidence** (Ioannidis et al. 2020, claiming Native American gene flow into Polynesia predating Easter Island settlement, from present Zenu people of Colombia) is treated with skepticism by Ramírez-Aliaga, citing Elizabeth Matisoo-Smith: "geneticists fail to consider testing DNA from Asian counterparts in spite of the fact that both South American and Polynesian populations share some Asian origin. The results show no admixture but genetic similarities."

The chapter in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] (Anderson, ch. 20) notes the absence of robust data for some older arguments: "no other arguments from linguistics, human skeletal morphology, or introduction of domestic fowl to South America are now based on robust data" when discussing the possibility that South Polynesians sailed east across the Southern Ocean.

Resolution status: **partially-resolved** — the sweet potato and bottle gourd transfer is settled as Polynesian-initiated; the Mapuche-specific evidence remains contested and under active investigation.

## Historiography and Primary Sources

The contact hypothesis has a long historiographical pedigree. The British missionary William Ellis first observed a link between Polynesia and America in 1829. Tahitian resident Jacques Moerenhout highlighted specific parallels between southern Chile and Polynesia in 1835. John Macmillan Brown identified new Polynesian elements (quipu, cooking ovens, toki) in 1924. Thor Heyerdahl's Kon-Tiki expedition (1947) attempted to prove *east-to-west* contact (South American to Polynesian), but his hypothesis has been rejected by the archaeological community: "no evidence has emerged to support Heyerdahl's speculations."

The modern debate was transformed by ancient DNA analysis: chicken DNA (Storey et al. 2007), rat DNA (Matisoo-Smith), and human skeletal DNA (pending). The chapter in [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]] represents the most recent synthesis integrating archaeobotany, animal archaeology, craniometry, and computer modelling.
