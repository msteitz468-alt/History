# The First World War (1914–1918)

The **First World War** (1914–1918) was the industrialized great-power war that destroyed the
nineteenth-century European order, killed some **9–10 million combatants** (and comparable civilian
numbers through war, famine and the 1918 influenza), and ushered in the
[[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]]. Its **origins** are the subject of a major historiographical dispute,
documented for the wiki through **[[joll-origins-first-world-war|James Joll's *The Origins of the First
World War*]]** and treated on **[[causes-of-the-first-world-war|its own controversy page]]**.

## Narrative

**1917 French phase (Doughty Ch. 7):** After attrition of 1916, Nivelle (succeeding Joffre Dec 1916) pursued "strategy of decisive battle" with his "formula" (massed artillery in depth + continuous 24-48 hr thrust to enemy guns for breakthrough and exploitation vs reserves). Main effort Chemin des Dames/Aisne 16-17 Apr after British Arras support. Despite unprecedented preparation, weather, German air/reverse slopes, and lost surprise limited gains to small salients; ~134k French casualties in 10 days. Failure by 20 Apr produced morale collapse and command transition to Pétain (chief of staff 29 Apr), setting stage for "healing"/limited offensives. See [[events/nivelle-offensive-1917]] and [[sources/doughty-pyrrhic-victory-2005]]. `contributed_to:` mutinies context; `produced:` shift from decisive-battle search.

**1915–1916 French Strategy and Operations (from Doughty).** After the Marne and the stabilization of the front, France confronted a prolonged war of attrition on the Western Front while seeking to maintain its grand strategy of multifront pressure against the Central Powers. Joffre's 1915 offensives in Artois and Champagne sought rupture but stalled against German second defensive belts; he accepted that France could not remain passive yet shifted language toward attrition (*usure*) while Britain and others bore more burden. Detailed below from exclusive Ch. 3–4 range.

**Siege Warfare and Positional Warfare, 1914–1915 (Doughty Ch. 3):** A visit by GQG liaison to Poincaré on 29 Sep 1914 "marked the opening of a new phase in the war and the end of Joffre’s hopes for continued movement and success." Poincaré: “we are condemned to wage a long war of siege against the enemy’s trenches.” Joffre halted attacks due to 75-mm shortage. Continuous front Channel–Switzerland by mid-Nov. Joffre expected brief siege phase only. He refused pure defense ("absolutely negative") and ordered "partial offensives"/"nibbling" (*grignoter*) from 17 Sep to maintain offensive spirit, fix Germans, prevent lateral shifts, and aid Russia. Examples: Third Army attacks on Aubréville heights (rail protection, ~10k cas Nov); Second Army local actions near Somme (gains of 300–1,000 m or failures at high price). "The French were less prepared for the grinding siege warfare that now began than the war of movement."

Industrial mobilization: critical 75-mm stocks post-Marne; production ramp (22k→42k rds/day by Jan); heavy artillery lag (300 vs 2k needed/day); new weapons (trench mortars, Estienne early armor prediction). Worker/industry losses (Briey basin) slowed adaptation. Doctrine: artillery must prepare attacks ("infantry should be held back"); ops "slower," "prepared down to the smallest detail." Joffre centralized control; created army groups (Foch Northern, Dubail Eastern); purged generals extensively. Opposed early peripheral ops (Salonika): "It is not Austria that we must beat; it is Germany." British tensions over Ypres relief for French reserves.

Dec 1914–Mar 1915 offensives: principal Artois (Tenth Army/Pétain XXXIII Corps for Vimy; inadequate 110 heavy guns) and Champagne (Fourth/de Langle; "continuous battle" successive efforts). Small gains (~1 km), 43k cas in one Champagne phase alone for 3 km². Joffre: "insufficient and too brief artillery support; too few personnel engaged on fronts that were too narrow." Total ~268k French dead Oct–Mar. `produced:` [[processes/attrition-warfare-wwi]], [[concepts/partial-offensive]], [[concepts/continuous-battle]].

**An Offensive Strategy, May–October 1915 (Doughty Ch. 4):** With incremental means, Joffre planned offensives to aid Russia post-Gorlice-Tarnów (2 May). Multifront simultaneity: "each helps the others." May–June Second Artois (Tenth Army): May 9 gains; June 16 (doubled heavy ammo 63k rds) smaller gains; 102.5k cas main (35% killed); ~3 km/8 km front. No rupture. Secondaries added heavy cost. Political shock; Joffre criticized but defended offensive: "We’re not the people to let go." Began explicit attrition language while pushing "drain the enemy."

Sept–Oct double offensive (Artois + Champagne): simultaneous to block German concentration. 25 Sep: Pétain Second Army XIV Corps 4 km; II Colonial 3 km; 16k+ prisoners. Second defensive band halted; false breach reports wasted reserves. Rain, uncut wire. Artois Oct failed (insufficient prep; exhausted troops). Four armies: 191.8k cas; 2–4 km gains. Joffre blamed insufficient artillery; ordered reconstitution and local actions only. "This is the end of our offensive." `contributed_to:` 1916 attrition battles; `part_of:` [[events/first-world-war-1914]].

See full claims and quotes in [[sources/doughty-pyrrhic-victory-2005]] (Range 3). Updates to [[concepts/rupture]], [[concepts/methodical-battle]], actors, and hub.

Political and military leaders debated alternatives to the Western Front stalemate. Operations in the Dardanelles/Gallipoli (French division under d’Amade then Gouraud/Bailloud; Kum Kale landing 25 Apr 1915 with heavy casualties) shifted focus after Bulgarian entry into the war (Sep 1915) to the Balkans. Influential voices (Franchet d’Espèrey, Gallieni, Castelnau, Briand) favored Salonika to aid Serbia and strike a weak point on the Central Powers perimeter. Joffre vehemently opposed large diversions of resources ("dispersion of our efforts will be true folly"; "We are simply changing the point of application of the forces we have in the Near East"). The Viviani government fell (29 Oct 1915); Briand became premier and Gallieni minister of war. A limited Franco-British force under Sarrail landed at Salonika (Oct 1915), advanced to Strumica Station and the Tcherna–Vardar junction, but withdrew to a fortified Salonika perimeter by mid-December after the Serbian collapse. The force became a "bluff" to fix Bulgarian troops and influence neutrals; Romania joined late and was overrun. Joffre gained formal command as commander-in-chief of all French armies (Dec 1915 decree) over opposition from some politicians, ensuring Salonika remained subordinate to the Western Front.

At the allied military conference at Chantilly (6–8 Dec 1915), Joffre's "Plan of Action" was accepted: coordinated offensives in principal theaters to attrit the enemy, with "partial and local offensives" by powers with abundant manpower. France would collaborate only as resources permitted.

French doctrine evolved to the "methodical battle." The GQG directive of 26 January 1916 required successive objectives, new preparation for each enemy belt, and operations under a single leader controlling the means. Attacks would be "powerful, slow, methodical, repeated": artillery preparation, infantry bound, forward displacement of batteries, repeat. The goal was to destroy reserves piece by piece until rupture became possible. Artillery stocks were rebuilt (target 17M 75-mm rounds); heavy pieces dispersed for flexibility; Pétain tasked with training large units in new methods.

**Verdun.** The Germans, under Falkenhayn, chose Verdun to "bleed to death" the French army at a point it would never abandon. French intelligence accurately reported the threat, but Joffre viewed it initially as a diversion. The German bombardment opened 21 Feb 1916; rapid gains followed; Fort Douaumont fell 25 Feb. Joffre sent Castelnau, then assigned Pétain and Second Army. After political pressure from Briand, the east bank was to be held "no matter what the cost." Pétain implemented the noria rotation system (divisions relieved before collapse; ultimately dozens rotated), defense in depth, and the Sacred Way supply line. Fighting spread to the west bank (Hill 304, Mort Homme). Command changes followed political discontent: Gallieni replaced by Roques; Dubail and de Langle removed; Pétain promoted to Central Army Group (May); Nivelle took Second Army at Verdun. Germans took Fort Vaux (June) and pushed toward Souville. Nivelle issued the order "Comrades, they will not pass!" French counteroffensives in Oct–Dec (Nivelle/Mangin), using concentrated artillery and rolling barrages, recaptured Douaumont (24 Oct) and Vaux (2 Nov) and pushed the Germans back 7.5 km from the citadel. French casualties ~377k (heaviest early and May–June).

**Somme (French role).** Joffre selected the Somme to join British and French forces and shift the main effort to Britain. After negotiations, the offensive was set for ~1 July. Verdun forced drastic reduction of French forces (Sixth Army under Fayolle/Foch with ~11 divisions instead of 30–39 planned). On 1 July, after preparatory fire, XX Corps (north bank) and I Colonial Corps (south) seized all assigned first-day objectives (1.5–2 km advances) and thousands of prisoners—far better results than the British on most of the front. French used methodical, artillery-dominant tactics with superior numbers of heavy guns. Subsequent operations (to Maisonette and beyond) made gains but exploitation was constrained by Joffre's insistence on supporting the British north bank and by Foch/Fayolle's frustrations with British methods. Joffre repeatedly urged Haig to attack on a broad front with greater vigor; relations were strained. French casualties on the Somme ~202k. The offensive contributed to drawing German reserves from Verdun but did not produce the decisive results Joffre had hoped for in coordination with Russian and Italian efforts.

By late 1916 the strategic situation had not fundamentally changed despite Allied offensives and Romanian entry. Political leaders noted the contrast between Nivelle's successful limited thrusts at Verdun and the costly, slow Somme and began to lose faith in Joffre. See dedicated pages [[events/battle-of-verdun-1916]], [[events/battle-of-the-somme-1916]], [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/first-world-war]], [[processes/attrition-warfare-wwi]], and [[concepts/methodical-battle]].

## Narrative (continued)

Triggered by the assassination of Archduke **Franz Ferdinand** at Sarajevo (28 June 1914) and the **July
Crisis**, the war pitted the **Central Powers** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, later the Ottomans and
Bulgaria) against the **Allies** (France, the British Empire, Russia, later Italy and the United States).
The German **Schlieffen Plan** drive through Belgium failed at the Marne; the **Western Front** congealed
into trench stalemate (Verdun, the Somme, Passchendaele) while the **Eastern Front** stayed mobile until
Russia's collapse. The war globalized through the Ottoman theatres (Gallipoli, Mesopotamia, the Arab
Revolt), the colonies, and the war at sea (Jutland, unrestricted submarine warfare). **Russia's
revolutions of 1917** took it out of the war; American entry (1917) and the failure of the German spring
offensive of 1918 brought the **Armistice of 11 November 1918**.

### Opening of the Western Front: War Plans and Battle of the Frontiers (August 1914)

Detailed operational analysis grounded in [[sources/strachan-first-world-war-to-arms-2001]]: see dedicated campaign page [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-the-frontiers-1914]].

German planning was never a single fixed "Schlieffen plan" but incremental and contingent: "There was no single solution, no one plan." The 1905 memorandum (often misidentified as the plan) was a post-retirement private paper. Elder Moltke had expected long wars ("a war lasting seven or even thirty years") and favoured counter-offensives. Schlieffen pursued west-first envelopment through Belgium to bypass French fortifications, but staff rides showed no decisive short victory. Moltke the Younger (fatalistic: "We are not superior to the French") modified: strengthened the left wing for Lorraine option, narrowed right (no Dutch violation; must seize Liège immediately), respected that "the French would not conform." French Plan XVII (developed 1911–14 under Joffre after Messimy's July 1911 High Command reorganization) was primarily a concentration/deployment plan, not a rigid war plan. Its main body stated the commander-in-chief's intention "to deliver, with all forces assembled, an attack against the German armies." It organized five armies in the northeast (1st–5th from right to left; 4th in reserve) with options to attack north or south of Metz-Thionville or into Belgium (toward Neufchâteau/Arlon). Joffre deliberately preserved flexibility and concealed full intentions from political superiors to avoid meddling (e.g., early Aug response to Dubail: "That's your plan, not mine"). 

Evolution (Doughty Ch. 1): Earlier Plans XI–XVI were largely defensive-offensive, relying on Séré de Rivières fortifications (Verdun-Toul-Épinal-Belfort). Joffre's immediate Sep 1911 revision of Plan XVI shifted Fifth Army left to Mézières, increased front-line divisions (~38 to 58), and emphasized immediate offensive. Political leaders (Caillaux, Poincaré) refused preemptive French violation of Belgian neutrality (fears of Britain/Italy aligning with Germany), though Joffre argued the terrain advantages. Franco-Russian military convention (1892–94) and 1910–13 staff talks required simultaneous offensives ("vigorous and determined"; France ~day 11, Russia day 14) to force Germany onto two fronts. Joffre and planners expected German advance through Belgium/Luxembourg but underestimated depth/scale west of Meuse-Sambre (assumed insufficient active corps for deep envelopment without effective reserve "shock" use; intel knew German plans called for employing reserves like active units).

Joffre also drove codification of offensive à outrance doctrine (catalyzed by Grandmaison's Feb 1911 lectures criticizing "nearly complete atrophy" of offensive spirit): 1913 regulations stressed "Only the offensive yields positive results," battles as "moral contests," artillery supporting (not preparing) attacks, and bayonet as infantry's "supreme weapon." Artillery remained weak in heavy field pieces (4,076 75-mm but only 308 heavy vs. German 848); Joffre's push limited and heavy guns placed at army level. The 1913 three-year service law increased active strength to ~700,000 for better covering force and immediate offensive capability. Short-war assumptions dominated; little preparation for industrial mobilization or prolonged conflict.

Joffre committed to Lorraine and Ardennes thrusts in initial execution while underestimating German Belgian swing and reserve use. Belgium's strict neutrality produced military confusion and improvisation ("the Belgian army could not be mobilized in 1914... it will have to improvise").

The Battle of the Frontiers (14–26 Aug) comprised:
- Alsace-Lorraine: French gains at Mulhouse reversed; Morhange heights disaster for 2nd Army ("advanced into devastating fire").
- Ardennes: 3rd/4th Armies attacked in columns; 3rd Colonial Division lost ~10,503 in one day at Bertrix.
- Detailed French GQG perspective (Doughty Ch. 2 exclusive range): Plan XVII execution under Joffre (Gen. Instr. No. 1 8 Aug: right jab Lorraine south of Metz-Thionville fixing; left main into Belgium to strike center). Early artillery/infantry doctrinal failure apparent (Castelnau 15 Aug: successive fortified approach positions + heavy prep fire needed). German counter 20 Aug shattered uncoordinated armies; 3rd/4th in Ardennes 21–23 Aug suffered fog/no-prep attacks, Rossignol Colonial disaster (11,648 for two divs), Virton panics, poor liaison. 5th Army (Lanrezac) Sambre crisis 21–22 Aug (easy German crossings); Guise counter 29 Aug slowed pursuit (d’Espèrey I Corps vigorous). Joffre relieved commanders (limogés: 2 armies, 10 corps, 38 divs by early Sep); ordered left withdrawal 24 Aug and left shifts. Battle ended 23 Aug with full initiative to Germans. See [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-the-frontiers-1914]] (Doughty-grounded expansion).
- Sambre/Charleroi: Lanrezac dithered into unplanned defensive; Germans secured crossings.
- Mons/Le Cateau: BEF successful delaying actions (Le Cateau: II Corps 7,812 casualties) enabled by German intelligence failures ("no enemy within 80 kilometres").

By 23 Aug Joffre reported shift "from the offensive to the defensive: 'Our object must be to last out as long as possible, trying to wear the enemy out...'". Retreat risked rout but cohesion held. See hub analysis for OOB, phases, principles of war, and critical decisions.

**Marne campaign and end of mobile warfare (Strachan Range 04, 25 Aug–Nov 1914):** Joffre's resolution and purges (three army, seven corps, 34 divisional commanders dismissed by 6 Sep) plus rail redeployment of 20 infantry + 3 cavalry divisions from right to left produced 41 allied divisions against the German right by 6 September (from 24.5 on 23 Aug). French 75 mm artillery proved devastating on the defensive. Kluck's turn south-east after Guise and subsequent shift of two corps north to face Maunoury on the Ourcq opened the gap the BEF advanced into. Hentsch's mission led to the ordered retreat to the Aisne (prepared ridge positions). Moltke relieved 14 Sep; Falkenhayn continued right-wing envelopment attempts. Antwerp fell 10 Oct; Belgians held Yser line by inundation (27 Oct onward). First Ypres exhausted both sides (German ~80,000 losses; BEF 54k of total 90k by 30 Nov fell in salient). By 25 Nov Falkenhayn ordered the western armies to form defensive positions and hold conquered ground. 

**French high command detail (Doughty Ch. 2 exclusive):** Gen. Instr. No. 4 (1 Sep) ordered deep withdrawal to Seine-Aube; 6th Army covered Paris. Government left for Bordeaux 2–3 Sep. Joffre relieved Lanrezac 4 Sep (replaced by d’Espèrey). Concept: draw into salient then cut neck with Paris attack toward Meaux (2 Sep mod). Gallieni ordered Maunoury east 4 Sep; d’Espèrey–Wilson agreement + Joffre's fist-banging appeal to Sir John ("honor of England at stake") fixed 6 Sep date. Kluck shifts opened gap; 5th/ British exploited; Foch countered at St-Gond; Sarrail held Verdun contact. Joffre credited for composure and not diverting from left. Slow pursuit; Germans on Aisne ~14 Sep. "Race" leapfrog west (Second Army first); Foch coordinator north. ~400k French dead by end mobile war. See [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-the-marne-1914]] (Doughty expansion) and new actors. "The Marne was a French victory... Germany had failed to secure the quick victory on which its war plan rested." Full analysis: [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-the-marne-1914]]. `produced:` [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] (campaign); `contributed_to:` trench stalemate on Western Front.

**Eastern Front 1914: East Prussia continuation, Serbia, Galicia, and Poland (Strachan Range 06):** The Eastern theatre remained mobile throughout 1914. After Tannenberg, Rennenkampf's 1st army "had barely moved, advancing with excessive deliberation, and doing so in the wrong direction." Zhilinskii prioritised security; the 1st army retreated 48 km then 88 km in ~50 hours once ordered. The Masurian lakes operation (early Sep) saw Germans (I, XVII, XX corps key) attempt envelopment of Rennenkampf but weight remained frontal; Russian 10th army covered gaps. Tannenberg itself yielded "the capture of 92,000 prisoners and nearly 400 guns"; its value was "propaganda effect" and illusion of German invincibility more than strategic crippling (Russia lost 310,000 in six weeks but not fatally). Russian command problems (Zhilinskii replaced 17 Sep by Ruszkii; Rennenkampf blamed) persisted; "the Grand Duke Nicholas, Zhilinskii, Rennenkampf, and Samsonov had all pulled in different and contradictory directions."

In **Serbia**, war opened with Serb destruction of Save/Danube bridges (28 July) and Austro-Hungarian bombardment of Belgrade (29 July). Conrad immediately diverted the 2nd army north to Galicia; Potiorek (Bosnia governor, three jobs) commanded 5th and 6th armies (max 290,000, Landsturm-heavy, 40% south Slav) for "defence alone." Potiorek pushed ambitious western thrust (6th army across upper Drina to Uzice) supported by 5th from NW (Jadar/Valjevo); "the immediate strategic purpose of the offensive from the west was defence." Serb forces under Putnik (chief of staff; Crown Prince Alexander nominal) completed concentration by 10 Aug despite rail shortages. First levy 180,000 mobilised to 350,000 but "in some units up to 30 per cent of infantrymen had no rifles"; shell production 250-260/day but powder for only 80-100. Serbs used "offensive defence" from central position (Palanka-Valjevo line). 5th AH crossed Drina 15 Aug and met 2nd/3rd Serb armies on Cer/Jadar plateau; "Austrians, sniped at by guerrillas, toiled uphill in soaring temperatures. They had outstripped their supplies." Stubborn Serb centre battle + flank restoration forced Austrian withdrawal by 20 Aug; divisions lost a third of rifle strength. 2nd army (IV corps held Sabac briefly) diverted to Galicia 20 Aug. Serb invasion of Srem (6 Sep) and Montenegro/ Uzice group toward Sarajevo produced no rising and was neutralised; second Austrian offensive (8-23 Sep) established Drina bridgeheads but ended in stalemate and "positional warfare." "Strategically, if not tactically, Serbia had won a major victory." Potiorek vs Conrad/AOK frictions over troops and munitions; no quick Balkan victory for prestige or alliance-building. `caused_by:` Serbian defensive preparations and recent combat experience; `produced:` failure of Austrian Balkan strategy; `contributed_to:` tying down Habsburg forces while Galicia collapsed.

In **Galicia**, Conrad (despite 2nd army late/wrong place and only 31-37 divisions vs Russian 45 infantry + 18+ cavalry) launched offensive north-east (1st army Dankl to Lublin; 4th Auffenberg to Cholm; 3rd Brudermann east of Lemberg). "Conrad was never good at modifying his grandiose strategic objectives." Cavalry reconnaissance "of extraordinary incompetence"; ignored evidence of Russian 8th army at Proskurov. Krasnik (23-25 Aug): 1st AH pushed 4th Russian back. Auffenberg hit Plehve's 5th left, separating it from Evert. South: Brudermann attacked at Zloczow/Zlota Lipa (26 Aug) "poorly co-ordinated, and pushed in too quickly"; routed, Lemberg abandoned without fight 2 Sep (fell back 32 km). Conrad attempted envelopment using late 2nd army (Böhm-Ermolli); uncoordinated attacks failed. Full retreat ordered 11 Sep to Dniester then San. "Its total losses were about 350,000 men; some divisions were reduced to a third of their combatant strength." "One thousand locomotives and 15,000 wagons were abandoned." Commanders purged (Brudermann, corps commanders); Franz Joseph inquiry thwarted. Przemysl (fortified with 50 km trenches, 1,000 km wire) besieged from 21 Sep (garrison actually 130k not 85k); its resistance "sucked in the Russians and covered the Austrian recovery." Russian logistics (gauge change, destroyed lines, waist-deep mud by Oct) halted South-West Front. `produced:` Austrian recovery around Carpathians/Przemysl; `contributed_to:` Russian pause before Cracow/Poland shift.

In **Poland**, post-Lemberg OHL formed 9th army (Hindenburg/Ludendorff; 4 corps + from reduced 8th) at Breslau. Advance 28 Sep toward Vistula achieved relief of Austrians (linked 4-5 Oct, Przemysl relieved 9 Oct) but "German surprise was virtually complete" when 9 Oct captured Russian order of battle revealed three armies massing. Germans fell back 20 Oct. Stavka exploited by shifting 9th army and concentrating Ivanov's front (2nd army Warsaw, 5th, 4th Ivangorod, 9th Sandomir). Second phase: Lodz operation. 9th army redeployed (800 trains) from Thorn area, advanced 11 Nov, broke hinge of 1st/2nd Russian, encircled Lodz with four corps by 18 Nov (Scheffer XXV reserve deep SE). Plehve's 5th army struck German right; Rennenkampf slow from north. Scheffer broke out 23-24 Nov via Brezeziny ("with them 10,000 prisoners") despite orders. "Lodz could be seen as a draw." Russians abandoned Lodz 6 Dec; fell back to Bzura-Ravka shorter line. "In the east 1.5 German divisions occupied a front that would have been held by five divisions in the west." Exhaustion and supply—not trenches—produced stasis. Independent commands (Conrad/Hindenburg-Ludendorff) produced rival envelopment schemes rather than coordinated action. ### Ottoman Entry and German Global Strategy, 1914 (Strachan Range 09)

Turkey entered the war through its own devices in pursuit of its own interests — sovereignty, reversal of recent losses, and consolidation — not as German proxy. Secret alliance 2 August; Goeben and Breslau arrival at Dardanelles 10 August (see dedicated [[events/goeben-escape-1914]]) “ordained” the outcome. Britain’s impoundment of Turkish dreadnoughts (29 July) inflamed opinion; Entente offered no concessions on capitulations or Russian threat. Abrogation of capitulations (8 Sept) and German gold loan secured the path. Enver (with Talaat, Djemal) authorized the Black Sea raid 29 Oct under Souchon: Sevastopol, Odessa, Theodosia, Novorossisk. Russia declared war 2 Nov; Britain and France 5 Nov after Dardanelles bombardment. On 14 Nov the Sheikh-ul-Islam proclaimed holy war before the Sultan, calling Muslims worldwide (270 million targeted) to rise against Britain, France, Russia et al. “All believers throughout the world were called upon to fight... The Crimea, Kazan, Turkestan... India, China, Afghanistan, Iran, and Africa were urged to make common cause with the Ottoman empire.”

Germany’s global strategy (Moltke 2 Aug onward) used Turkey as land bridge for peripheral blows: revolution in India/Egypt, Caucasus diversion, Suez seizure, Persia/Afghanistan thrust to India. “It was not the Entente but the Central Powers that broadened the war in 1914.” Kaiser and Oppenheim rhetoric framed jihad to “lose India” for Britain; Moltke’s “shopping list” embraced Asia, Egypt, Caucasus, Afghanistan. Enver’s war plan (22 Oct) listed holy war, Egypt attack (most specific), Caucasus hold, possible Odessa — “not a plan” but political vision (pan-Turanian + pan-Islamic) grafted onto alliance. Logistical reality limited execution: Turkish army “not a finely honed instrument”; 19,000-man Suez force vs 70k British; Sarikamish (Caucasus) disaster (75k Turkish losses incl. 30k frostbite). Missions to Kabul (Niedermayer, Indian exiles) fractured by command disputes. India’s exposure (British garrison 15k) and Bengal terrorism noted but no mass rising. Dardanelles closure strangled Russian trade.

## A total and global war (Winter)

[[winter-cambridge-history-first-world-war-v1|Jay Winter's *Cambridge History*]] reframes the
conflict as the **first [[total-war|total war]]** and a genuinely **global** event — "no
region of the planet, or nearly none, was spared." Beyond the European fronts it drew in the
colonial empires on a vast scale. [[sources/strachan-first-world-war-to-arms-2001|Hew Strachan's *The First World War, Vol. I: To Arms*]] (ingested 2026-06-24) supplies the detailed operational and planning layer for 1914 itself: critique of the Schlieffen Plan myth, Battle of the Frontiers and Marne, Eastern Front mobility (Tannenberg/Galicia), naval and colonial opening moves, German global strategy, and the economic/industrial transition away from the short-war illusion.

## Causal Analysis

The causes are genuinely contested — see **[[causes-of-the-first-world-war]]**. In brief, Joll's layered
account combines the **immediate** (the July Crisis and mobilization timetables), the **structural** (the
alliance system, the arms race, imperial and economic rivalry, the primacy of domestic politics in the
Fischer thesis), and the **ideological** (the nationalist, social-Darwinist "mood of 1914").

### Social and ideological dimensions of the 1914 outbreak (Strachan, Range 02)

Hew Strachan's Chapter 2 shows that popular enthusiasm did not cause the war but was essential to its acceptance. 'Popular enthusiasm might not cause war, but it certainly needed to condone it.' The response was strongest in capitals among students, white-collar workers, and intellectuals; rural areas and peasants more often showed calm, resignation, or quiet acceptance. Crowds frequently gathered for news rather than out of prior bellicosity; the press and public formed a symbiotic relationship that amplified the image of enthusiasm.

A powerful sense of fraternity temporarily erased internal divisions. In Vienna, Stefan Zweig described 'a rushing feeling of fraternity. Strangers spoke to one another in the streets... his person, his hitherto unnoticed person, had been given meaning.' In a French village Louis Barthas recorded brothers reconciled and neighbours becoming 'the best of friends'; 'the first effect of the war was, paradoxically, to bring peace.' Hatred of the enemy was initially muted; 'the general feeling in Paris was that the Germans were mad, not bad.'

The 'Ideas of 1914' framed the war as epochal rupture and moral test. Friedrich Meinecke called 1 August 'a new historical epoch for the world' and 3 August 'one of the most beautiful moments of my life.' Intellectuals and artists welcomed war as personal liberation and social regeneration: 'it is the good war that hallows every cause' (Nietzsche); recruits were 'as swimmers with cleanness leaping, glad from a world grown old and cold and weary' (Rupert Brooke). War would purge bourgeois decadence and 'sweep out our salons.' Avant-garde movements had already glorified strife and the machine as liberating.

The Second International collapsed. Despite growth and pacifist rhetoric, socialist parties proved unable to prevent war. The SPD initially demonstrated against an Austrian war of aggression but by 30 July accepted defensive war against Russia; on 4 August it voted as a bloc for war credits (78–14 internally). The Kaiser declared 'I no longer recognize parties; I recognize only Germans.' In France the assassination of Jean Jaurès (31 July) and the CGT's pivot produced the **union sacrée**: all 98 socialist deputies supported credits; Jouhaux affirmed the justice of the French cause before Jaurès's coffin. Poincaré formalized what was already a fait accompli. In Britain Labour offered little resistance once war was declared.

These responses were not uniform. In Austria-Hungary Viennese exuberance ebbed as the conflict widened to Russia. Russian peasants worried primarily about harvest and family provision. Britain lacked an equivalent slogan to union sacrée or Burgfrieden; acceptance rested on parliamentary process and defence of international law (Belgian neutrality). Prior military service and youth movements (Jungdeutschlandbund, Boy Scouts, gymnastics societies) prepared many for duty. The common denominator across belligerents was 'passive acceptance, a willingness to do one’s duty; enthusiasm was the conspicuous froth.'

`contributed_to:` the domestic truces (union sacrée / Burgfrieden) that sustained integration far longer than statesmen expected. `produced:` the cultural and ideological cement for the 'mood of 1914' later labelled the Ideas of 1914. See [[concepts/ideas-of-1914]] and [[concepts/union-sacree]].

### Naval and global dimensions of 1914 (Strachan, Range 07)

Hew Strachan's Chapters 5–6 establish that the war was global and maritime from its first weeks. British pre-war naval policy concentrated the fleet in home waters against Germany while relying on the Anglo-Japanese alliance and Dominion contributions for oceanic security. The result left the Pacific and trade routes vulnerable to a determined German cruiser force.

**Distant blockade and North Sea opening.** The Royal Navy abandoned close blockade plans (submarine, mine, and torpedo threats; Heligoland as forward base rejected) in favour of distant blockade exploiting Britain’s geographic position. Planning was hampered by personality conflicts (Fisher–Beresford feud), frequent leadership changes, and the absence of a proper staff. The cabinet initially conceived a naval war complementary to any continental commitment.

The first major action was the Heligoland Bight (28 August 1914). Keyes and Tyrwhitt’s Harwich Force raided German patrols; poor coordination with Beatty’s battle cruisers and Goodenough’s light cruisers produced confusion (Keyes mistook friend for foe; wireless lost on *Arethusa*). Beatty’s intervention turned the tide: three German light cruisers (*Mainz*, *Köln*, *Ariadne*) and one torpedo boat sunk with no British losses. The victory was real but masked command failures and gunnery/shell defects (many British shells failed to explode or penetrate). Neither side’s submarines achieved hoped-for results. `produced:` moral superiority at sea; `contributed_to:` German caution and later raids.

German battle-cruiser raids followed. The 3 November Yarmouth raid (minelaying cover) met little response. On 16 December Hipper bombarded Scarborough and Hartlepool — the first successful attack on English shores since 1667 — while Ingenohl took the High Seas Fleet out in support (defying Kaiser’s restrictive orders). British intelligence (Room 40) gave warning but the trap failed through incomplete picture (assumed no battleships), patchy reporting, ambiguous signals (“Light cruisers—resume your position”), worsening weather, and command hesitation. Hipper escaped; British civilian casualties 122 killed, 443 wounded. The official historian condemned two efficient squadrons failing to bring a conforming enemy to action despite contact. `contributed_to:` Jellicoe tightening control; Beatty’s battle cruisers moved to Rosyth.

**Cruiser warfare and von Spee’s cruise.** Germany prepared for commerce raiding with a global wireless network (Nauen to Yap etc.), Etappen supply, auxiliary cruisers, and the East Asiatic Squadron (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau + lights) at Tsingtao — the only major overseas base. Cruiser warfare was valued as a force-multiplier for the inferior power: one warship in the Pacific could draw many British vessels; raising insurance and freight rates could disrupt trade and morale more effectively than tonnage sunk. Von Spee kept the squadron concentrated and at sea to preserve mobility. At Pagan Island (12 August) *Emden* (Müller) was detached for classical raiding; the main body headed southeast to South America for coal and space.

British China station commander Jerram lost track amid conflicting orders. *Emden* achieved major success in the Indian Ocean. On 1 November von Spee defeated Cradock at Coronel; the news forced the Admiralty to dispatch three battle cruisers from home waters, temporarily reducing the North Sea margin. On 8 December Sturdee’s battle cruisers destroyed von Spee’s squadron at the Falklands. The German surface threat in the outer oceans was eliminated. `produced:` Japanese and Dominion colonial operations proceeded without interference; `contributed_to:` the global character of the war from August 1914.

**Japan’s entry and Pacific seizures.** Britain initially sought Japanese neutrality, then (6 August) limited naval aid against armed merchantmen. Kato Takaaki used the opening to eliminate German influence in China. Japan issued its own ultimatum (15 August, no reference to Britain) and declared war 23 August. Landings at Lungkow and Laoshan Bay (2 September) violated Chinese neutrality; Yuan Shih-kai was powerless. The siege of Tsingtao (slow, methodical Japanese artillery and consolidation; air support) ended 7 November. Parallel operations: New Zealand occupied Samoa (30 August without fighting); Australia took German New Guinea (Rabaul/Bitapaka 11–15 September). Japan occupied Micronesia (Marshalls, Carolines) by mid-October for future bases and phosphates, subverting civilian restrictions; the equator became the de facto division. `produced:` removal of German naval presence in East Asia; Japanese expansion in China and the Pacific.

`part_of:` the genuinely global opening of [[first-world-war-1914]]. `contributed_to:` British ability to concentrate on Europe while periphery was cleared; German global strategy (jihad, colonies) left without naval support. See new dedicated pages [[events/heligoland-bight-action-1914]], [[events/scarborough-raid-1914]], [[events/cruise-of-von-spee-squadron-1914]], [[events/japan-enters-first-world-war-1914]], [[events/siege-of-tsingtao-1914]], and [[actors/von-spee-maximilian]].

### 1914 origins per Strachan (Range 01)

Hew Strachan's account stresses contingency, militarization of diplomacy through arms races and war plans, fatalism rather than master plan, and the interaction of domestic structures with immediate decisions. Germany did not go to war "in pursuit of its Weltpolitik" but the conduct of Weltpolitik (and its setbacks) "contributed to its sense of humiliation, beleaguerment, and fatalism in 1914" (Ch. 1, Germany as a World Power). The "blank cheque" of 5–6 July was "genuinely blank": "the council had made little effort to discuss the implications of what it was doing" (Ch. 1, The July Crisis). Moltke's advocacy of preventive war ("we must wage a preventive war to conquer our opponents as long as we still have a reasonable chance") reflected social-Darwinist fatalism more than calculated domestic crisis-resolution (Ch. 1). Russian partial-then-general mobilization decisions were driven by technical military realities (partial mobilization "a nonsense" for railways and corps areas) and alliance pressures as much as aggression. Britain's entry hinged on Belgian neutrality as the bridge that allowed Liberals to unite around defence of small nations plus Entente commitments. Individuals mattered: "the wrong turn of Archduke Franz Ferdinand’s driver, and the fortuitous positioning of Princip"; Conrad's personal motivations intertwined with policy. Long-range factors (alliances, imperialism) shaped postures but "the July crisis cannot stand in isolation" from prior crises; no single power followed a consistent grand design (Ch. 1, The July Crisis, closing analysis). `caused_by:` [[july-crisis-1914]]; `contributed_to:` rigid mobilization timetables and alliance obligations; `preceded_by:` Moroccan crises and Bosnian annexation 1908 (humiliation of Russia, confirmation of Austrian dependence on Germany).

### French High Command Evolution, "Healing," Limited Offensives, and 1918 Defensive Response (Doughty, Range 6)

From [[sources/doughty-pyrrhic-victory-2005]] Ch. 8–9 exclusively. Post-Nivelle failure and 1917 mutinies (54 divisions, Apr–Jun), Pétain (GS chief 29 Apr; C-in-C 17 May) imposed "strategy of healing" + limited offensives. Directive No. 1 (19 May): equilibrium precluded breakthrough; mass arty on forward enemy positions; short infantry advances + consolidate (no successive/continuous battle). "A breach can be envisaged only when successful pushes or attrition have created intervals..." Rearm (aircraft, 155mm, FT-17 tanks); centers of resistance. Manpower forced conservation (20–25 divs to suppress). Malmaison (23–26 Oct, Sixth/Maistre): major limited success (6km, Chemin des Dames heights, 11k POW for ~11.8k cas — <1/10 Nivelle cost). Russia collapse -> defensive priority (23 Oct to Haig: 40+ div reserves first). (Ch. 8)

New doctrine (Dec 1917): position of resistance (bulk rear), islands/centers forward, active reserves for counterattack; "economize our forces"; tenacious but terrain-yielding if needed. Intelligence warned accurately of 1918 German offensive. Michael (21 Mar): reserves (Third/First + Fayolle) rushed; Fayolle mixed command; Pétain prioritized Paris vs contact (controversial). Doullens (26 Mar): Foch coordinator; Beauvais (3 Apr): strategic direction. Lys (9 Apr): northern French moves. Blücher (27 May): Duchêne violated doctrine (crest resistance); Germans broke to Marne (55km). June 9: reserves early; counter halted. Modest French cas due to husbanding. Foch-Pétain friction over reserves/priorities; Clemenceau enforced Foch supremacy. (Ch. 9)

`produced:` army restoration + Malmaison model + doctrine that absorbed 1918 blows. `contributed_to:` conditions for Allied 1918 offensive. `caused_by:` mutinies + means shortage + Eastern collapse. `preceded_by:` Nivelle offensive failure.

### Economic and Industrial Transition, 1914 (Strachan Range 10: Financing the War + Industrial Mobilization)

The opening campaigns exposed the collapse of pre-war assumptions about both finance and munitions. The "short-war illusion" (widely held expectation of 3–9 months' fighting, rooted in 1866/1870 precedents and financial orthodoxy) was not the primary driver of the shell crises. "The shells crisis of the autumn of 1914 was emphatically not a manifestation of a belief that the war would be short." Consumption rates in positional fighting (Marne resistance, Ypres, Galicia) vastly exceeded every pre-war model for quick-firing artillery, regardless of length assumptions. France (most exposed by Marne) hit first: 75 mm reserves fell from 530k to 33k rounds by 10 Sep; Joffre warned rear "exhausted... impossible to continue fighting for lack of munitions within fifteen days." Germany, Britain, and Russia followed within weeks (batteries limited to 5 rounds/day or 9 shells/gun; Russian corps ~7 days at assumed rates). Pre-war stocks (France ~1,400 rds/75 mm gun; Germany 750–987 effective; Britain 1,500; Russia target 1,000–1,500) had been doubled from earlier estimates precisely because of Manchuria/Boer War lessons, yet proved inadequate. "The immediate causes of shell shortage lay in the fact that the manner of fighting was so different from pre-war expectation." Locator (all): SHELL SHORTAGE.

Financially, the gold standard (international by July 1914 with 59 adherents) proved incompatible with war needs. Britain alone stayed effectively on it (Empire gold inflows, US coordination, Treasury notes for domestic use); reserves actually rose 109% over the war. Germany suspended convertibility 4 Aug and used Reichsbank + Darlehenskassen (1.5bn+ marks) for liquidity and small change; Austria-Hungary (already weakened by 1912 Bosnian liquidity drain) suspended its Bank act and extended moratoriums to 1916; France imposed sweeping, long-lasting moratoriums that "carried an immense burden for the rest of the war"; Russia suspended specie payments but held gold internally. All saw explosive note issue and treasury-bill financing. "The process of German mobilization had worked a more fundamental revolution in the mechanism of German credit than was realized at the time." Taxation lagged; borrowing (domestic loans + Entente foreign credit via London/US) dominated. Central Powers ultimately funded via disinvestment ("falling real incomes and declining output"); Entente accessed US markets at the price of gold shipments and later coordination. "The Entente’s readier access to foreign credits is crucial to explaining the proposition that the war cost twice as much to win as to lose."

Immediate adaptations: Germany created the Kriegsrohstoffsamt (KRA) 13 Aug 1914 under Walther Rathenau for central raw-materials appropriation/allocation after blockade fears (copper etc.); "the story of the establishment of the KRA is bewitching in its suddenness." France divided into regions, set 100k shells/day target (Bordeaux 20 Sep), returned skilled labour, expanded furnaces in unoccupied zones (despite losing NE industrial heartland: 74% coal, 81% pig iron, 63% steel). Russia formed War Industries Committees (fragmented); Britain moved toward dedicated Ministry of Munitions. By 1916 output had transformed (Russian 3-inch shell monthly 15x to 1.618M; similar surges elsewhere), but heavy artillery, small arms, and specific calibres remained bottlenecks, and transport/logistics often limited front-line effect. The short-war illusion persisted in popular and policy thinking as "a series of short episodes" well into 1916. `contributed_to:` the shift from 1914 mobile campaigns to sustained industrial attrition; `produced:` [[processes/war-financing-wwi]] and [[processes/industrial-mobilization-wwi]]; `enabled:` prolonged two-front war for Germany and global Entente purchasing power. See dedicated concept [[concepts/short-war-illusion]] and Range 10 Extraction on source page.

### Eastern Front 1914: War Plans, East Prussia, and Tannenberg (Strachan, Range 05)

Hew Strachan’s Chapter 4 details the Eastern Front in 1914 as a theatre of illusions on both sides and of rapid German operational success rooted in terrain, interior lines, and Russian command disintegration. Austro-Hungarian planning and German co-operation were fundamentally misaligned: “Each had formed a mental picture of the other which conformed to its own wishes rather than to the other’s intentions.” Conrad expected German support and a joint envelopment (“super-Cannae”) in Poland; Moltke on 10 February 1913 told him “the fate of Austria will not be decided on the Bug, but definitely on the Seine.” By 1 August the truth was clear: “a German attack in East Prussia was most unlikely, there would be no offensive in Poland, and thus Austria-Hungary was the mainstay of the Triple Alliance in the east.” The Austro-Hungarian army was qualitatively inferior (training 0.29% of population vs Germany’s 0.47%; most under-gunned in Europe; ethnic/language frictions; 2nd Army rail chaos from partial Serbian mobilization). Its Galician deployment (pulled west after Redl treason fears) forfeited the chance to meet Russians piecemeal.

Russian planning (Plan 19 and variants) was compromised between Case A (Alekseev: main effort vs Austria-Hungary) and Case G (Danilov: vs Germany). French pressure forced offensive commitment from the outset despite defensive deployment logic and inadequate railways. Stavka added 9th and 10th Armies for a Polish thrust, dissipating superiority. “Russia had a mobilization plan focused on Austria-Hungary, but a grand strategy directed towards Germany.” Command structure divorced supply from operations; Stavka at Baranovitchi exercised little control; Fronts became autonomous.

In East Prussia (secondary theatre for both sides), geography (Masurian lakes, Insterburg gap, Königsberg) favoured German defensive-offensive on interior lines using the Insterburg–Allenstein–Osterode rail/road net. Russian 1st Army (Rennenkampf) and 2nd Army (Samsonov) held numerical superiority (30 inf + 8 cav divs vs German 11–13 divs) but were dispersed, uncoordinated, and logistically crippled. Samsonov’s 2nd Army lacked lateral communications, advanced on unmetalled roads after 10–12 hour marches, sent most messages in clear, had no advance guard, and operated on false assumptions (Germans fleeing to Vistula; Rennenkampf still advancing). Communications collapsed; cavalry reconnaissance failed.

At Gumbinnen (20 August) François’ I Corps forced an unplanned battle east of the planned Angerapp position. German attacks were hurried and uncoordinated; XVII Corps (Mackensen) suffered ~8,000 casualties out of 30,000. Russians showed effective firepower from entrenched positions with telephone-linked artillery. Prittwitz ordered retreat to the Vistula. Hoffmann’s immediate counter-proposal (rail I Corps to XX Corps’ right against Samsonov’s left; XVII and I Reserve to support on the right) “lay the manoeuvre which enabled the victory at Tannenberg.” Hoffmann noted the battle developed “entirely by itself, not as a result of a master plan.” Rennenkampf did not pursue (consistent with the May 1914 war game warning). By 23 August the Germans facing the 1st Army were gone except one cavalry division.

Hindenburg and Ludendorff (appointed 22 August) executed the switch. Ludendorff confirmed moves and directed the eastern corps south-west once Rennenkampf’s passivity was clear; Hindenburg steadied his chief of staff. On 23–24 August Samsonov’s XV Corps contacted Scholtz’s XX Corps at Orlau-Frankenau. “A fierce frontal battle developed on 24 August, with Hindenburg and Ludendorff telling Scholtz to hold well...” The 8th Army achieved local superiority against the isolated and exhausted 2nd Army while the 1st remained inert. Russian 2nd Army strength had fallen to just over three corps on a 96 km front amid supply and command collapse.

`produced:` the conditions for the Tannenberg victory (encirclement of Samsonov’s army). `contributed_to:` early German success and prestige on the Eastern Front; destruction of one Russian field army; contrast with Western Front deadlock. `contrasts_with:` Russian advantages in manpower and the defensive potential of depth were nullified by planning compromises, C2 failures, and logistics. See dedicated [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/battle-of-tannenberg-1914]] (full tactical/operational analysis from this range only) and [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/first-world-war]].

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the collapse of **four empires** (Habsburg, Romanov, Ottoman, Hohenzollern); the
  **Versailles settlement** and the redrawn map of Europe and the Middle East; the **Russian Revolution**
  and the first communist state.
- `enabled:` the rise of [[communism-and-fascism|communism and fascism]], the grievances exploited by Nazi
  Germany, and thus the path to the [[second-world-war-1939|Second World War]]; the acceleration of
  [[decolonization|anti-colonial]] nationalism and the discrediting of Europe's "civilizing mission."
- `produced:` a continent in mourning — at least **2 million widows** and ~**6 million orphans** — and a
  new culture of **[[war-memory-and-mourning|commemoration]]** (the Tombs of the Unknown Soldier, Armistice
  Day, the village war memorial) that shaped how the twentieth century remembered war (Winter, Vol. III).

## Historiography

The dominant scholarly energy has gone into **origins** (see [[causes-of-the-first-world-war]]) — from the
war-guilt clause of Versailles through the **Fischer controversy** (German responsibility) to recent
"shared-responsibility" and contingency accounts (Clark's *Sleepwalkers*). The war's conduct and meaning
are also contested: the "lions led by donkeys" critique of the generals versus revisionist "learning
curve" accounts of the British army.

Military effectiveness is analyzed comparatively in Millett and Murray, *Military Effectiveness*, Vol. 1 (new ed. 2010) via a 4-level framework (political, strategic, operational, tactical). Key insight: excellence at lower levels does not redeem higher-level failures (German tactical brilliance produced more total defeats due to strategic myopia). Editors' 2010 introduction stresses adaptation/innovation under uncertainty, cultural factors, and lessons for later conflicts (Iraq 2003: conventional success followed by failure to recognize/adapt to insurgency; "déjà vu" with Vietnam; Mattis on studying history). Provides an essential analytical baseline beyond narrative/operational accounts.

## Colonial and African Theatres (Strachan, Range 08)

The war was global from its first hours. "As much from its outset as beyond its formal conclusion, therefore, the First World War was far more than just a European conflict." In August 1914 belligerence "embraced the entire continent of Africa with the exception of Liberia, Ethiopia..." "in 1914 the flow of major war was the reverse of that in the eighteenth century—from Europe to the colonies, rather than vice versa."

**Togoland (Aug 1914):** First British shot fired 12 Aug by Regimental Sergeant-Major Alhaji Grunshi (West African Frontier Force). Improvised Gold Coast Regiment under Capt. F.C. Bryant seized Lome then Kamina wireless (principal strategic objective); Germans destroyed station and surrendered 25 Aug. "its principal strategic objective was accomplished in its first month."

**Cameroons (1914–1916):** Germans planned interior defence (Ngaundere plateau) to preserve colony for peace conference claims. "colonization mattered as an end in itself." Epic porterage crisis ("two and three porters for each soldier"); French/British discoordination (Brazzaville/Duala conferences produced plans never executed on ground). Jaunde fell 1 Jan 1916; Germans evacuated ~15,000 (incl. 6,000 askaris) into Spanish Muni before Allied arrival; Mora surrendered 18 Feb. "The failure of the Campo and Gabon columns to cut off the German retreat..." Provisional partition followed diplomacy more than conquest.

**South-West Africa (1914–1915):** Union politics produced Afrikaner rebellion (Maritz, De Wet, Beyers, Kemp). Botha led invasion using Boer mobility/envelopment. "maximum of movement and a minimum of casualties." Germans ~5,000; surrendered 9 July 1915 at Otavi/Tsumeb area without climactic battle (4,740 men). "By biding his time in 1915, Botha laid the foundations for South Africa’s own brand of colonialism in 1918."

**East Africa (1914–1918):** Longest theatre. Tanga (2–5 Nov 1914) major British defeat (Aitken re-embarked after left disintegrated; Lettow's reserves achieved local envelopment). Königsberg sunk July 1915; Rubens/Marie delivered critical munitions. 1916 Smuts offensive (northern thrust, Belgian west, Northey SW) achieved territory but not destruction of force; "it is Africa and its climate we are really fighting not the Germans." Rains, tsetse, 135k–175k porters on Rufiji. Germans retreated south, Wahle linked from Tabora; Wintgens/Naumann raids drew thousands. Lettow crossed to Mozambique 1917–18. "On 25 November 1918... at Abercorn... Colonel Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck surrendered, the last German commander of the war to do so."

### 1918: French Strategy of Opportunism and the “Misery” of Victory (Doughty Range 7)

Grounded exclusively in [[sources/doughty-pyrrhic-victory-2005]] (Ch. 10 pp. 461–507 + Conclusion pp. 508–518 cache). See [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/first-world-war/first-world-war]] for full 1918 section with quotes/locators.

Foch (supreme via Doullens/Beauvais/Jun 26 Pétain obedience): "opportunism" ("advances could occur only when the opportunity... appeared"; "lacked any grand strategic theories but... vision, energy, and knack"). Rapid successive blows ("right fist, then his left... vigorous kick") to keep off balance. Still planned 1919 mid-Jun but seized July opportunity for 1918 decisive. Halt 5th drive (1 Jul memo); Pétain Directive No. 5 12 Jul "resumption of the offensive... simple, audacious, and rapid". Marne salient 18 Jul: Tenth (Mangin, US 1st/2nd) "greatest day since the Marne"; Foch interventions key vs Pétain pessimism; 29k captured, initiative, Foch Marshal. 24 Jul: "abandon the general defensive... pass to the offensive" successive blows. 8 Aug Amiens "black day"; converging Mézières. US tensions (Pershing refused divide). Sep offensive + Balkans (Franchet d’Espèrey) collapse forced armistice feelers. Slow Meuse-Argonne: Clemenceau "criminal" attrition, "in hand", "truth" to Wilson; Foch pressed. Oct pressure broke positions; Ludendorff "unconditional surrender" order/resign; Foch/Pétain clash (direction vs successive objectives). French fatigue + US supply ("without you... completely defeated"). Final methodical Fr pursuit; armistice 11 Nov. 

**"Misery of Victory":** 1.4M dead/missing +4M wounded; devastated NE; prices quadrupled; resolve (Clemenceau "War. Nothing but War"; soldiers duty). Arsenal (52k aircraft; 3/4 US equip). Versailles imbalance. Postwar: methodical battle (Pétain dominant; Debeney legacy); caution/forts/ally reliance. Prestige peak; confidence 1918 methods.

`produced:` initiative + German collapse + armistice. `enabled:` 1918 campaign. `contributed_to:` interwar doctrine. `caused_by:` salients/US/Foch discipline. `part_of:` WWI. `contrasts_with:` earlier phases.

"over 2 million Africans served... upwards of 200,000 of them died." Disease and supply (not battle) dominated. "the African campaigns... bore more relationship to the nineteenth-century campaigns of colonial conquest than they did to the Great War itself." Yet "relatively the impact of the war on the dark continent was as great as that on Europe."

## Why Germany lost in 1918 (Stevenson, via Evans 2015)

Evans's review of David Stevenson, *With Our Backs to the Wall* (2011), in
[[evans-third-reich-history-memory-2015]] (ch. 3), attributed to Stevenson
and generally endorsed by Evans. Spring 1918 looked winnable for Germany
(French mutinies, Caporetto, Russia's exit lifting Western Front strength
past 4 million; Operation Michael's creeping barrage broke the front on 21
March). The reversal within ~3 months rested on: Allied signals
intelligence and air superiority by mid-1918; the gas balance flipping
(Germany released 52,000 tons causing 300,000 Allied casualties, but
British masks and Livens projectors outmatched rubber-starved German
equipment); Allied tank numbers (Ludendorff in 1919 cited the prospect of
thousands of Allied tanks as a reason for the armistice); and above all
economics — American food, steel and munitions plus the blockade: German
female death rates ran ~25% above prewar, >500,000 German civilians died
of malnutrition-linked causes (the "turnip winter" of 1916–17), and troop
quality collapsed (April 1918 alone: 54,000 killed/missing, 445,000
wounded/sick; 340,000 desertions/surrenders from July). Stevenson calls
the spring offensive itself, with unrestricted submarine warfare, the
"cardinal error of the war" — Ludendorff pursued "the mirage of total
victory." Evans goes beyond Stevenson in reading Ludendorff not as a
technician but as "a highly political general" with proto-fascist
politics, and flags Stevenson's thin treatment of the medical dimension.
The *Dolchstoßlegende* — Hitler's Pasewalk "revelation" that Jewish
revolutionaries had stabbed the army in the back — is, on this account,
pure post-hoc mythologization of a materially overdetermined defeat.

## The German "Silent Dictatorship" (Evans 2003)

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) on the German home
front/command: from 1916 [[hindenburg-paul-von|Hindenburg]] and
[[ludendorff|Ludendorff]] — not a civilian politician, unlike Clemenceau or
Lloyd George — took over the direction of the war, a "silent dictatorship":
the Hindenburg Programme (initially run by Wilhelm Groener, who co-opted
unions and civilian politicians before being pushed out) imposed central
economic control, severe civil-liberties curbs, and generals dictating war
aims. Evans: this "provide[d] significant precedents for the more drastic
fate that overtook German democracy... less than two decades later"
(contributed_to [[weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]]). Hindenburg's
"virtually invincible" Eastern Front reputation masked, per Evans, "a man of
limited political vision and ability" fronting for the more radical
Ludendorff. Systematic economic exploitation of occupied Belgium, France, and
East-Central Europe contributed_to postwar Allied punitiveness, and
Brest-Litovsk supplied the template Germans expected in reverse — undercutting
later claims that [[treaty-of-versailles-1919|Versailles]] was uniquely harsh.
The concealment of looming defeat until late (the Kaiser told only at the end
of September 1918) created the plausibility gap the
[[stab-in-the-back-myth|stab-in-the-back myth]] filled.

## Griffin (2007): 1914 as "The Beginning of a Beginning"

*Modernism and Fascism* (chs. 5–7) treats August 1914 as the moment social modernism went to war: Stromberg's "almost manic bellicosity of the European intellectuals"; Thomas Mann's "the beginning of much that was still in the process of beginning"; Barrès declaring 3 August "a sacred day"; Papini's *Lacerba* greeting the war as "a Malthusian operation"; Marinetti's "most beautiful Futurist poem"; Stadler's *Der Aufbruch* (its author killed at Ypres, October 1914); in Germany, the "culture vs civilization" framing Jung called a "psychic epidemic." Combat itself is read through rite-of-passage theory (Leed on No Man's Land; Emilio Gentile: "with the baptism of fire occurred the metanoia of the old man into the fighter or the new man"; Koenigsberg's sacrificial body-politic fantasy). For Italy, Caporetto (Oct–Nov 1917) is the hinge: the threatened death of the mythic fatherland transposed the nation "onto an absolute plane," feeding Mussolini's "Trenchocracy" and the postwar Two-Italies myth amid the Paris Peace disillusion and the influenza pandemic (20–40 million dead) that made Spengler a bestseller. Jünger's alchemy ("everything that has taken shape hitherto is melted down in a thousand furnaces to be cast into new values") and *Der Arbeiter*'s homo faber close the arc into interwar political modernism. (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
