# Treaty of Versailles (1919)

Peace treaty signed 28 June 1919 formally ending WWI between the Allies and Germany. Imposed significant territorial losses (Alsace-Lorraine, Polish Corridor, colonies), severe military limits, and massive reparations. The "war guilt" clause (Article 231) and economic burden became potent symbols in German politics. Established the League of Nations (though the U.S. Senate refused to ratify). Seen by many contemporaries and later historians as a flawed settlement that sowed seeds for the next war.

One contemporary economic reading ([[flynn-as-we-go-marching-1944|Flynn 1944]],
attributed): the disarmament clauses banned what had been "not merely Germany's
greatest industry but the industry that was the medium for government
spending," leaving the [[weimar-republic|republic]] the empire's fiscal
pressures without its historic employment-and-spending mechanism.

Payne's figures ([[payne-history-of-fascism-1995]]): Germany lost 13 percent
of territory and 12 percent of population (14.3 percent of arable land, 15
percent of productive capacity); army capped at 100,000, navy at 25,000, air
force abolished; the Rhineland demilitarized 50 km east. In his retrodictive
theory of fascism, the resulting status deprivation is one precondition —
but only one — of the "fascistogenic situation."

## Evans (2003): Terms in Detail and the Perception Mechanism

Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]) itemizes the settlement: 10% of
population and 13% of territory lost (Alsace-Lorraine; Eupen-Malmédy; the Saar
under League mandate; Northern Schleswig; Memel; Posen/West Prussia/Upper
Silesia to Poland; Danzig a Free City; the Polish Corridor cutting off East
Prussia); Rhineland occupied through most of the 1920s; colonies mandated
away; army capped at 100,000 with no tanks, heavy artillery, air force, or
conscription; 6 million rifles, 15,000+ aircraft, and 130,000+ machine guns
destroyed; interim seizures of 2M+ tons of shipping, 5,000 locomotives,
136,000 rail coaches, 24 million tons of coal.

His assessment (attributed, not wiki-voice): the territorial terms were
**mild** compared with what Germany would have imposed had it won — the
September 1914 Bethmann Hollweg war-aims program and the actual precedent of
Brest-Litovsk against Russia; reparations were "not beyond the country's
resources to meet" and "not unreasonable" given the destruction in Belgium and
France. What drove the political consequences was **the perception of
injustice, not objective severity**. Two Evans-flagged causal legacies:

- The Leipzig war-crimes fiasco (7 convictions from 900 accused) convinced
  German opinion that the laws of war were Allied propaganda — "a fateful
  legacy for the attitudes and conduct of German armed forces during the
  Second World War" (contributed_to).
- The Anschluss ban was the provision least defensible by the Allies' own
  self-determination principles — German-Austrians (including Austrian
  socialists) were overwhelmingly pro-union; cross-link [[anschluss-1938]].
- French occupation conduct in the Rhineland (banned patriotic songs,
  sponsored separatism) manufactured nationalists: a former pacifist SPD
  member — "I came to feel the rifle butt of the French and became patriotic
  again."

## Historiography

The classic indictment is Keynes's *Economic Consequences of the Peace*
(1919); later scholarship (Marks, MacMillan) has partially rehabilitated the
settlement as harsh-but-workable, blaming enforcement failure rather than
the terms themselves — the treaty was "too harsh to reconcile Germany, too
soft to keep it down" (see [[causes-of-the-second-world-war]]). Flynn (1944)
supplies the contemporary economic-structural reading above; Payne (1995)
treats the resulting status deprivation as one precondition among many in
his retrodictive theory, explicitly rejecting Versailles-determinism (the
"concatenation of crises" argument at [[nazi-seizure-of-power-1933]]).
