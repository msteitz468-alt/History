# Sixteenth Amendment (1913)

The Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, authorizes Congress to "lay and collect taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment among the several States, and without regard to any census or enumeration." It overturned the 1895 Supreme Court decision in *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co.* that had treated a federal income tax as a "direct tax" requiring apportionment by population (effectively impossible for an income tax).

## Narrative

After *Pollock*, a twenty-year campaign by Populists, agrarians, and progressive reformers sought a constitutional amendment. The amendment passed Congress in 1909 and was ratified by the required number of states in 1913. Southern and Western states were crucial; some Northeastern industrial states resisted or failed to ratify. The first modern federal income tax followed shortly thereafter.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Pollock decision and the structural inability of the pre-amendment Constitution to sustain a federal income tax without apportionment.

**contributed_to:** Populist mobilization against concentrated wealth and the tariff system's regressive character; the political realignment that brought progressive Republicans and Democrats into power.

**preceded_by:** The failed 1894 income tax law struck down in Pollock; decades of state-level experiments and agrarian tax-reform agitation.

## Consequence Analysis

The amendment made possible the revenue system that financed the modern American state (World War I, New Deal, World War II, Cold War). It also set the terms for a century of conflict over who pays, how much, and with what exemptions. Williamson notes that the same direct-tax language the framers used partly to protect slavery later blocked progressive taxation until the amendment.

## Actors

- Populist and agrarian reformers (key drivers of the amendment campaign)
- Progressive-era Congress and state legislatures
- The Supreme Court (Pollock majority and later reinterpretations)

## Historiography

The amendment is usually treated as a straightforward progressive victory. Williamson (2025) situates it within the longer arc of tax fights as democracy fights: the Gilded Age Court had used the founders' fiscal compromises to block taxation of concentrated wealth; the amendment partially restored popular fiscal power. Southern ratification was often tactical (white supremacist regimes confident Black voting rights were dead) rather than a full endorsement of strong-state democracy. The amendment's text says nothing about wealth taxes, leaving that question for later constitutional interpretation.
