# The Second World War (1939–1945)

The **Second World War** (1939–1945) was the largest and deadliest conflict in human history — some **60
million dead**, the majority of them civilians — and the hinge of the twentieth century. Documented for
the wiki through **[[beevor-second-world-war|Antony Beevor's *The Second World War*]]** (narrative) and
**[[taylor-origins-second-world-war|A. J. P. Taylor's *Origins*]]** (causation), it ended the
[[world-wars-era|World Wars Era]] and opened the [[cold-war|Cold War]].

## Narrative

Conventionally dated from Germany's invasion of **Poland (1 September 1939)** — though the Sino-Japanese
war had raged since 1937 and Beevor links the war's outbreak to the 1939 Soviet-Japanese clash at
**Khalkhin Gol** — the war passed through the **fall of France (1940)**, the **Battle of Britain**, and
then its decisive phase on the **Eastern Front** after **Operation Barbarossa (June 1941)**: Stalingrad
(1942–43) and Kursk (1943) broke the Wehrmacht in the theatre where the war was overwhelmingly decided.
**Pearl Harbor (December 1941)** brought the United States and the Pacific war; the Mediterranean, the
strategic-bombing campaign, and the **Normandy landings (1944)** converged with the Soviet advance on
Germany, which surrendered in **May 1945**. The **atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki** and Soviet
entry forced Japan's surrender in **September 1945**.

## Causal Analysis

Contested — see **[[causes-of-the-second-world-war]]**. The mainstream view roots the war in the
**unresolved legacies of 1914–18** (Versailles, reparations, the German grievance), the **Great
Depression**, the **failure of collective security** (appeasement, the League's collapse), and above all
the **expansionist ideologies** of Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan. A. J. P. Taylor's revisionist
provocation — that Hitler was an opportunist improviser rather than the executor of a blueprint — is the
classic dissent.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Holocaust** and the era's defining [[genocide]]; the defeat of fascism; the
  **[[1945-postwar-order|postwar order]]**, the United Nations, and the Bretton Woods system.
- `produced:` the **[[cold-war|Cold War]]** and the bipolar US–Soviet world; the **nuclear age**.
- `enabled:` accelerated **[[decolonization]]** as the European empires were fatally weakened.

## Historiography

Debate concentrates on **origins** (see [[causes-of-the-second-world-war]]), on the **Holocaust**
(intentionalist vs. functionalist accounts of the path to genocide), on the **morality of strategic
bombing and the atomic bombs**, and on the relative weight of the Eastern Front, long underplayed in
Western narratives that Beevor and others have corrected.

## The war as coalition and total war (CHSWW)

The [[cambridge-history-second-world-war-v1|Cambridge History of the Second World War]] frames the
conflict as, above all, a **coalition war decided by the matching of strategy to resources** across
global theatres (Vol. I, *Fighting the War*) and as the supreme **[[total-war]]** that "overwhelms
and eats up society" (Vol. III, *Total War: Economy, Society and Culture*, Geyer & Tooze). Two
strands the wiki now develops from it: the **[[strategic-bombing-campaign|strategic bombing
campaign]]** (the air war against the enemy's economy and cities) and the **[[war-economy|war
economy]]** — where the Allies' overwhelming material preponderance, mobilised through the
[[total-war]] state, ground down the resource-poor Axis. Its catastrophic core, the
**[[the-holocaust|Holocaust]]**, is treated as both genocide and the extreme of the war's
"destruction through labour" and displacement. (The series' Vol. II, *Politics and Ideology*, is
absent from the collection.)

## Fateful choices and problem-solvers (Kershaw and Kennedy, via Evans 2015)

From Evans's reviews (chs. 18–19 of
[[evans-third-reich-history-memory-2015]], all attributed):

**Kershaw, *Fateful Choices* (2007)** — ten interconnected decisions of
1940–41 made "under preconditions and under external constraints," not in
a vacuum. Britain's May 1940 cabinet crisis: Halifax pushed for mediated
peace via Mussolini; Churchill overrode him ("We should get no worse
terms if we went on fighting, even if we were beaten"). Kershaw's careful
counterfactual (Evans endorses the method): had Halifax prevailed, the
plausible puppet premier was Lloyd George — not Mosley — possibly under a
restored Edward VIII, a British Vichy; and the Cowling/Charmley claim
that a 1940 separate peace would have saved the Empire fails because
Hitler would soon have dismembered it anyway, while the USSR "was the
decisive force in the defeat of Germany" regardless. Mussolini's entry
and the "fatal decision" to invade Greece drew Germany into an unwanted
theatre; Roosevelt's stepwise undeclared Atlantic war (Lend-Lease,
Atlantic Charter, convoy protection) forced Hitler's hand after Pearl
Harbor.

**Kennedy, *Engineers of Victory* (2013)** — Allied victory as
middle-level problem-solving (centimetric radar, Hedgehog, escort
carriers; the P-51; defense-in-depth; amphibious craft; Pacific carrier
logistics), skeptical of intelligence-history triumphalism. Evans's
critique (attributed): Allied productive superiority predated 1943
(21,000 Soviet + 15,000 British combat aircraft vs. 10,000 German in
1940); Germany had its own innovation culture and lost anyway; and
treating WWII "as a rational conflict along the lines of the Seven Years
War" misses that "defeat was pre-programmed for the Axis by the very
nature of its war aims" — unlimited racial-imperial objectives including
the murder of the Jews as "a paramount war aim." He also flags Kennedy's
outdated Kursk figures against Frieser's revisions.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer's narrative closes with the German surrender sequence and his own postwar reckoning, distinctive as first-person testimony from a correspondent who covered the regime from 1934. Sequence of German surrenders: Italy unconditionally on 29 April 1945 (news never reached Hitler, communications having broken down); northwest Germany, Denmark, and Holland to Montgomery on 4 May; Army Group G (1st and 19th Armies) on 5 May. Admiral Hans von Friedeburg reached Eisenhower's Reims HQ on 5 May to negotiate; Jodl arrived 6 May to stall for time, moving German troops and refugees west away from the Soviets — Eisenhower threatened to close the entire Allied front to German refugees unless the delay ceased. Dönitz radioed full surrender authority to Jodl at 1:30 a.m. on 7 May. Germany signed unconditional surrender at Reims at 2:41 a.m., 7 May 1945, for the Allies by Gen. Walter Bedell Smith, witnessed by Gen. Ivan Susloparov (USSR) and Gen. François Sevez (France), and for Germany by Friedeburg and Jodl. Jodl's closing statement — "With this signature the German people and the German Armed Forces are, for better or worse, delivered into the hands of the victors... I can only express the hope that the victor will treat them with generosity" — met with no response from the Allied side; Shirer draws the parallel to the French general's similarly futile plea at the 1940 Compiègne surrender. Fighting ceased at midnight, 8–9 May 1945 (Shirer 1960).

[SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer's summary framing of total losses: "millions of men and women had been slaughtered on a hundred battlefields and in a thousand bombed towns, and millions more done to death in the Nazi gas chambers or on the edge of the S.S. Einsatzgruppen pits in Russia and Poland." He contrasts 1945 with 1918: in 1918 "the Kaiser had fled, the monarchy had tumbled, but... a government chosen by the people had continued to function," whereas in 1945 "the Third Reich simply ceased to exist. There was no longer any German authority on any level." [EYEWITNESS] Shirer states he personally "found little bitterness toward [Hitler] when I returned to Germany that fall [1945]" — a striking observation on postwar German public sentiment, worth reading alongside the historiography of collective guilt and denazification.

[EYEWITNESS] Shirer attended the Nuremberg trials and describes the defendants — men he had covered "in their hour of glory" at party rallies — as reduced to "a drab assortment of mediocrities." Verdicts: acquitted — Fritzsche, Schacht, Papen; life imprisonment — Hess, Raeder, Funk; 20 years — Speer, Schirach; 15 years — Neurath; 10 years — Dönitz; death — Ribbentrop, Keitel, Kaltenbrunner, Rosenberg, Frank, Frick, Streicher, Seyss-Inquart, Sauckel, Jodl, ten of whom were hanged beginning 1:11 a.m., 16 October 1946. Göring evaded hanging by swallowing smuggled poison two hours before his scheduled execution; Robert Ley hanged himself in his cell before trial began (Shirer 1960). See [[nuremberg-trials-1945-1949]] for fuller treatment.

**Afterword (1990) and the persistence of the "German character" thesis.** Writing thirty years after the book's first publication, Shirer records the book's reception: Trevor-Roper's front-page *New York Times* rave ("a splendid work of scholarship, objective in method, sound in judgment, inescapable in its conclusions") against Gordon Craig's more critical *Herald-Tribune* review and a cool academic reception generally ("as if I were a usurper"); German reviewers "furiously attacked" it, with Adenauer reportedly calling Shirer "A German-hater!" [SHIRER-THESIS] In the 1990 Afterword, written amid German reunification, Shirer still voices doubt that Germans had fundamentally changed: "Europe will be faced again with the German problem… I myself am not so sure," proposing that a reunified Germany be enmeshed in a European security system, and arguing the H-bomb renders "an old-fashioned conqueror like Adolf Hitler obsolete." This is Shirer's enduring national-character/Sonderweg-adjacent interpretive stance persisting to the end of his life — see [[german-sonderweg]] for the fuller historiographical debate this position sits within.
