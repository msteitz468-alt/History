# Siege of Antioch (21 October 1097 – 28 June 1098)

## Narrative

The decisive and nearly fatal episode of the [[first-crusade-1095|First Crusade]]. Antioch, a great
fortified city below Mount Silpius, lay astride the road to the Holy Land and could not be bypassed.
The crusaders' circuit was too long to seal, and the **winter of 1097–98** brought starvation: per
the [[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]] (John France), "by Christmas
1097… the army was starving," a foraging force collided with Duqaq of Damascus, and at nearby
Ma'arra "some crusaders resorted to cannibalism." **Maritime resupply** by Genoese and English
fleets kept the siege alive. With **Kerbogha of Mosul** approaching with a relief army, the
crusaders took the city by **treachery** (a subverted wall-tower) on 3 June 1098 — only to be
besieged in turn. On **28 June 1098** they sortied in a disciplined "complex formation" and routed
Kerbogha's larger but disunited coalition, saving the crusade.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[first-crusade-1095]]` — Antioch was the central obstacle on the crusade's route. Its
fall and the defeat of Kerbogha were enabled above all by **Seljuq political disunity** (France:
"they would never have been victorious if their enemies had not been divided") and by **sea-borne
logistics**, the campaign's true centre of gravity.

## Consequence Analysis

`enabled:` the march on and capture of **Jerusalem** (July 1099) and the founding of the **Latin
East**. `produced:` the **Principality of Antioch** and a durable **crusader–Byzantine quarrel**
over who was owed the city, since Alexios I had launched the crusade partly to recover it.

## Actors

Bohemond of Taranto (engineered the city's capture and kept it) · Raymond of Toulouse · Godfrey of
Bouillon · Adhémar of Le Puy (legate) · Yaghi Siyan (Seljuq governor) · Kerbogha of Mosul.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] reconstructs the siege from Arabic chronicles and Frankish gaps, stressing **ephemeral Saljuq loyalties** more than Frankish destiny.

**Coalition failure before the walls.** In summer 1097 Ridwan, Yaghi-Siyan, Karbuqa of Mosul, and Sukman of Diyar Bakr were campaigning in central Syria when news of Franks toward Antioch arrived at Shayzar. Yaghi-Siyan urged an immediate return; Sukman preferred seizing Diyar Bakr first; the coalition argued and dispersed. News that Qilij Arslan and the Danishmendids had failed in Anatolia confirmed the threat. Yaghi-Siyan sent sons to Duqaq at Damascus and to Karbuqa/Mosul, and messengers to “all the amirs of the Muslims”; Damascus scholars debated whether *jihad* duty was local only or also for neighbors — the broader duty won. Fatimid envoys, meanwhile, negotiated a **separate peace** with the Franks, removing an Egyptian threat for the duration.

**Siege and betrayal.** Franks planned a long siege with foraging and sea links (English fleet took St. Simeon and Latakia). One chronicler blamed early castle losses on “Yaghi-Siyan’s ugly conduct and oppressive grip.” Ridwan–Duqaq relief attempts failed; early Muslim setbacks emboldened Franks and Armenian allies who ousted Turkish garrisons. Yaghi-Siyan expelled able-bodied Christian men while vowing to protect their families; many Antiochenes camped with the Franks. The wall-guard **Firuz** (Muslim, of Armenian Christian origin, an armorer) offered Bohemond of Taranto entry for safe conduct. On the night of **4 June 1098** Firuz let down ropes; mass killing and enslavement followed (“beyond computation”); ~**3,000** fled to the citadel. Yaghi-Siyan, hearing alarm from the citadel direction, panicked, fled south (away from the Muslim army north of Aleppo), and died of wounds or thirst; an Armenian peasant brought his head to the Franks.

**Karbuqa’s reverse siege and 28 June.** Karbuqa advanced from Marj Dabiq via Artah and the Iron Bridge, took Mt. Silpius, and appointed his own citadel commander; Franks sealed the citadel off and starved inside the depleted city (“The powerful fed on their horses, while the wretched poor ate carrion and the leaves of trees”). Sources nearly unanimously paint Karbuqa as headstrong — one claims the retort “My sword alone will eject you.” Ridwan’s messages against Duqaq sparked Turcoman desertions; a Bedouin–Turk fight drove Bedouin abandonment; Karbuqa forbade engaging small Frankish foragers, insulting tribal honor. The one Muslim source that mentions the **Holy Lance** discovery depicts it as a cunning ruse to rally Frankish troops. On **28 June 1098** the Franks emerged en masse; the remaining Turcomans routed; Karbuqa burned tents and fled toward Aleppo. Jihad volunteers “stood firm… seeking martyrdom” and were cut down “by the thousands”; survivors were seized by Armenians; the citadel garrison surrendered on terms then was attacked en route to Aleppo.

**Thesis.** Loss of Antioch was the invasion’s “first and most stinging shock,” revealing how ephemeral personal loyalties among Saljuq military elites were; blame split between Yaghi-Siyan’s statesmanship and Karbuqa’s contempt. The fragile unity of Yaghi-Siyan’s initial *jihad* call collapsed under battle. In 1098 the ʿAbbasid caliph sent an envoy to the Saljuq sultan to seek aid against the Franks — the idea of massive centralized *jihad* did not disappear, but centralized reactions remained “slow and infrequent.”

## Historiography

Western operational treatments ([[sources/france-victory-in-the-east-1994|France]]; Cambridge History of War) emphasize logistics, the wall-tower betrayal, and the disciplined 28 June sortie. Muslim Arabic sources (Ibn al-Qalanisi, Ibn al-Athir, later compilers) center **Yaghi-Siyan**, **Karbuqa**, and the collapse of elite solidarity; the Holy Lance is almost invisible or treated as a ruse. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] synthesizes both streams and rejects monocausal “Crusader fervor” explanations: Antioch fell because Saljuq Syria’s personal politics made concerted defense impossible. Later literary memory (e.g. *abdal* at the Field of Blood) answered Frankish miracle narratives with Muslim ones.
