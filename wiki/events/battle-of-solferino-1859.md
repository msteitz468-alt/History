# Battle of Solferino (24 June 1859)

## Narrative

The last great European battle in the classic mold: a single day of concentrated
fighting (~300,000 men engaged) in which the Franco-Piedmontese army under
Napoleon III defeated the Austrians under Franz Joseph — all three monarchs
personally present on the field, in the full ancien-régime manner, and the
outcome decided by the traditional retreat rule, without pursuit. The verdict was
accepted — Franz Joseph reportedly said "I have lost a battle. I pay with a
province" (Strachan), "the spirit of the ancien régime exactly" (Whitman);
armistice at Villafranca within weeks, Lombardy ceded — and the battle thereby
"resolved its war, producing a momentous historic verdict: the unification of
Italy" (Whitman). Its double afterlife defines the transition it stands on: Henri
Dunant — who had arrived as an ordinary "battle tourist" in fine dress, expecting
to witness history, and only pages into his own memoir began registering the
horror — wandered the field among the ~40,000 casualties and wrote *Un Souvenir
de Solférino* (1862), founding the Red Cross movement and inspiring the 1864
Geneva Convention — the seed of modern International Humanitarian Law.

## Causal analysis

- part_of: the Second Italian War of Independence; caused_by the Cavour–Napoleon
  III design (Plombières) to expel Austria from Lombardy.

## Consequence analysis

- produced: Villafranca armistice, cession of Lombardy, momentum to Italian
  unification (1861).
- enabled: the humanitarian turn in the law of war — Dunant, the Red Cross,
  Geneva 1864. Whitman's irony: the horror that launched humanitarian law occurred
  at one of the *last functioning* [[verdict-of-battle|battle-verdicts]]; the
  humanitarian regime rose exactly as the containment mechanism it replaced was
  dying ([[death-of-pitched-battle]]).

## Actors

Napoleon III (France) and Victor Emmanuel II (Piedmont) vs. Franz Joseph
([[habsburg-empire]]).

## Historiography

Standard accounts treat Solferino chiefly as the origin story of the Red Cross;
Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012]]) repositions it as the terminal
exemplar of the old [[jus-victoriae]] system — a one-day Aristotelian battle whose
verdict was honored. Military historians note command was chaotic and the
casualties shocking to contemporaries precisely because European great-power war
had been rare since 1815.
