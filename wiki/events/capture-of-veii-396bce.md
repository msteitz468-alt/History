# The Capture of Veii

## Narrative

The capture of **Veii** in **396 BCE** (Varronian) was Rome's most consequential
military achievement before the Punic Wars — what Cornell (CAH VII.2, Ch. 7) calls
the **epoch-making conquest** that transformed Rome from a city-state into a
territorial power.

### The City and the Conflict

[[veii|Veii]] was one of the major cities of the **Etruscan dodecapolis**, situated
on a plateau carved out by two streams approximately 16 km north of Rome. It
controlled the right bank of the Tiber at the point where Rome needed access
northward, and it commanded the primary **salt routes** from the Tiber mouth into
the interior. The two cities had been locked in intermittent conflict throughout the
fifth century: Rome had captured and held the satellite settlement of **Fidenae**
(between Rome and Veii) at various points, but Veii itself remained untaken.

### The Siege

The tradition records a **ten-year siege** — a number obviously modeled on the
Trojan War and not to be taken literally. A prolonged siege there certainly was,
probably beginning in the **400s BCE** and culminating in 396. The tradition's
most dramatic element is the **tunnel**: Camillus is said to have captured the
city by driving a tunnel to the citadel and erupting inside while the Veientes
were distracted by sacrifice. Archaeological investigation has not confirmed this
particular tunnel, but the siege itself and the city's destruction are confirmed
by the material record.

**M. Furius Camillus** was appointed dictator to command the final assault.
Whatever the precise mechanics of the capture, it succeeded: Veii was taken,
sacked, and its population **enslaved or dispersed**.

### The Evocatio of Juno

Before the assault, Roman ritual required that the **patron deity** of the
enemy city be invited to abandon her people and transfer her allegiance to Rome
— the *evocatio* ceremony. Camillus performed the *evocatio* of **Juno Regina**,
Veii's principal goddess. After the city fell, a temple was founded for Juno
Regina on the Aventine hill in Rome. This ritual transfer was not merely religious
theater: it symbolized the **total absorption** of Veii into the Roman world — its
gods, its population (in principle), and its territory.

## Territory and Settlement

The *ager Veientanus* — Veii's extensive agricultural territory — was distributed
to Roman citizens in **allotments of 7 iugera** (approximately 1.75 hectares).
This was not colonization in the later sense: the beneficiaries were existing Roman
citizens, who now held land north of the Tiber as Roman citizens living in Romanized
territory. The effect was a **near-doubling of Rome's cultivable citizen territory**
at a stroke.

Cornell emphasizes the structural significance: the distribution created a new
class of **medium landholders** whose economic stake in Rome's continued expansion
aligned their interests with the expansionist patrician-plebeian nobilitas. The
Veientes themselves were not expelled from the entire territory — some stayed, and
within a generation their descendants were apparently absorbed into the Roman
citizen body. Cornell notes a Roman tradition that the Veientes were eventually
"given equal citizenship" (*aequo iure* in citizenship) — a foreshadowing of
Rome's future incorporation strategy.

## Scale and Significance

The capture of Veii represents a **qualitative shift** in Roman strategy and capacity:

1. **Territorial transformation.** No Italian city-state had previously destroyed
   a rival of comparable size and simply annexed its territory. The usual pattern
   was defeating rivals, extracting tribute or alliance, and leaving their political
   organization intact. Veii was obliterated as a political entity.

2. **Economic windfall.** The slave sales from Veii and the land distribution
   gave Rome substantial economic resources for the first time. The tradition
   claims Camillus sent a tenth of the booty to the Delphic oracle — a detail
   reflecting the unusual scale of the gains.

3. **Strategic door.** Veii had been the barrier to Roman movement north of the
   Tiber. Its removal opened Etruria, Umbria, and eventually the Po valley to
   Roman military and commercial access.

4. **Template for expansion.** The model — military conquest, destruction of
   the enemy city's political organization, annexation and distribution of the
   land — became the template for the Roman treatment of recalcitrant opponents.
   The contrast with Rome's usual treaty-and-alliance approach makes Veii (and
   later Carthage and Corinth, 146 BCE) the **exceptional cases** that defined
   the outer boundary of Roman power.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` The rivalry between Rome and Veii was structural — two roughly equal
powers competing for the same ecological niche (the Tiber crossing and salt trade).
Expansion by either required reducing the other. The specific occasion of the final
war is obscured by the legendary tradition, but the structural causation is clear.

`contributed_to:` Roman **population pressure** and land hunger (the same pressures
that drove the Struggle of the Orders) made the ager Veientanus a prize of enormous
political and economic significance. The distribution promises would have motivated
the extended siege effort.

`preceded_by:` Decades of intermittent conflict, including the dramatic episode of
the **Fabii clan's private war against Veii** (c. 479 BCE) and their destruction
at the Cremera, and the multiple Roman captures and losses of Fidenae.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` The 7-iugera distributions that nearly doubled Roman citizen territory —
the single largest addition to Roman land resources in the fifth and fourth centuries.

`produced:` The Juno Regina cult on the Aventine, which became one of Rome's major
official cults and was closely associated with the plebeian movement (the Aventine
was plebeian space).

`enabled:` The subsequent Roman advance into Etruria and toward the Apennines and
Umbria. Sutrium and Nepete (Nepi), colonized probably in the 380s BCE, were described
as "the gates of Etruria" — forward positions the capture of Veii made possible.

`contributed_to:` The Gallic invasion of 390 BCE — the Gallic war-band's trajectory
through northern Etruria suggests the Etruscans, alarmed by Rome's destruction of
Veii, may have facilitated or invited Gallic involvement as a counterweight.

## Historiography

The ten-year siege narrative is universally rejected as a Trojan War analogy inserted
to give epic dignity to what was probably a long, grinding blockade of a well-fortified
city. The tunnel story is entertaining but unverified.

The **archaeological evidence** confirms destruction: Veii shows a clear destruction
horizon datable to the late fifth / early fourth century BCE, unlike the absence of
destruction evidence at Rome for 390. The city was then sparsely occupied (votive
deposits continued at some sanctuaries) but not rebuilt as a political entity.

Cornell's contribution in CAH VII.2 is to place the capture within the structural
account of Roman expansion: it was not merely a military event but the moment when
Rome **crossed the threshold** from city-state warfare to territorial imperialism.
