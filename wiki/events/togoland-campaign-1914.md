# Togoland Campaign (1914)

A brief Anglo-French invasion of German Togoland in August 1914, generally
regarded as the first Allied victory of the First World War, fought within
weeks of the war's outbreak in Europe.

## Narrative

Lacking significant defensive forces, the small German colonial
administration in Togoland could not resist a coordinated advance by
British forces from the Gold Coast and French forces from Dahomey. The
principal German objective was to protect the powerful wireless transmitter
at Kamina before its destruction; German forces demolished the station
themselves and surrendered on 26 August 1914, less than three weeks after
hostilities began.

## Causal Analysis

The campaign's speed reflects the colony's minimal military establishment
relative to its British and French neighbours' colonial garrisons, and the
overriding Allied priority of eliminating German long-range wireless
communications that could coordinate commerce raiders and other colonial
forces.

## Consequence Analysis

Togoland was divided between Britain and France as League of Nations
mandates after the war (1922), a partition whose administrative legacy
persisted through decolonization and shaped the later, separate paths of
British Togoland (which joined Ghana in 1957) and French Togoland (which
became independent Togo in 1960).

## Historiography

The campaign is thinly documented relative to European theatres of the
First World War; most accounts derive from British and German colonial
administrative records. It receives little attention in general First World
War historiography despite its symbolic status as the war's opening Allied
victory, consistent with the collection's broader gap in West African
coverage for this period.
