# The Jugurthine War (112–105 BCE)

Rome's war against **Jugurtha** of Numidia — militarily a sideshow, but politically a
catalyst that exposed senatorial corruption and launched the career of
**[[marius]]**. The subject of **Sallust's** monograph; treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs 2–3 (Lintott).

## Narrative

On the death of **Micipsa** (c. 118), Numidia passed jointly to his sons Adherbal and
Hiempsal and his adopted nephew **Jugurtha** — able, popular, and known to Rome from
Numantia. Jugurtha murdered Hiempsal, drove out Adherbal, and, despite a Roman
partition of the kingdom (116), besieged and killed Adherbal and the **Italian
businessmen** defending him at **Cirta** (112). The resulting outcry forced Rome to
war (111).

The early war was a tale of **bribery and humiliation**: Jugurtha bought peace from
the consul Bestia, came to Rome under safe-conduct (and had a rival murdered there),
and trapped a Roman army into a disowned treaty. The scandal produced the **Mamilian
court** (109), which condemned several leading senators. **Metellus** restored
discipline (109–108) but could not finish a mobile, cavalry-based enemy in ideal
terrain. **[[marius]]**, agitating against his commander, won the consulship (107) and
the command; he took the war to Numidia's edges, but it was ended by **diplomacy** —
Sulla persuaded **Bocchus of Mauretania** to **betray Jugurtha** (105), who died in
the Mamertine prison.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **disputed succession** and Jugurtha's provocations, but above all the
**humiliation of Roman diplomacy** and the threat to the province of Africa and its
Roman/Italian settlers. Lintott argues the Senate's initial caution was *rational*
(a Numidian war was costly; a strong friendly king served Rome), and that **popular
agitation** — fed by the killed businessmen — drove Rome into the war, against
Sallust's simpler thesis of pure senatorial corruption by bribes.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **rise of Marius** (a *novus homo* consulship and a people-granted
command) and his army recruitment from the **capite censi** (see
[[marian-army-reforms]]); the **Mamilian court** as a weapon of *popularis* attack on
the nobility.
enabled: the political pattern — military failure and senatorial corruption exploited
by tribunes — that ran through the 100s into the age of Saturninus.

## Historiography

**Sallust's *Bellum Iugurthinum*** is the central source — a moralising indictment of
the *factio paucorum*, to be read critically (Lintott corrects its account of Roman
motives). It is also a key witness to the **transitional Roman army** (the last traces
of the manipular system).
