# The Battle of Zama (202 BCE)

The decisive battle of the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|Second Punic War]], in
which [[scipio-africanus|Scipio]] defeated [[hannibal]] in Africa and ended
Carthage as a great power. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 3
(Briscoe).

## Narrative

Scipio's invasion of Africa (204) and his victories — the burning of the camps
of Hasdrubal Gisgo and **Syphax**, and the battle of the **Great Plains** —
forced Carthage to recall [[hannibal]] from Italy (203). A provisional peace
collapsed when the Carthaginians attacked a Roman supply convoy during the truce.
Hannibal met Scipio in the interior; a parley between the two generals failed
(Hannibal offering Rome the islands, Scipio demanding more). At Zama the decisive
factor was **cavalry**: the defection of the Numidian **[[masinissa]]** gave
Scipio mounted superiority, reversing the balance of Cannae. Scipio's lines
absorbed Hannibal's elephant charge through deliberate lanes; the returning Roman
and Numidian cavalry struck the Carthaginian rear. Hannibal was defeated and fled.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: Scipio's strategic choice to **carry the war to Africa** — opposed at
Rome by [[fabius-maximus|Fabius]] and Fulvius Flaccus, who wished to expel
Hannibal from Italy first — forcing Carthage to recall Hannibal and fight on its
own ground.
contributed_to: the **defection of Masinissa** to Rome, which gave Scipio the
cavalry edge.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **peace of 201** — Carthage kept its African territory but
surrendered Spain, its fleet (all but ten triremes) and elephants, agreed to wage
no war without Roman permission, recognised Masinissa's enlarged kingdom, and paid
a 10,000-talent indemnity over fifty years.
enabled: Roman supremacy in the western Mediterranean and the eastward turn of
Roman arms; the clause recognising "all lands possessed by Masinissa's ancestors"
was, in Briscoe's phrase, "the seed of later disputes" leading to the Third Punic
War.

## Historiography

Reported by [[polybius]] (XV.4–14) and [[livy]] (XXX.29–35). The **exact site**
("Zama" is a later name; the battle was inland from Carthage) is disputed. The
tactical reconstruction — Scipio adapting and reversing the lessons of Cannae,
his use of cavalry and of lanes against the elephants — rests largely on Polybius,
whose Scipionic sympathies colour the account. Walbank's commentary is the
standard control.
