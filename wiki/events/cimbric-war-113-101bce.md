# The Cimbric War (113–101 BCE)

## Narrative
The Cimbric War was a twelve-year conflict between the Roman Republic and a
migrating confederation of Germanic and Celtic peoples — the **Cimbri**,
**Teutones**, and **Ambrones** — who moved south and west out of Jutland and
central Europe from the late second century BCE. First contact came at
**Noreia** (113 BCE), where the consul Gnaeus Papirius Carbo was defeated. Over
the following decade the migrants wandered through Gaul, repeatedly defeating
Roman armies sent to contain them.

The nadir came at **Arausio** (6 October 105 BCE), where the personal rivalry
between the proconsul Quintus Servilius Caepio and the consul Gnaeus Mallius
Maximus — who refused to cooperate or combine their camps — produced one of the
worst defeats in Roman history. Ancient sources report casualty figures rivaling
Cannae (Roman tradition claims up to 80,000 dead, a figure to treat with
caution). The catastrophe caused panic at Rome and the specter of the *terror
Cimbricus*.

In the emergency, [[marius|Gaius Marius]] was elected consul repeatedly and in
absentia — a flagrant breach of the [[mos-maiorum|customary norms]] on iterated
office-holding. Given time to retrain and re-equip the army (the reforms
associated with his name), Marius destroyed the Teutones and Ambrones at
**Aquae Sextiae** (102 BCE) and, with his colleague Quintus Lutatius Catulus,
annihilated the Cimbri at **Vercellae** in the Po valley (101 BCE). The migrants
were effectively exterminated or enslaved.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` the southward migration of the Cimbri and Teutones — its ultimate
  causes (climate, population pressure, displacement) are debated in the ancient
  and modern literature.
- `contributed_to:` Roman command dysfunction — the refusal of Caepio and Mallius
  Maximus to cooperate at Arausio turned a difficult situation into a disaster,
  itself a symptom of aristocratic rivalry overriding collective interest.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the political dominance of [[marius|Marius]], who held an
  unprecedented five consecutive consulships (104–100 BCE) on the strength of
  being Rome's savior.
- `enabled:` the acceleration and vindication of the [[marian-army-reforms|Marian
  military reforms]] — the recruitment of the landless *capite censi*,
  standardized equipment and training, and the reorganization that produced
  armies loyal to their general. This is a central node in the [[mos-maiorum|norm-erosion]]
  account of the Republic's fall.
- `preceded_by:` the [[jugurthine-war-112-105bce|Jugurthine War]], which
  overlapped with it and first raised Marius to prominence.

## Historiography
The war is known almost entirely from later literary sources — Plutarch's *Life
of Marius*, Livy's *Periochae*, Velleius Paterculus, Florus, and Orosius — with
no contemporary narrative surviving. Casualty figures (especially the ~80,000
claimed for Arausio) derive from this tradition and are almost certainly
inflated in the standard Roman manner; they should be read as expressing the
scale of the shock rather than as reliable counts. The identity of the Cimbri and
Teutones (Germanic, Celtic, or mixed) is debated. Modern accounts foreground
Arausio as a case study in how Roman aristocratic rivalry could paralyze command,
and the war as a whole as the proximate driver of the Marian reforms — though
historians debate how "revolutionary" and how deliberate those reforms actually
were, versus a gradual evolution Marius merely accelerated.
