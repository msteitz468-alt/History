# Battle of Hanoi (December 1946 – February 1947)

The Battle of Hanoi (19 December 1946 onward) marked the outbreak of the full-scale First Indochina War. After the French bombardment of Haiphong, Viet Minh forces in Hanoi launched coordinated attacks on French positions. The ensuing two-month urban battle allowed the DRV government and its main army to withdraw intact to the Viet Bac mountains while pinning down French forces.

## Narrative

On the evening of 19 December 1946, following a power outage that plunged Hanoi into darkness and an explosion, Viet Minh tu ve (self-defense) militiamen attacked French installations across the city. Jean Sainteny, the French commissioner, was seriously wounded.

The Viet Minh plan, developed after the Haiphong catastrophe, was not to hold the capital indefinitely but to conduct house-by-house resistance long enough for the government and the bulk of the regular army to evacuate to the remote, forested Viet Bac region, which would serve as the new base area.

French expectations of a quick victory proved false. It took approximately two months of bitter street fighting for French forces to clear the city sufficiently for the Viet Minh to slip away under cover of darkness. The French held the major towns and main roads; the Viet Minh retained control of the surrounding countryside, remote villages, and the mountain trails.

Fighting simultaneously erupted in Bac Ninh, Nam Dinh, Hue, Tourane (Da Nang), and Saigon/Cochin China, confirming that the conflict was now nationwide.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Haiphong Incident and French decision to use overwhelming force to break the political deadlock. d’Argenlieu and Valluy had been pushing for decisive action; the Haiphong pretext was seized upon.

**contributed_to:** Collapse of remaining trust in negotiations. Ho Chi Minh had previously told a reporter that neither side could “afford the luxury of a bloody war,” yet the Vietnamese leadership concluded they had no choice but to fight an “atrocious struggle” rather than renounce independence.

**preceded_by:** The uneasy post-August 1945 and March 1946 coexistence, the failed Fontainebleau conference, and repeated French efforts (under d’Argenlieu) to reassert control without genuine concessions.

## Consequence Analysis

The battle established the basic strategic pattern of the early war: French superiority in conventional arms and control of urban centers versus Viet Minh control of the population and countryside, intelligence dominance, and ability to choose when and where to fight.

The successful evacuation preserved the Viet Minh leadership and regular forces (chu luc) for the protracted struggle. French claims of having “won” the battle of Hanoi were tactically correct but strategically hollow.

The event also accelerated the internationalization of the conflict as France began portraying it more explicitly as part of the global anti-Communist struggle to secure American support.

## Actors

- **Viet Minh/DRV**: Ho Chi Minh (political leadership), Vo Nguyen Giap (military), local tu ve militias who bore the brunt of urban fighting.
- **French**: General Valluy, High Commissioner d’Argenlieu, Commissioner Sainteny (wounded).

## Historiography

Logevall (2012) stresses French agency and provocation. Vietnamese accounts emphasize the defensive character of the resistance and the heroism of the population. French military histories often focus on the difficulties of urban combat and the skill with which the Viet Minh withdrew. The exact trigger (who fired first on 19 December) remains disputed in detail, but the larger responsibility for escalation is widely assigned to French policy choices in late 1946.
