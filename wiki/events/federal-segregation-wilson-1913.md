# Federal Resegregation under Wilson (from 1913)

Systematic introduction of racial segregation into previously integrated (or less formally segregated) federal workplaces after [[wilson-woodrow|Woodrow Wilson]] took office — Post Office, Treasury, Navy, and other departments under southern Democratic appointees. Cox treats this not as a side scandal but as core presidential practice: "Wilson did not preach white supremacy, he practiced it" (quoting Adriane Lentz-Smith).

## Narrative

Within months of the inauguration (cabinet discussions from April 1913), Postmaster General Burleson and other southern appointees introduced segregated toilets, screens, and demotions across federal departments. Bridgman and others recorded presidential approval of “friction” reduction. Protests by [[trotter-william-monroe|William Monroe Trotter]], [[wells-ida-b|Ida B. Wells]], and the NAACP (notably 6 Nov 1913 and the explosive 10 Nov 1914 White House interview) met irritation and defense of segregation. Cox pairs this bureaucratic revolution with the same coalition’s fear of a federal Anthony Amendment that might re-open voting-rights enforcement in the South. A 2021 study of civil-service records (cited by Cox) documents the scale of harassment and rank loss.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: Wilson administration personnel and racial ideology
- contributed_to: national Jim Crow consolidation in the Progressive era
- concurrent_with: [[woman-suffrage-procession-1913]] and early Anthony Amendment lobbying — Cox's key structural link: the same coalition feared Black *and* woman voting power under a federal amendment

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: durable federal workplace segregation
- contributed_to: [[wilson-race-and-gender-legacy]] reassessment; Klan revival climate of the 1910s–20s
- enabled: southern Democratic leverage over any federal voting-rights vehicle, including the Anthony Amendment

## Historiography

Ignored or minimized in mid-century Wilson biography (Walworth, early Link); now central to the post-2010s reassessment (Princeton School renaming debates; academic literature Cox synthesizes). Cox's contribution is to *integrate* resegregation with suffrage obstruction as one civil-rights story.
