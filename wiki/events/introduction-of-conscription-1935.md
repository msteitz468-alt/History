# Introduction of Conscription (1935)

## Narrative

On 16 March 1935, Hitler announced the creation of a new Wehrmacht of thirty-six divisions and the introduction of general military service (conscription), in open defiance of the military restrictions of the Versailles Treaty. This was the first major public breach of Versailles in the military sphere and a key step in the diplomatic revolution of the mid-1930s.

Army planning (Beck memorandum 6 March 1935, exchanges with Fritsch) had long foreseen a peacetime army of 21–36 divisions, with conscription from October 1934 or summer 1935 at latest. The size of 36 divisions (550,000 men) had been the intended eventual goal; Hitler made it the immediate target.

Tensions rose in early 1935. A joint British-French communiqué (3 February) condemned unilateral rearmament. A British White Paper (mid-March) cited German rearmament as reason for British increases. Hitler developed a diplomatic illness and postponed a planned visit by Simon and Eden. On 10 March Göring publicly announced a German air force (doubling the figures for effect). French renewal of the 1921 military treaty with Belgium and the two-year service law provided the pretext.

On 13 March in Munich, Hitler (still in bed) summoned his Wehrmacht adjutant Hoßbach and announced the decision to reintroduce conscription immediately. He expounded reasons for two hours (advantageous situation, French measures). Astonishingly, he did not consult Fritsch or Beck directly on size; Hoßbach (familiar with army thinking) stipulated 36 divisions, which Hitler accepted without demur. This was larger and faster than Beck had proposed days earlier.

Hitler initially resisted informing military leaders and ministers to preserve secrecy. Hoßbach persuaded him. On 14–15 March Blomberg and Fritsch were astonished and initially opposed on grounds of risk and timing (though not the expansion itself). By the day of announcement Blomberg had overcome doubts, praised the 'great deed' at the last pre-announcement cabinet, and led 'Heil' cheers. Fritsch confined objections to technical speed issues.

On 16 March Hitler (with Neurath) informed ambassadors. The French ambassador protested immediately; British inquired about air and naval ratios. The dramatic announcement followed: new Wehrmacht of 36 divisions, general conscription justified by other states' rearmament and German desire for peace. Special newspaper editions and delirious crowds outside the Chancellery celebrated 'the first great measure to liquidate Versailles'.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Hitler's unilateral decision on timing and scale (pretext from French moves; desire for spectacular coup).

`contributed_to:` Shared rearmament goals across military, Foreign Office, and regime; British signals of relative tolerance; French/Belgian moves as convenient justification.

`preceded_by:` Secret rearmament; withdrawal from League (1933); Poland pact (1934).

Hitler supplied the decisive timing and propaganda framing. Military had planned the expansion but not the haste or lack of consultation.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Open breach of Versailles; announcement of 550,000-man conscript army; major domestic propaganda victory.

`enabled:` Path to further revision (Rhineland 1936); loosened constraints on military build-up.

`concurrent_with:` Saar plebiscite triumph (Jan 1935) still fresh; Simon/Eden talks postponed then held.

The move enhanced Hitler's domestic position even among regime opponents. Abroad it tested reactions and found them weak.

## Actors

- Primary: Hitler (sole key decision); Hoßbach (sizing advice); Blomberg/Fritsch (acquiescence after initial shock).
- Secondary: Göring (air force announcement as precursor); Neurath (diplomatic side); foreign powers (responses).

## Historiography

Kershaw stresses Hitler's 'more spectacular the better' maxim, secrecy, and first instance of a major foreign-policy step without prior military consultation. Military opposition was to timing/risk, not substance. The episode illustrates the pattern: Hitler determined timing; broad revisionist aims united policy-makers. Public response showed near-universal support for ending Versailles military shackles. Sets stage for Rhineland gamble.

**Sources from range:** Detailed reconstruction from Hoßbach account, cabinet minutes, Rosenberg diary, contemporary diplomatic reports, Shirer observations.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer notes the decree was announced on a Saturday, 16 March 1935 — "most of Hitler's surprises were reserved for Saturdays," per wry Wilhelmstrasse comment, since British officials took weekends off. The decree established a peacetime army of twelve corps and thirty-six divisions (~500,000 men), unilaterally ending Versailles's military restrictions; Britain and France protested but took no action (Shirer 1960).

[EYEWITNESS] Shirer attended the Heroes' Memorial Day ceremony at the Berlin State Opera House the next day (17 March): "the entire lower floor was a sea of military uniforms, the faded gray uniforms and spiked helmets of the old Imperial Army mingling with the attire of the new Army, including the sky-blue uniforms of the Luftwaffe, which few had seen before." Field Marshal von Mackensen, the last surviving Kaiser-era field marshal, stood at Hitler's side in Death's-Head Hussars uniform — deliberate staging linking the new Wehrmacht to Imperial military tradition.

Manstein's later Nuremberg testimony corroborated the wiki's account of unilateral decision-making on scale: the General Staff, if consulted, "would have proposed twenty-one divisions... The figure of thirty-six divisions was due to a spontaneous decision of Hitler." Generals including Manstein and Witzleben learned of the decree over the radio, not having been consulted; Hitler was at Berchtesgaden at the time (Shirer 1960).
