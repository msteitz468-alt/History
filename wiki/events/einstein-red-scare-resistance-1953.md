# Einstein Red Scare Resistance, 1953

Discrete public interventions by Einstein against McCarthy-era congressional investigations into loyalty and communism, centered on advice to invoke First Amendment non-cooperation rather than Fifth Amendment.

## Narrative

In 1953, Brooklyn schoolteacher William Frauenglass, called before a Senate Internal Security Subcommittee on communist influence in schools and having refused to testify, sought Einstein's endorsement. Einstein replied in a letter he authorized for publication: "The reactionary politicians have managed to instill suspicions of all intellectual efforts. They are now proceeding to suppress the freedom of teaching." He advocated "the revolutionary way of non-cooperation in the sense of Gandhi’s. Every intellectual who is called before one of the committees ought to refuse to testify."

The letter sparked widespread editorial condemnation (New York Times, Washington Post, Philadelphia Inquirer, Chicago Tribune) and angry citizen mail, but also notable support (including from Bertrand Russell, who compared it to George Washington, and Abraham Flexner).

Shortly after, physicist Albert Shadowitz (former union member with Communist Party past, facing McCarthy probe on defense industry ties) drove unannounced to Mercer Street. Admitted after explaining his plight, he received Einstein's assurance that "If you take this path then feel free to use my name in any way that you wish." Shadowitz cited Einstein before the committee ("I refuse to answer that and I am following the advice of Professor Einstein"); McCarthy took over questioning.

Einstein also volunteered as character witness for W.E.B. Du Bois (indicted over World Peace Council petition); case dismissed upon mention of Einstein. In Oppenheimer security clearance hearings (1954), Einstein advised resignation over answering charges ("The trouble with Oppenheimer is that he loves a woman who doesn’t love him—the United States government") and signed Institute faculty petition of support.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` McCarthy hearings, loyalty programs, H-bomb decision atmosphere, Soviet bomb (1950). `contributed_to:` Einstein's perception of parallel to 1930s Germany ("The German calamity of years ago repeats itself"). `enabled:` his prior commitment to free expression and individualism ("Tolerance of free expression and independence of thought... were the core values that Americans... most cherished").

## Consequence Analysis

Heightened Einstein's image as "enfant terrible" and "black sheep" in America; galvanized some resisters; provoked debate on academic freedom (plumbers' union card offer after "plumber or peddler" remark). Contributed to discourse that helped McCarthy's later discredit (1954 Army hearings, Murrow). FBI dossier grew but produced "nothing incriminating." Reinforced Einstein's view that "America is incomparably less endangered by its own Communists than by the hysterical hunt..."

## Actors

Primary: Einstein. Secondary: Frauenglass (and son Richard), Shadowitz, Oppenheimer, McCarthy/Cohn, Russell (defense), Flexner (support), Du Bois.

## Historiography

Isaacson presents Einstein's stance as consistent with lifelong nonconformity and defense of individual liberty against authoritarianism (Nazi or Stalinist or McCarthyite), tempered by recognition that American democracy ultimately self-corrected (unlike Germany 1932–33). Debates persist on whether his interventions were effective or counterproductive; his advice to "refuse to testify" on First Amendment grounds was principled but legally risky.
