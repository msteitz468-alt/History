# Collapse of the First Silk Roads Era

A **transition page** marking the systemic unwinding of the integrated Afro-Eurasian
exchange network of the [[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]] in the mid-third century CE.

## Narrative

The network that bound Afro-Eurasia together rested on the simultaneous strength of four
empires; in [[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin's]] account it unravelled
because all four faltered at nearly the same time:

- **Later [[han-dynasty|Han]]** — abdication of Emperor Xian in **220 CE**, followed by the
  Three Kingdoms and the "Age of Disunity."
- **[[parthian-empire|Parthia]]** — Artabanus V killed by Ardashir I at **Hormozdgan (224
  CE)**, ending the Arsacid empire.
- **[[kushan-empire|Kushans]]** — death of the last Great Kushan, **Vasudeva, c. 225 CE**;
  the [[sasanian-empire|Sasanians]] destroyed Begram and Taxila by 262 CE.
- **[[roman-empire|Rome]]** — the [[crisis-of-the-third-century|Crisis of the Third
  Century]] (235–284 CE).

Benjamin reads this as the **"vulnerability"** trend of an ancient
[[proto-globalization|globalization]] (Jennings): interdependent communities so tightly
linked that failure in one place harmed everyone. Disease compounded it — the
[[antonine-plague-165|Antonine Plague]] and [[plague-of-cyprian-250|Plague of Cyprian]]
devastated populations at both ends, and the trade routes themselves helped spread them.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the near-simultaneous imperial contractions (above). `contributed_to:` the
epidemics. The simultaneity is the key analytical claim — the **supply side (Han, Kushan,
Parthia) and the demand side (Rome) failed together**, so no single recovery could revive
the whole. Whether the collapses were causally linked or merely concurrent is itself open;
Benjamin emphasizes concurrence amplified by shared vulnerability rather than a single
prime mover.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` a steep decline in trans-Eurasian trade. Physical evidence: the **absence of
third-century Roman coins** at Indian sites; the **abandonment of Myos Hormos** in the
fourth century; the **Indus Valley (Bactria-to-NW-India) route ceasing** to operate.
`followed_by: [[silk-roads|the Second Silk Roads Era]]` — a revived network (c. 600–1000
CE) linking Tang China, the Dar al-Islam, and Byzantium, in which the [[sogdians|Sogdians]]
emerged as the dominant overland carriers.

## Actors

[[han-dynasty]] · [[parthian-empire]] · [[kushan-empire]] · [[roman-empire]] ·
[[sasanian-empire]] (successor) · [[sogdians]] (later carriers).

## Historiography

Synthesizes Chinese dynastic histories, Greco-Roman sources, numismatics, and port
archaeology. Benjamin stresses (with Kyle Harper on disease) that the role of epidemics is
increasingly central to explanations of the third-century contraction at both ends of the
network. See the standing debate at [[crisis-of-the-third-century]] over how
"catastrophic" the Roman end really was.
