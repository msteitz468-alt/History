# Second Bull Run (Second Manassas)

Major Confederate victory in northern Virginia that cleared the way for Lee’s invasion of Maryland.

## Narrative

See detailed tactical analysis at [[hubs/warfare/long-19th-century/american-civil-war/battle-of-second-bull-run-1862]].

Pope’s Army of Virginia was outmaneuvered by Lee. Jackson captured supplies and held a defensive position; Longstreet delivered a devastating flank attack on the second day.

## Causal Analysis

Lee’s audacious division of his army and use of interior lines. Pope’s overconfidence and failure to account for Longstreet’s arrival.

## Consequence Analysis

Boosted Confederate morale and allowed the first invasion of the North. Led directly to Antietam.

## Historiography

The battle's documentary base includes the Official Records, Lee's and Jackson's staff reports, and Longstreet's own postwar writings, which became embroiled in the broader "Lost Cause" controversy over his conduct at Gettysburg the following year — a dispute that colored (and in some cases distorted) retrospective assessments of his generalship generally, including his celebrated performance at Second Bull Run. Pope's own reports and later testimony to the Congressional Joint Committee on the Conduct of the War are self-exculpatory, blaming subordinates (notably Fitz John Porter, court-martialed and later exonerated) for the defeat — a case study in how wartime political rivalries shaped the documentary record. Military historians generally treat Lee's use of interior lines and willingness to divide his army in the face of a larger force as one of the war's clearest demonstrations of maneuver against a numerically superior but poorly coordinated opponent.
