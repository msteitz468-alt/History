# Pompey's Settlement of the East (65–62 BCE)

The sweeping reorganization by which **[[pompey|Pompey]]**, after the
**[[third-mithridatic-war-73-63bce|Third Mithridatic War]]**, redrew the political
map of the Roman East — annexing new provinces, ringing them with **client
kingdoms**, and roughly **doubling Rome's revenue**. The most consequential single
act of Roman imperialism between the Gracchi and Augustus. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 8a (Sherwin-White).

## The new provinces

- **Bithynia–Pontus** — Pompey combined Bithynia with central and western Pontus,
  reorganizing the latter (where Mithridates had ruled through district "eparchies")
  into **eleven self-governing Greek cities**, each given a swathe of territory. The
  ***Lex Pompeia*** governing these cities — its oligarchic councils of life-aldermen
  picked by censors — still framed civic life when **Pliny the Younger** governed the
  province under Trajan (c. 110 CE).
- **Syria** — Pompey **annexed the Seleucid heartland** (64), refusing to confirm the
  last claimant **Antiochus XIII**: with no ruler able to hold the country against
  "Arabs and Jews" and brigands, and with Parthia looming, direct rule was the only
  option. He stationed some four legions in Syria and Cilicia.
- **Cilicia** — enlarged with the Cilician plain and resettled with former pirates.

## The ring of client kings

Beyond the provinces Pompey preserved **indirect rule** through reliable dynasts:
**Ariobarzanes** in Cappadocia, **Deiotarus** the Galatian in Lesser Armenia,
**Antiochus** in Commagene (holding the Euphrates crossings), **Tigranes II** confined
to core Armenia, and **Pharnaces** in the Crimean Bosporus. This buffer system
framed the **Roman–Parthian frontier**: Pompey negotiated with **Phraates III**,
withheld from him the title "King of Kings," and left the Euphrates boundary
deliberately unsettled — seeds of [[third-mithridatic-war-73-63bce|the conflict]] that
would destroy Crassus at Carrhae.

## Judaea

Drawn into the **Hasmonean civil war** between **Hyrcanus II** and **Aristobulus
II**, Pompey marched on Jerusalem (63) and, after a **three-month siege**, stormed
the **Temple Mount on a Sabbath/Day of Atonement** — entering the Holy of Holies, to
lasting Jewish outrage. He installed **Hyrcanus as high priest and ethnarch** (not
king), stripped Judaea of the Greek coastal cities the Maccabees had taken, and made
it a tribute-paying dependency — the beginning of direct Roman overlordship of the
Jews (developed in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 8b).

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **defeat of Mithridates and Tigranes** and the **collapse of Seleucid
Syria**, which left a vacuum no trustworthy local power could fill.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: two new **provinces**, a frontier system of client kings, the subjection of
**Judaea**, and a **~70% increase in Rome's public revenue** (plus 36,000 talents of
captured treasure and a personal fortune).
enabled: **Pompey's political supremacy** on his return (62) — and the crisis when
the Senate balked at ratifying his settlement and rewarding his veterans, driving him
toward the compact with Caesar and Crassus (the "First Triumvirate," Cycle 5).

## Historiography

Strabo (a Pontic native), Plutarch's *Pompey*, Appian, Dio, and **Josephus** for
Judaea; Cicero's letters illuminate the provinces. Sherwin-White stresses that the
settlement was **Pompey's personal act**, conducted with minimal reference to Rome —
the scale of one man's authority over the affairs of nations being itself a symptom
of the Republic's transformation.
