# The Battle of Adrianople (378)

The annihilation of the **eastern Roman field army** and the death of the emperor **[[valentinian-and-valens|Valens]]**
by the Goths under **Fritigern** on **9 August 378** — ranked by Ammianus Marcellinus as **the worst
Roman defeat since Cannae** (216 BCE), and a hinge event of late antiquity. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 3 (**John Curran**).

## Narrative

The **Hunnic** advance (c. 375) shattered the Gothic **Tervingi** and **Greuthungi**, who fled to the
Danube. In autumn 376 Valens admitted the Tervingi into **Thrace** — expecting recruits and revenue — but
the *comes* **Lupicinus** mismanaged and exploited the refugees (bartering dog-meat for slaves), and an
attempt to seize the chieftains provoked a full Gothic **revolt**, soon joined by the Greuthungi and by
bands of Huns and Alans. After two years of inconclusive war, Valens (newly arrived from Antioch) learned
that his nephew **Gratian** was marching east with the western army. Unwilling to share the glory —
goaded by Gratian's recent victory over the Lentienses — Valens **refused to wait**, and on 9 August led
his army out from Adrianople against Fritigern's wagon-laager. Negotiations were under way when a
premature skirmish triggered the battle; the returning **Greuthungi cavalry** of Alatheus and Saphrax
charged the Roman flank. The Roman lines, exhausted in full armour under the August sun, broke and
disintegrated. **Two-thirds of the army was destroyed**; Valens himself was killed (his body never
recovered), along with 35 tribunes and the generals Trajanus and Sebastianus — perhaps 15,000–30,000 dead.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Hunnic irruption** that drove the Goths to the frontier — the ultimate trigger.
- `contributed_to:` the **mismanaged admission** of 376 (Roman corruption and the failure to disarm or
  feed the Tervingi), which turned refugees into enemies.
- `caused_by:` **Valens' decision to give battle alone**, refusing to await Gratian — a fatal misjudgment.
- `contributed_to:` the fourth-century army's inexperience operating in large numbers — per
  [[sources/goldsworthy-roman-warfare-2000|Goldsworthy]], the defeat is attributable "largely to
  Valens' mistakes and not to the army's inefficiency," though it may also reflect this
  unpreparedness. He cites the reserve **Batavi** unit, which "could not be found when it was
  needed," as illustrating a structural problem of the late army: smaller late-Roman units,
  individually competent, lacked the command structure and mutual-support experience needed to
  run coordinated multi-line reserve tactics — a capability the old Republican/Principate legion,
  purpose-built for large set-piece battles, had possessed.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the destruction of the eastern field army and Valens' death, forcing Gratian to elevate
  **[[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]]** (379).
- `produced:` the **Gothic settlement of 382** — Theodosius' *foedus* that settled the Goths inside the
  empire as **autonomous allies under their own chiefs**, a precedent without parallel and of vast
  consequence for the later western collapse.
- `enabled:` the long-term presence of independent barbarian *gentes* within the frontiers — the Goths
  who would, a generation later, sack Rome under [[alaric|Alaric]] (see [[sack-of-rome-410]]).

## Historiography

Ammianus' near-contemporary account (his narrative's climax) frames it as a Cannae-scale catastrophe and
a verdict on Valens; some Catholics read his death as divine punishment for his **Homoian ("Arian")**
faith. Modern debate (Heather, Wolfram) concerns the numbers, the degree of Tervingi–Greuthungi
coordination, and whether 382 created a true *foedus* or a *deditio* — but all agree Adrianople opened a
new era of barbarian settlement.

[[sources/goldsworthy-roman-warfare-2000|Goldsworthy]] pushes back explicitly against reading Adrianople
as a verdict on the late Roman army's quality, and against inflating its long-term significance:
"Adrianople was a setback and a significant blow to Rome's prestige, but it was not an indication of a
decline in quality of the army." Valens's force was probably not much more than 20,000 men, of whom
roughly two-thirds were lost along with the emperor himself — "a major disaster, but its consequences
should not be exaggerated." In Goldsworthy's reading the empire's underlying resilience is shown by what
followed: Rome reverted to successful low-level operations against the Goths, and by 382 they accepted
peace terms and began supplying Roman troops — a markedly less catastrophist framing than Heather's
"true beginning of the western collapse" cited above, worth holding alongside it rather than resolving.

## Woolf, *Rome: An Empire's Story* (2012)

Source: [[woolf-rome-empires-story-2012]].

Huns appear 370s north of Black Sea; Goths faced pull (wealth/security in empire) and push (Hunnic pressure). ## CHGRW contribution

[[sabin-van-wees-whitby-cambridge-history-greek-roman-warfare-2007|CHGRW]] does not rewrite the battle narrative (Ammianus remains primary), but places 378 in several distinctive frames:

- **Rance (II.10) — operational reframing of Valens’s decision.** Sebastianus’ anti-Gothic irregular campaign is read as intentionally forcing Gothic concentration at Adrianople; Valens’ decision to fight the united Goths **before they dispersed again** is “less rash than is commonly assumed.” This softens (without erasing) the traditional charge of pure glory-seeking vs Gratian. Rance also notes Adrianople heads the traditional chronology of a “medieval cavalry age,” a periodization recent work has reinterpreted; two-thirds Roman losses were an **exceptional massacre** relative to typical Roman-victory casualty asymmetries (Strasbourg ~243 Roman dead vs Alamanni 6–8,000).
- **Elton (II.8) — borders not “challenged” as system.** Even after disasters like Adrianople, the contemporary border system was not challenged as such; the army’s dual field/limitanei structure continued. (Cross-link [[late-roman-army]].)
- **Humphries (II.7) — teleology vs contingency.** Adrianople appears in the traditional teleology Humphries critiques (post-378 consistent retreat / balance of power shifting to Rome’s enemies), but the volume as a whole resists reading one battle as structural military collapse. Valens scorned low-born Gothic envoys on the eve of battle; Fritigern used Christian clergy as negotiators; Eunapius alleged Christian profession eased the 376 admission.
- **Lee (II.11) — planning, not quality; imperial risk.** Major defeats including Adrianople are attributed above all to poor planning and decision-making, not fighting quality of the late army; Valens’ death is also a caution against campaigning emperors (education/staff-work theme also in Whitby).
- **Fear (II.12) — manpower shock and settlement path.** Mutual distrust and mismanagement after the 376 Gothic admission culminated in Valens’ death at Adrianople; the manpower shock shapes the 381 law equating two mutilated recruits with one whole recruit; settlement ends in the 382 federate peace under Theodosius (autonomous unit providing troops when required). Cross-link [[barbarization-of-the-roman-army]], [[war-and-society-greek-roman]], [[causes-of-the-fall-of-the-western-roman-empire]].

Together these reinforce Goldsworthy’s “setback not army-quality verdict” while adding Rance’s operational defence of fighting before dispersal and Lee/Elton’s insistence that **fiscal-territorial and political contexts**, not ethnic “barbarization” or frontier softening, carry the long-term story.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]] (Ch. XXVI).

- **Cannae comparison.** “the battle of Hadrianople, which equalled in the actual loss, and far surpassed in the fatal consequences, the misfortune which Rome had formerly sustained in the fields of Cannæ.”
- **Causal chain (Gibbon).** Northern pastoral motion → Huns drive Goths → Valens admits the nation → Lupicinus’s greed and arms-stipulation eluded by bribery → Marcianopolis spark → unseasonable bravery of timid Valens and pride vs Gratian → catastrophe dated as start of the “disastrous period of the fall.”
- **Crossing scale.** ~200,000 warriors probable; near a million with dependents; officers quit counting in amazement.
- **Aftermath settlement.** Theodosius’s “deliverance was work of prudence rather than valor”; Fritigern’s death shattered discipline; foederati settlement left Goths as autonomous nations who “might soon become the conquerors.”
- **Terminus.** Since the foundation of the city, “the genius of Rome, the god Terminus… had never retired before the sword of a victorious enemy” — until this age of reverse (Gibbon’s symbolic reading of territorial loss).

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

After abuses alienated admitted Tervingi and Greuthungi crossed against Roman wishes (Eunapius: ~200,000 fugitives into Thrace), Valens advanced from Constantinople without waiting for Gratian’s reinforcements, miscalculated Gothic strength, and died with perhaps **10,000** troops at Adrianople (**9 Aug 378**). Ammianus: biggest setback since Cannae; emperor’s body never recovered; Goths occupied Thrace and Illyricum blocking the West; virtually **last time a Roman emperor led troops into a major encounter until Heraclius**. “The death of Valens marked the end of an epoch.” Links to the 382 *foedus* and [[barbarization-of-the-roman-army]].
