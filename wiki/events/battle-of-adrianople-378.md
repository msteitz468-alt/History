# The Battle of Adrianople (378)

The annihilation of the **eastern Roman field army** and the death of the emperor **[[valentinian-and-valens|Valens]]**
by the Goths under **Fritigern** on **9 August 378** — ranked by Ammianus Marcellinus as **the worst
Roman defeat since Cannae** (216 BCE), and a hinge event of late antiquity. Treated in
[[cameron-cah-v13-1998|CAH XIII]] Ch. 3 (**John Curran**).

## Narrative

The **Hunnic** advance (c. 375) shattered the Gothic **Tervingi** and **Greuthungi**, who fled to the
Danube. In autumn 376 Valens admitted the Tervingi into **Thrace** — expecting recruits and revenue — but
the *comes* **Lupicinus** mismanaged and exploited the refugees (bartering dog-meat for slaves), and an
attempt to seize the chieftains provoked a full Gothic **revolt**, soon joined by the Greuthungi and by
bands of Huns and Alans. After two years of inconclusive war, Valens (newly arrived from Antioch) learned
that his nephew **Gratian** was marching east with the western army. Unwilling to share the glory —
goaded by Gratian's recent victory over the Lentienses — Valens **refused to wait**, and on 9 August led
his army out from Adrianople against Fritigern's wagon-laager. Negotiations were under way when a
premature skirmish triggered the battle; the returning **Greuthungi cavalry** of Alatheus and Saphrax
charged the Roman flank. The Roman lines, exhausted in full armour under the August sun, broke and
disintegrated. **Two-thirds of the army was destroyed**; Valens himself was killed (his body never
recovered), along with 35 tribunes and the generals Trajanus and Sebastianus — perhaps 15,000–30,000 dead.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Hunnic irruption** that drove the Goths to the frontier — the ultimate trigger.
- `contributed_to:` the **mismanaged admission** of 376 (Roman corruption and the failure to disarm or
  feed the Tervingi), which turned refugees into enemies.
- `caused_by:` **Valens' decision to give battle alone**, refusing to await Gratian — a fatal misjudgment.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the destruction of the eastern field army and Valens' death, forcing Gratian to elevate
  **[[theodosius-i|Theodosius I]]** (379).
- `produced:` the **Gothic settlement of 382** — Theodosius' *foedus* that settled the Goths inside the
  empire as **autonomous allies under their own chiefs**, a precedent without parallel and of vast
  consequence for the later western collapse.
- `enabled:` the long-term presence of independent barbarian *gentes* within the frontiers — the Goths
  who would, a generation later, sack Rome under [[alaric|Alaric]] (see [[sack-of-rome-410]]).

## Historiography

Ammianus' near-contemporary account (his narrative's climax) frames it as a Cannae-scale catastrophe and
a verdict on Valens; some Catholics read his death as divine punishment for his **Homoian ("Arian")**
faith. Modern debate (Heather, Wolfram) concerns the numbers, the degree of Tervingi–Greuthungi
coordination, and whether 382 created a true *foedus* or a *deditio* — but all agree Adrianople opened a
new era of barbarian settlement.
