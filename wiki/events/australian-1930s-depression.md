# The Great Depression in Australia

The Great Depression struck Australia from 1929 as the world financial crisis interacted with the structural vulnerabilities of a small export-dependent economy that had over-borrowed from London throughout the 1920s. It produced Australia's worst unemployment crisis, a constitutional and political emergency, and the conditions for the long-delayed renovation of state and economy that culminated in the post-war reconstruction of the 1940s.

## Narrative

### Causes

The "historical consensus remains that the critical influences for Australia were the drying up of external capital and collapse of export prices." In the 1920s Australia had borrowed heavily in London — the Loan Council was established in 1927 partly to coordinate this — while the terms of trade were already shifting against primary producers before 1929. The Wall Street crash and ensuing international crisis accelerated both tendencies catastrophically.

At the time, however, many Australians blamed the "money power" — the banks — for locking up credit. The Scullin Labor government (elected late 1929) attempted practical measures: cutting defence and immigration spending, raising tariffs, prohibiting gold exports (effectively taking Australia off the gold standard), and guaranteeing wheat minimum prices (which backfired when a glut further depressed prices).

### Niemeyer Visit and Melbourne Agreement

In June 1930 Sir Otto Niemeyer, an emissary of the Bank of England, arrived to advise. His counsel — that Australians' standard of living was too high and budgets must balance — was met with general hostility. Niemeyer's "arrogant bearing and bluntness of delivery did nothing to help his cause." The Commonwealth and State governments signed the **Melbourne Agreement**, committing to deep spending cuts, but most States feigned agreement while avoiding implementation.

### Lang Plan vs. Premiers' Plan

Two rival responses competed. NSW premier **Jack Lang** proposed the **Lang Plan**: suspend interest payments to British moneylenders and divert funds to unemployment relief. Critics called it repudiation of overseas debts. Federal Labor treasurer **Edward Theodore** advocated mild credit expansion and public works financed by "fiduciary notes" — printed without gold backing — but this was rejected by the Senate and opposed by the conservative Commonwealth Bank board under Sir Robert Gibson.

In mid-1931 the Commonwealth and States agreed the **Premiers' Plan**: a three-year programme of government expenditure cuts (including pensions), tax increases, and interest rate reductions on domestic government bonds. It was "on balance deflationary, especially when considered in conjunction with the recent cut in the basic wage" (10 per cent, January 1931). The Plan was partially offset by deficit financing through treasury bills, which increased credit flow.

### Scale of Suffering

"Most estimates of unemployment during the worst year, 1932, lie between a quarter and a third of the workforce." State governments provided unemployment relief — "the susso" (sustenance payments) — but it was insufficient. Local government and charities assisted those in greatest difficulty. Psychological suffering was considerable, especially "for those for whom unemployment or its threat was a new experience." Birth rates fell to an all-time low in the early 1930s.

Historians divide between "optimists" (who argue suffering has been overstated and some prospered) and "pessimists" (rich regional testimony of hardship). The evidence suggests: "a society under stress, but not disintegrating."

### Political Crisis

The Depression produced acute political violence and polarisation:
- **Lang government** in NSW pursued radical policies frightening to conservatives: "right to the end he maintained a radical policy that set his government apart from every other in Australia"
- **Citizen and loyalist organisations** formed (All for Australia League, most successfully) with middle-class and rural support, eventually merging into the UAP
- **Paramilitary groups**: the Old Guard (NSW), White Army / League of National Security (Victoria), and the New Guard (Sydney middle class, ex-diggers). The New Guard's most famous exploit was cutting the ribbon at the **Sydney Harbour Bridge opening (1932)** before Lang could perform the ceremony
- **Western Australian secession**: 1933 majority voted to secede; defused by Labor election win and economic recovery
- **Lang dismissed**: May 1932, Governor Sir Philip Game dismissed Lang for issuing an unlawful order to a civil servant

### Recovery

Recovery was sluggish through the 1930s. Currency devaluation (early 1931, largely a private bank initiative) helped manufacturers. Export prices recovered modestly. At the Ottawa Conference (1932), Australian negotiators secured trade concessions from Britain. The Lyons UAP government implemented the Premiers' Plan but recent research (Gregory and Butlin) suggests its "failure to act on the advice of professional economists at key moments during the 1930s undermined recovery."

Full renovation of the state did not occur until the 1940s: "that would not occur until the 1940s, for despite the growing influence of the theories of the British economist John Maynard Keynes, the new federal government had little taste for change."

## Causal Analysis

**External causes** (decisive): collapse of commodity export prices; cessation of British capital flows; global financial contagion.

**Internal contributing factors**: heavy public sector borrowing in 1920s; tariff-protected manufacturing whose inefficiencies reduced export competitiveness; arbitration system with jurisdictional complexity that complicated wage adjustment.

**Preceded by**: [[bruce-stanley|Bruce–Page]] government's borrowing programme and "protection all round"
**Produced**: Premiers' Plan; UAP; currency devaluation; Commonwealth Arbitration Court wage cuts; Lang crisis; Western Australian secession agitation

## Consequence Analysis

The Depression demonstrated that "markets could not in themselves solve the problem of economic instability — or not, at least, without inflicting an unacceptable level of human suffering." This conviction underpinned the post-war reconstruction agenda: the 1937 Royal Commission on Monetary and Banking Systems called for demand management; the 1945 Full Employment White Paper implemented it. The Depression also hardened the case for Commonwealth power over banking (Chifley's post-war banking controls) and social services (1946 referendum).

## Historiography

Economic historians debate the relative weight of internal vs. external factors but the external consensus is strong. T.J. Valentine (1987) and Mahinda Siriwardana (1995) model the causes; Gregory and Butlin (*Recovery from the Depression*, 1988) provide the standard macro-economic account. C.B. Schedvin (*Australia and the Great Depression*, 1970) is the classic study of policy responses. David Potts (*The Myth of the Great Depression*, 2006) represents the "optimist" position, arguing suffering has been overstated. A "rich literature of regional and local histories, oral testimony, anecdote, memoir and folklore" sustains the pessimist case.
