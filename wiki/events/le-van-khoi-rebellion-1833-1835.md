## Narrative

Le Van Khoi (originally Be Van Khoi, d. 1834) was a former bandit chief from Cao Bang who had been adopted into the entourage of the Saigon viceroy [[nguyen-dynasty|Le Van Duyet]] in 1819 and rose to become a trusted subordinate in the Mekong plain during the 1820s. Le Van Duyet's viceroyalty had drawn its military strength from pardoned insurgents and militiamen from Nghe An and Thanh Hoa, from banished northern criminals granted a "second chance" and remitted sentences in 1824, and from Chinese, Cham, and Khmer communities that Le Van Duyet protected and left largely to govern themselves.

When Le Van Duyet died in 1832, [[minh-mang|Minh Mang]] abolished the Saigon viceroyalty, reorganized the Mekong plain into the "Six Provinces" (Luc Tinh) under Hue-appointed officials, revoked Le Van Duyet's clemency toward banished northerners (returning them to criminal status and forcibly resettling them on the Cambodian frontier), moved to bring Chinese merchant wealth under direct control, intensified persecution of Christians, and posthumously charged Le Van Duyet himself with corruption and sedition. Taylor describes this as an attempt "to break the spirits of the southerners" by any means.

In spring 1833, while under investigation by Minh Mang's officials for irregularities in timber supply, Le Van Khoi received word that a rebel prince, Le Duy Luong, had been proclaimed king in the northern uplands (Hoa Binh) and that two or three provinces had fallen under his control — a rising itself rooted in lingering Le-dynasty loyalism among upland Muong communities and encouraged by the earlier Phan Ba Vanh rebellion of 1826–27. Reportedly summoned by a message from Le Duy Luong, Le Van Khoi mobilized his followers, killed the senior Hue officials at Saigon, and within days rebel sympathizers had seized or driven off Hue officials at every provincial center in the Mekong plain. His following was drawn from Le Van Duyet's old circle: militia officers from Thanh Hoa and Nghe An, banished-criminal militias, Chinese (led by the merchant Luu Tin), Christians, and mainstream local Vietnamese.

Minh Mang, occupied simultaneously with suppressing Le Duy Luong in the north, characterized the southern rising as the natural outcome of Le Van Duyet's "heterodox regime," which he accused of tolerating "gambling and lasciviousness" and breeding an "arrogant, lazy, and rebellious" populace; he had Le Van Khoi's relatives in Cao Bang arrested (fourteen people, including a brother and two sons). The rebellion's northern connections were reinforced by the near-simultaneous rising of Nong Van Van — Le Van Khoi's brother-in-law — in the mountains near the Qing border, which lasted into 1835.

The decisive turn against Le Van Khoi came from Thai Cong Trieu, second-in-command of the southern military forces, who had initially joined the rebels under coercion but secretly maintained loyalty to Hue. He defected back to the royal side, recovered My Tho, Vinh Long, Chau Doc, and Ha Tien for Hue, and coordinated with the royal commander Truong Minh Giang, freeing Truong Minh Giang to concentrate on the simultaneous Siamese invasion of Cambodia.

Le Van Khoi and several thousand of his most committed followers — mainly Christians, banished militiamen, and Chinese — withdrew into the great Saigon fortress that Le Van Duyet had fortified and provisioned for a long siege. Le Van Khoi himself died in early 1834 of an "eruption of tumors," and command passed to Nguyen Van Cham, leader of the banished-militiamen faction. The fortress held out for a year and a half before falling in autumn 1835. According to Taylor, 554 ringleaders and their families were beheaded on the spot; 1,278 lower-ranking rebels and their families were seized and later killed, buried in a common grave (666 of the dead reportedly Christian); and six people — including Nguyen Van Cham, Luu Tin, the French missionary Joseph Marchand, and one of Le Van Khoi's sons — were taken to Hue for public execution. The fortress itself was demolished.

## The Concurrent War with Siam

Le Van Khoi, anticipating the arrival of Hue's armies, sent a message to King Rama III of Siam apparently informing him that "the time for action had arrived." Siamese forces staged demonstrations along several Vietnamese frontiers (Xieng Khouang, Ha Tien, the Cam Lo Road near Hue) to divert Minh Mang's attention while, in autumn 1833, tens of thousands of Siamese troops invaded Cambodia along the Tonle Sap, coordinated with a seaborne force moving up the Vinh Te Canal. King Ang Chan of Cambodia fled downriver as Thai Cong Trieu's defection stabilized the Vietnamese position at Saigon. Truong Minh Giang forced the Siamese out of Cambodia by early 1834, though the retreating Siamese army devastated the Cambodian countryside and deported much of its population. This began an extended Vietnamese-Siamese struggle over Cambodian vassalage — including the effective Vietnamese annexation of Cambodia in 1836 and further wars through 1845–47 — that outlasted Le Van Khoi's rebellion by more than a decade.

## Causal Analysis

The rebellion was `caused_by` Minh Mang's dismantling of Le Van Duyet's regime and his reversal, after 1832, of the policies of ethnic and regional accommodation that had kept the far south quiescent under Gia Long and Le Van Duyet. It was `contributed_to` by the revocation of clemency for banished northern militiamen (removing their only path to stable resettlement), by Minh Mang's anti-Christian edict of early 1833 (alienating an already-integrated Christian population), and by the posthumous prosecution of Le Van Duyet (which Taylor says was "too much for local Vietnamese" to bear). It was `enabled` by the timing of the near-simultaneous Le Duy Luong uprising in the north, which both inspired Le Van Khoi to imagine restoring the Le dynasty and stretched Hue's military attention across the country. The rebellion `concurrent_with` and became entangled with the Siamese invasion of Cambodia, though Taylor treats the Siamese and Le Van Khoi actions as coordinated by messenger contact rather than as a single planned campaign.

## Consequence Analysis

The rebellion's suppression `produced` a demonstrative mass execution at Saigon and the physical demolition of the Saigon fortress, erasing a major symbol of Le Van Duyet's regime. It `enabled` an even harsher turn in Minh Mang's southern policy — assimilationist pressure on Khmer villages, tightened control of Chinese communal organization, and continued anti-Christian persecution — that Taylor identifies as part of the same "countrywide reaction to Minh Mang's effort to propagate and discipline a stricter sense of Vietnamese cultural identity" that also produced later Khmer uprisings (such as Lam Sam's rebellion of 1841). The rebellion also `contributed_to` a prolonged and costly Vietnamese-Siamese contest over Cambodia that persisted through the reigns of Minh Mang and [[nguyen-dynasty|Thieu Tri]], not resolving until the 1847 peace settlement (which nonetheless favored Siam).

## Historiography

Taylor (*A History of the Vietnamese*, 2013) presents Le Van Khoi's rebellion as the clearest case study of the costs of Minh Mang's centralizing project, framing it within a pattern of contemporaneous uprisings (Le Duy Luong in the north, Nong Van Van in the northern mountains, and later Khmer risings) that together reveal "a countrywide reaction" rather than isolated local grievances. Cambodian historians, per Taylor, have commonly read the roughly contemporaneous rebellion of the monk Ke (1820, a full decade earlier and unrelated in cause) as an anti-Vietnamese revolt tied to canal-building conditions — a reading Taylor treats skeptically on geographic grounds, noting Ba Phnom's distance from the actual worksite; this is a separate but analogous historiographical caution about attributing southern unrest reflexively to anti-Vietnamese or anti-Siamese sentiment without checking the local particulars. Taylor draws on Vietnamese court sources (implicitly including the *True Records of the Great South*) for casualty figures and official rhetoric, and treats Minh Mang's own explanation of the revolt (blaming Le Van Duyet's supposed toleration of "evil customs") as self-serving rather than descriptive.
