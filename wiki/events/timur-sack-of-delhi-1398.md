# Timur's Sack of Delhi

## Narrative

In late 1398, [[timur|Timur]] (Tamerlane) invaded the Delhi Sultanate, crossing the Indus and advancing on the capital under pretext of punishing its rulers for excessive tolerance of non-Muslim subjects, though plunder was the campaign's evident central motive. After defeating the forces of Sultan Nasir-ud-Din Mahmud Shah Tughlaq outside Delhi in December 1398, Timur's army entered and sacked the city, massacring a large portion of its population — chroniclers describe the killing of tens of thousands of captives and residents, in part triggered by fears that a captured population too large to guard or feed might revolt — and departed with immense plunder, including craftsmen deported to adorn Timur's capital at Samarkand.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Timur's broader pattern of campaigns of conquest and plunder that had already devastated much of Central Asia, Persia, and Mesopotamia; the fragmented, weakened state of the Delhi Sultanate under an ineffective sultan unable to mount unified resistance.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced:** mass death and destruction in Delhi itself, with the city's population and economy shattered for years afterward; a decisive acceleration of the Delhi Sultanate's political fragmentation, as regional governors and successor states (eventually consolidating into the Sayyid and Lodi dynasties) filled the resulting power vacuum.

## Actors

## Historiography

The sack is documented in Timurid court chronicles (notably Sharaf al-Din Ali Yazdi's *Zafarnama*) that tend to celebrate the campaign's scale and severity as evidence of Timur's power, and in Indian and Persian historical traditions that emphasize the catastrophe inflicted on Delhi; casualty figures in both traditions are almost certainly inflated in the manner typical of premodern conquest narratives and should be read as indicating scale and atrocity rather than precise counts.
