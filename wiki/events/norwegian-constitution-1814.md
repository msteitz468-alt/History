## Narrative

The Norwegian constitution of 1814 emerged from the collapse of the Napoleonic order in Northern Europe. By the Peace of Kiel, signed on 14 January 1814, Denmark's King Frederik VI was forced to cede Norway to Sweden, ending the centuries-old dual monarchy of Denmark-Norway. The Danish Prince Christian Frederik, serving as governor in Norway, refused to accept the transfer. Working with prominent Norwegian civil servants and elites, he convened a constituent assembly at Eidsvoll. On 17 May 1814 the assembly adopted a constitution that was, "in formal terms, one of the most democratic in Europe" (L41249). It established a parliamentary legislature (the storting), a supreme court, and an independent Norwegian government; it gave roughly 40 per cent of adult males over 25 the right to vote if they swore an oath to uphold it (L41681–82).

Sweden, however, refused to accept Norwegian independence. After a brief Swedish military campaign in the summer of 1814, Norway was forced to accept union with Sweden under the Swedish king as a joint monarch. The revised constitution of 4 November 1814 modified the 17 May document: the king retained a continuing veto over legislative measures and command of the army, but needed the storting's agreement to employ it in offensive war; Norway kept its own merchant flag, bank, and currency and was answerable only for its own national debt (L41287–93). Foreign policy and consular services were held in common, with Sweden dominant (L41298–99).

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause was the Peace of Kiel, by which Denmark conceded Norway to Sweden as a result of Frederik VI's alliance with Napoleon (L40581–83). The political logic of the Napoleonic Wars thus directly caused the dynastic transfer. The Norwegian elite's resistance—drawing on a consciousness of Norway as a "historic state" that had enjoyed centuries of independence in the Middle Ages (L41223–26)—and on the experience of provisional central institutions built between 1807 and 1814 (L41469–77)—gave the constitution movement its social base. German and French constitutional thought provided the models. Christian Frederik's willingness to champion Norwegian independence was an enabling contingent factor; without his initiative the assembly would not have met.

The 4 November union was caused_by the Swedish military advantage and by Karl Johan's insistence that the Peace of Kiel's provisions be respected; Great Power pressure from Russia reinforced Swedish demands. The constitution itself shaped all subsequent Norwegian politics through a structural "constitutional conservatism": every Swedish demand for amendment was rejected on the principle that the constitution must remain inviolable, a reflex that persisted for the rest of the century (L41326–29).

## Consequence Analysis

**Immediate**: Norway acquired central state institutions—storting, supreme court, government, Bank of Norway (established 1816)—and its own capital at Kristiania. The civil service replaced the aristocracy and merchant class (both weakened by the Napoleonic Wars) as the dominant political and cultural force (L41265–77).

**Long-term structural**: The 17 May constitution became the foundational reference point for the entire Norwegian nation-state project. Norwegian politics throughout the century revolved around resisting Swedish attempts to amend it and extending Norwegian independence within the union. The constitution that began as an elite document slowly widened participation: voter turnout among those eligible was only about 30 per cent in elections from 1859 to 1879 (L41686–88), but the principle of popular representation was established.

**Regional reverberations**: The Norwegian constitution became a model for Scandinavianist aspirations. Danish liberals in the 1830s–40s looked to it as the template for introducing constitutional monarchy in Denmark (L39905–06; L41817–18). Swedish national liberals likewise regarded it as a source of inspiration for reforming the Swedish four-estate riksdag (L41817–18). Christian VIII of Denmark, who had helped bring about the Norwegian constitution as a young man, was expected—but failed—to extend constitutional reform when he became king in 1839 (L40748–53).

**Notable paradox**: The 17 May constitution originally barred Jews from entering Norway; this provision was not revoked until 1851 (L43417–18).

## Actors

- **Christian Frederik** (Danish prince, later Christian VIII of Denmark): convened the Eidsvoll assembly, signed the 17 May constitution, then accepted the union arrangement.
- **Karl XIV Johan** (Marshal Bernadotte, Crown Prince of Sweden): engineered the military and diplomatic pressure that forced the November union; had long planned to use Norway as compensation for Finland's loss to Russia (L44157–58).
- **Frederik VI of Denmark**: signed the Peace of Kiel ceding Norway; also signed the compulsory education laws of 1814 (L40596–604).
- **Norwegian civil servant elite**: provided the political leadership at Eidsvoll and dominated the storting for decades after 1814, defending the constitution against Swedish encroachment (L41265–77).

## Historiography

The source treats the 17 May constitution as "the obvious and most fundamental starting point for the building of Norway as a nation state in the nineteenth century" (L41241–43), while the 4 November revision is described as the document "as modified" after Norway was forced to accept the union. The Norwegian scholarly tradition has emphasised the constitution's democratic credentials and its role as a national foundation myth. Recent historiography has moved toward analysing the social and economic preconditions that gave the constitution movement its force rather than treating it as a purely ideological achievement. The gap between constitutional principle and actual political participation (low voter turnout, rural indifference, no compulsory military service until 1854 for all social classes) is stressed by the chapter author Sivert Langholm as a corrective to triumphalist narratives.
