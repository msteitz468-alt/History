# Council of Pisa (1409)

## Narrative

Most cardinals of both camps met at **Leghorn** on **29 June 1408**, swore to reunite the church by securing both claimants’ resignations or, if not, by general council, and summoned a general council at **Pisa** for **25 March 1409**—realizing the earlier solution proposed by Henry of Langenstein and Conrad of Gelnhausen.

[[logan-history-of-the-church-in-the-middle-ages-2013|Logan]] argues historians underrate Pisa: it was Europe’s most impressive assembly since **Fourth Lateran (1215)**—24 cardinals from both obediences, 4 patriarchs, 84 archbishops/bishops plus 102 proxies, 128 abbots plus 200 proxies, friar generals, chapters, 13 universities, ~300 theologians/canonists, and nearly all secular princes (absentees: Scotland, Scandinavia, Naples, Spain loyal to Benedict XIII). From late March to early August 1409 Europe’s focus fixed on Pisa.

On **5 June 1409** Pisa, “acting for the universal church,” declared **Peter de Luna (Benedict XIII)** and **Angelo Corrario (Gregory XII)** schismatics, heretics, and perjurers; deposed and excommunicated them; forbade them to act as pontiff; and declared the Roman see vacant. Logan stresses that the council cited **canonical tradition** (Huguccio 1188 on deposition for heresy/crime; Hostiensis 1270 on corporate authority of the whole church), not a brand-new constitutional principle.

Cardinals of both obediences, as **council-delegated electors**, elected Crete-born Franciscan **Peter Philargi** (cardinal archbishop of Milan) as **Alexander V** by two-thirds of each college after 11 days; authority for election derived from the **council itself**, not from the cardinals as such. The council’s last session (**7 August 1409**) called another council in three years for internal reform.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` prolonged dual papacy after 1378 and failure of *via cessionis*; Gregory XII’s refusal to resign (4 May 1408) and cardinals’ abandonment (11 May).
- `contributed_to: [[conciliarism]]` — practical realization of Langenstein–Gelnhausen teaching that ultimate authority reposes in the community of believers and a council can act without a papal summons in crisis.
- `preceded_by:` failed Savona mutual-abdication meeting (29 September 1407).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` a **third obedience**—Alexander V (1409–10) widely recognized while Gregory held mainly Naples and Benedict mainly Spain.
- `followed_by:` Alexander’s sudden death at Bologna (1410) before entering Rome; succession of **John XXIII** (Baldassare Cossa); incomplete reform agenda that the [[council-of-constance-1414|Council of Constance]] was formally summoned to finish.
- `enabled:` escalation from dual to triple schism until Constance resolved all three lines.

## Historiography

Logan’s rehabilitation of Pisa against “traditional historical wisdom” that treats the Pisan line as disastrous and Alexander as mere anti-pope (as later Vatican lists) is a major interpretive point of his ch. 16. He distinguishes Pisa’s appeal to long canon-law traditions of deposition from the more radical claims of *Haec sancta* at Constance.
