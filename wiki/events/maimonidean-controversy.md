## Narrative

The Maimonidean Controversy was not a single event but a recurring pattern of conflict — a debate between advocates and opponents of philosophical rationalism within medieval Jewish communities, organized around the towering figure of [[maimonides]]. Two major episodes stand out: the controversy of the 1230s centered on Montpellier and Saragossa, and the 1305 Barcelona ban on teaching philosophy to young Jews ([[adamson-philosophy-islamic-world-2016]]).

### Background: The Tibbonid Translation Movement

The controversy's proximate cause was the dissemination of Maimonides' *Guide for the Perplexed* and related philosophical texts to Hebrew-reading Jewish communities in Christian France and Spain — communities that had not previously been exposed to Andalusian Arabic-language philosophy. This was accomplished by the Tibbonid family:

- **Judah Ibn Tibbon**: translated Saadia Gaon, Ibn Paquda, and Judah Hallevi
- **Samuel Ibn Tibbon** (Judah's son): translated the *Guide for the Perplexed* into Hebrew in 1205; also translated Aristotle's *Meteorology*; wrote his own radical Maimonidean philosophical works
- **Moses Ibn Tibbon** and **Jacob Anatoli**: translated Averroes' commentaries on Aristotle

The Tibbonids were, in Adamson's phrase, "like a medieval Jewish version of the Jackson 5, if the Jacksons had been spread over several generations instead of being brothers, and if Jermaine had specialized in rendering Averroes into Hebrew." Their work made Aristotelian and Maimonidean philosophy available to Jews who could read only Hebrew, not Arabic.

### The First Controversy (c. 1232–1233)

Conservative French Jews, alarmed by the spread of Maimonides' rationalist interpretations of Scripture, resurrection, and the afterlife, appealed to the Jewish leader of Provence to stop the dissemination. The central figure of opposition was **Solomon ben Abraham** (not to be confused with Solomon Ibn Adret of the later 1305 controversy). He reserved his attack not on Maimonides himself — widely revered even by his critics — but on those who had translated and spread the *Guide*.

The outcome was extraordinary and tragic: an appeal to *Christian* ecclesiastical authorities, who burned the *Guide for the Perplexed* in Montpellier, probably in 1232 or 1233. The clerics who did so were the same authorities suppressing the Cathar "heresy" in that region. An author writing for the pro-Maimonidean side accused the opponents of having said to the Christians: *"Since you are destroying heretics among you, destroy ours as well."*

The pro-Maimonidean camp was outraged: to have Jews inform on fellow Jews to Christian authorities was unprecedented; to have Maimonides' writings burned was a cultural desecration. A counter-ban was pronounced at Saragossa against Solomon ben Abraham and his allies.

### Naḥmanides as Mediator

Moses ben Naḥman (Naḥmanides / Ramban) was called upon by the anti-Maimonideans to join their side. He refused, instead writing a diplomatic letter of extraordinary balance. He defended Maimonides' purpose — the *Guide* was written to bring back Jews at risk of losing their faith to philosophy, not to corrupt pious Jews with philosophy — and cited Scripture in support: "how many outcasts from faith has he gathered?" (Isaiah 56:8). He also argued that it was legally inappropriate for Jews in one community to regulate the practices of those in another; what served Jews in philosophical Andalusia might not be right for Jews in traditionalist Provence, and vice versa.

Naḥmanides was himself a significant figure in the [[kabbalah]] tradition, showing that conservatism on philosophy and openness to mysticism were compatible positions.

### The Second Episode: Barcelona 1305

Approximately seventy years later, similar tensions erupted again. Solomon Ibn Adret (Rashba), the leading Catalan rabbinic authority, imposed a ban in Barcelona: anyone under 25 was forbidden from reading Greek works on physics or metaphysics. The ban was intended to set an example for Jews in southern France. Instead, it provoked counter-excommunication by pro-philosophy Jews in France, mutual bans, legal confusion, and the eventual expulsion of the Jews from French royal territory by Philip IV ("the Fair") — an ironic outcome given that the controversy began with a plea for Christian intervention.

## Causal Analysis

The controversy has multiple intersecting causes:

- **Intellectual**: Maimonides' synthesis was genuinely radical. His naturalistic account of prophecy, his allegorical reading of resurrection and afterlife, his claim that Jews who attribute a body to God are heretics — each of these provoked real doctrinal anxiety.
- **Social**: The importation of Andalusian philosophy into communities with no Arabic-language philosophical tradition created a cultural shock. Northern French Jews had not grown up with the *convivencia* of Andalusia.
- **Political**: Jewish communities in Christian Europe faced constant pressure to convert; internal philosophical disputes that invited Christian intervention were politically dangerous, as the burning of the *Guide* demonstrated.
- **Structural**: The controversy was structurally analogous to debates between [[al-farabi]]–[[ibn-rushd|Averroes]] rationalists and critics of philosophy in the Islamic world. The same fault line between philosophy as supplement to faith vs. philosophy as rival to faith runs through both traditions.

## Consequence Analysis

Despite the controversy's violence and tragedy, Jewish philosophical culture survived and flourished in both Spain and France throughout the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. [[gersonides]], [[hasdai-crescas]], [[joseph-albo]], and [[isaac-abravanel]] all continued the philosophical tradition. The controversy ultimately produced no lasting resolution of the underlying tension; it "agreed to disagree, but more quietly, simply because they were horrified by the intervention of Christian authorities."

A decade after the burning of Maimonides' books, the Talmud itself was burned in Paris — the greater danger had always come from outside.

## Historiography

The Maimonidean controversy is one of the best-documented internal conflicts in medieval Jewish intellectual history. The primary sources (correspondence, formal declarations, polemical treatises) survive in some quantity. Modern scholarship has debated the exact responsibility for the burning of the *Guide* (Adamson notes this is contested) and the extent to which the controversy reflects a fundamental clash of values versus a more contingent political dispute over communal jurisdiction.
