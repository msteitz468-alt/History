# The Tulip Age (*Lâle Devri*, 1718–1730)

A twelve-year period in Ottoman Istanbul under the grand vizier İbrahim Pasha, following the humiliating Treaty of Passarowitz (1718) with Austria, characterized by elite cultural openness toward Occidental fashions, patronage of poets and scholars, and an ultimately failed attempt to restore the central absolutism against the decentralized order that had prevailed since the seventeenth century. Named for the cultivation of tulips as a fashionable elite pastime. From Marshall Hodgson, [[hodgson-venture-of-islam-v3-1974|*The Venture of Islam*, Vol. 3]].

## Narrative

### Background: Passarowitz and Its Aftermath

After the disaster of the second siege of Vienna (1683) and the subsequent Holy League wars, the [[ottoman-empire]] had been forced into the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699), ceding Hungary. A partial recovery under subsequent [[koprulu-viziers|Köprülü-era]] viziers could not prevent further losses: in 1718 the Treaty of Passarowitz with Austria ceded even Belgrade. This was the most decisive Ottoman territorial defeat yet.

> "Since the unsuccessful campaign against Vienna in 1683 and the disastrous treaty that eventually followed in 1699, the Ottomans had been trying to recover their losses; but despite successes against the Russians and the Venetians, in 1718 the Ottomans had to sign a new and seemingly decisive treaty (at Passarowitz) with Austria, in which even Belgrade was lost."

### The Tulip Age Itself

The twelve years following Passarowitz, under Grand Vizier İbrahim Pasha (son-in-law of Sultan Ahmed III), saw a cultural flowering in Istanbul:

- **Poetry**: poets were patronized who "broke relatively free of the old Persian patterns, though not from the general Ottoman tradition"
- **Decorative arts**: elite home decoration incorporating Occidental details (tulip cultivation, garden design); Hodgson compares this to the simultaneous French upper-class fashion for pseudo-Ottoman elements
- **Architecture**: "even architecture was rather transiently influenced by Italian post-Renaissance styles"
- **Scholarship**: "among the scholars, who were encouraged to inquire broadly, some even took an interest in Occidental learning"
- **Luxury consumption**: wealthy courtiers spent on Occidental manufactures

### The Political Dimension

Hodgson insists the Tulip Age was not merely aesthetic. İbrahim Pasha represented "a court party which hoped to restore the absolutism against the defenders of the civilianized, decentralized society which had come to prevail." The cultural openness to the Occident was linked to a political program: partial Occidentalization of military methods to restore central power.

Specifically, İbrahim Pasha "envisaged having Christian Europeans train certain military corps where the Ottoman forces were most notably weak." This measure met fierce opposition from the forces of the decentralized order:

> "When he attempted to introduce such a measure, the opponents of the absolutism saw their position obviously menaced. Crying in the name of Islam against the infidel (and commercially competitive) luxuries of the court, they struck knowingly against the Occident-based measures which might have increased the court's power."

### The Opposition and Its Composition

The forces opposing İbrahim Pasha's court absolutism comprised two groups:

1. **The Sharī'ah hierarchy** — the trained ʿulamāʾ whose cursus honorum legitimacy depended on an institutional independence that the absolutism threatened
2. **The Janissaries** — by this period including "not only the central garrison at the capital but the greater part of the artisan classes there, as well as their allies in the provinces"

This alliance of popular artisan interests with the learned estate was, Hodgson observes, a deeply ironic transformation: "the latter-day form which the primitive alliance of ghāzīs and 'ulamā' had finally issued in." The original ghāzī-ʿulamāʾ alliance had driven the expansion and Islamization of the Ottoman frontier state; its degenerate descendant now defended the artisan guilds and semi-hereditary Janissary privileges against central reform.

Behind the popular opposition stood "the semi-autonomous *derebey* powers, hereditary and well-entrenched near principalities which among them made up the greater part of the Ottoman territories in Anatolia and Rūmelia."

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by**: [[ottoman-classical-system|decay of the classical Ottoman absolutism]] — İbrahim Pasha's reforms were a response to structural weakness revealed by military defeats
- **caused_by**: Treaty of Passarowitz humiliation — creating both the imperative and the political opening for reform
- **contributed_to**: the Patrona Halil revolt (1730) that overthrew İbrahim Pasha — the cultural and political program of the Tulip Age generated a populist backlash

## Consequence Analysis

İbrahim Pasha was overthrown in 1730 following a defeat by Nādir Shāh (heir to the Safavid empire). "Both the aesthetic and the military fashions of the Occident received a setback at court under the frowns of the party opposed to the absolutism." The attempt to restore the absolutism "fell into temporary abeyance."

Yet the underlying problems remained. By 1739 Belgrade had been regained from Austria, "and this could seem to vindicate the established order" — but the structural weakness continued. "Occidental fashions of one sort or another soon reappeared on a more local basis." The question the Tulip Age had posed — whether the Ottoman state could reform itself through selective adoption of Western techniques without destabilizing its internal social order — would recur with increasing urgency through the nineteenth century.

## Historiography

Hodgson's reading of the Tulip Age as a politically serious reform attempt (not merely cultural dilettantism) is characteristic of his approach: what seems like luxury and fashion reveals, on examination, a structural conflict over the distribution of power. The Occidental fashions were "accidental and quite secondary" to both parties at the time; "yet in retrospect it was decisive" — the first rehearsal of the fundamental dilemma that would define Ottoman history to the end.
