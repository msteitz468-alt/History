# Red River War (1874–1875)

## Narrative

The **Red River War** was the final U.S. Army campaign to crush free [[actors/comanches|Comanches]], Kiowas, Southern Cheyennes, and allies on the southern plains. Following [[events/second-adobe-walls-1874|Adobe Walls (27 June 1874)]] and a summer outbreak that killed an estimated **~190 whites** (hide hunting stopped; frontier "forted up"), Grant on **26 July 1874** put agencies under military control, ending five years of failed peace policy. Davidson at [[places/fort-sill|Fort Sill]] ordered friendly Indians to register by 3 August with daily roll call; the army was free to pursue hostiles to the agency porch. Grant's order: "to subdue all Indians who offered resistance to constituted authority."

### Demographic and force balance (Gwynne)

Late summer 1874: **~3,000 Comanches left** (~2,000 on reservation; ~1,000 untamed with ≤300 fighters); ~1,000 untamed Southern Cheyenne + comparable Kiowa/Kiowa Apache hostiles → roughly **~3,000 hostiles / ≤800 warriors** on the southern plains. Free bands concentrated in the northern Texas Panhandle — [[places/palo-duro-canyon|Palo Duro]] and Red River canyon lands as last hideout near the reservation. Sheridan's method: harass through four seasons, starve, burn villages, take horses.

### Five converging columns (~46 companies, ~3,000 men)

Gwynne: largest force ever dispatched to hunt Native Americans:

| Column | Unit / base | Commander (as framed) |
|---|---|---|
| Fourth Cavalry | Fort Concho / Blanco | [[actors/mackenzie-ranald-s|Mackenzie]] |
| Tenth Cavalry | Fort Sill | Davidson |
| Eleventh | Between | Buell |
| Eighth | Fort Bascom, NM | Price |
| Sixth Cavalry + Fifth Infantry | Fort Dodge | Miles |

Permanent supply bases; adversaries forced to move with families, lodges, and herds.

### Campaign pattern, autumn–winter 1874

Months of hide-and-seek ("Keystone Kops"); few major engagements. Miles 30 August vs Cheyennes near Palo Duro — claimed exaggerated numbers, killed 25, burned large village. Price mid-September 1.5-hour fight screening families. Buell October burned 2 villages, killed 1. Davidson October: 69 warriors + 250 women/children + 2,000 horses surrendered. Miles's infantry November routed Cheyennes out of ammo. Graybeard Cheyennes 6 November ambushed Price detachment (2 killed, 4 wounded). Indians called the rainy season the **"Wrinkled Hand Chase."**

### Decisive blow: Palo Duro Canyon, 28 September 1874

Mackenzie's column (fielded 23 August: 560 enlisted, 47 officers, 3 surgeons, 32 scouts = **642**) feinted up Tule Canyon, night-marched north after a Comanchero tortured on a wagon wheel gave camp location. Dawn 28 September: seven companies at the rim of [[places/palo-duro-canyon|Palo Duro]] — five villages, ~200 lodges (O-ha-ma-tai Comanches majority; Maman-ti Kiowas; Iron Shirt Cheyennes), large horse herd over 3 miles. Maman-ti's medicine said no bluecoat attack → no sentries. Descent by precipitous ~900-ft goat trail; running fight ~4 miles; 4 Comanches killed. Villages burned (robes, meat, white trade goods). **1,424 horses** captured; best to scouts; **more than 1,000 shot** at Tule Canyon until ~3 p.m. — logistical destruction of mobility. Survivors afoot into winter; most straggled to Fort Sill never to roam free again.

### Surrender wave, 1875

Large numbers back in fall 1874; February Lone Wolf + last Kiowas; March **825 Southern Cheyennes**; April Shaking Hand, Hears the Sunrise, Wild Horse with 35 warriors, 140 women/children, 700 horses — disarmed, stock taken, internment west of Fort Sill. Satanta to Huntsville prison (later suicide diving from hospital window); others rail exile to Florida, mostly returned when authorities saw tribes broken.

**Quahadi holdouts:** ~400 including ~100 fighters + large horse herd completely evaded patrols after Adobe Walls by staying SE of Lubbock (Gail/Snyder area). Mackenzie's December 1874 scout: 17 days, 255 miles, deep snow/ice, only 3 Indians killed. 16 March 1875 Mackenzie took Fort Sill command; Sturm mission found Quanah's camp 1 May; **noon 2 June 1875: 407 Quahadis surrendered** at Signal Station a few miles west of Fort Sill with ~1,500 horses and arms — treated better than prior bands (no icehouse raw-meat prison). Independent Comanchería ended.

Sheridan 1875 report: Red River War "the most successful of any Indian campaign in this country since its settlement by the whites." Gwynne: "grand finality… the end of the horse tribes' dominion was the end of the very idea of limitlessness."

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** Strategic decision (Grant / Division of the Missouri) to end dual peace-and-war policy failure after Adobe Walls and the summer 1874 outbreak.
- **contributed_to:** Hide-men's destruction of the southern buffalo herd (ecological warfare by market); Isa-tai/Quanah multi-tribal mobilization that failed at Adobe Walls then spilled into wider raiding.
- **preceded_by:** [[events/medicine-lodge-treaty-1867]]; 1871 Sherman hard line; Mackenzie's learning campaigns (Blanco Canyon 1871; North Fork 1872).
- **part_of:** [[processes/southern-plains-indian-wars]]; terminal phase of [[processes/comanche-empire]].

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** Military end of free Comanche power; reservation confinement at and around Fort Sill; destruction of free horse-herd mobility.
- **enabled:** Cattle ranching and settlement boom on the former Comanche range; Quanah's reservation-era career.
- **contributed_to:** Template of winter/logistical war against plains villages (contrast Custer's 1876 disaster on the northern plains).

## Actors

- **U.S.:** Mackenzie (decisive column); Sherman; Sheridan; Miles, Davidson, Buell, Price columns
- **Indigenous:** Quanah (Quahadi holdout then surrender); Isa-tai (post–Adobe Walls discredit); Kiowa and Cheyenne leaders (Lone Wolf, Iron Shirt, etc.); remaining free bands in canyon country

## Historiography

James L. Haley *The Buffalo War*; army official records; Gwynne's operational narrative (columns, demography, Palo Duro horse slaughter, Quahadi surrender sequence). Scale_consequential rated **civilizational** for the Comanche imperial system (end of a multi-century indigenous power structure on the southern plains), not for the United States as a whole. Avoid U.S.-centric "small Indian war" framing without noting Comanchería's prior imperial role (Hämäläinen). Campaign "few major engagements" pattern can understate the logistical blow of Palo Duro; do not reduce the war to a single battle.

### Fowler, CHNPA I.2 (append — name absent in source)

Fowler **does not name** “Red River War” or Adobe Walls. Her ethnohistorical summary: Comanches and Kiowas camped near the agency for issues then left to raid when supplies ran out; sporadic Army engagements **1868–1875**; 1870s professional non-Indian hunters with repeating rifles killed animals for hides **leaving the meat to rot** and became targets for attacks; by the 1870s the federal government was determined to crush the Comanches and Kiowas and launched **winter campaigns** attacking winter camps and **destroying the families’ provisions**; by **1875 hunger drove the 2,500 Comanches and Kiowas** back to their reservation between the Canadian and Red Rivers, **this time to stay**. Commercial hide hunting and game destruction are also treated at Plains-wide scale (buffalo nearly extinct by the 1880s in U.S. and Canada). Use Gwynne for operational names, columns, and Palo Duro; use Fowler for hunger/winter-camp political economy and the ~2,500 confinement figure.

**Causal Analysis (Fowler-aligned addenda):**
- **contributed_to:** commercial hide hunters; failed agency supply that forced off-reservation raiding.
- **part_of:** [[processes/southern-plains-indian-wars]], [[processes/comanche-empire]], [[processes/great-plains-native-history-1650-1885]].
- **produced:** permanent reservation confinement of free southern equestrian bands.
