# Court-Packing Fight (1937)

## Narrative

On 5 February 1937, fresh from the largest electoral landslide in modern
American history, [[roosevelt-franklin-d|Franklin D. Roosevelt]] sent Congress
the Judicial Procedures Reform Bill: for every sitting federal judge over
seventy who declined to retire, the president could appoint an additional
judge — up to six new Supreme Court justices. The stated rationale was judicial
efficiency; the transparent purpose was to overcome a Court that had struck
down core [[new-deal|New Deal]] statutes (the NRA in *Schechter*, 1935; the
first AAA in *Butler*, 1936). The proposal, prepared in secrecy without
consulting congressional leaders, provoked the fiercest intra-party revolt of
Roosevelt's presidency. Chief Justice Hughes's letter to the Senate demolished
the efficiency rationale; in spring 1937 the Court itself turned — Justice
Roberts's votes in *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish* (minimum-wage law upheld,
March) and the *Jones & Laughlin* decision (Wagner Act upheld, April), the
so-called "switch in time," followed by Justice Van Devanter's retirement —
draining the bill's urgency. Majority Leader Joseph Robinson's death in July
killed it; on 22 July the Senate recommitted the bill 70–20.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by [[new-deal]] — specifically the Court's 1935–36 invalidations,
  which threatened the Wagner Act and Social Security then pending review.
- contributed_to (enabling): the 1936 landslide, which Roosevelt read as a
  mandate; the advanced age of the "Nine Old Men"; the absence of any
  constitutional bar to changing the Court's size by statute.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: the collapse of the New Deal's legislative momentum — the fight
  consumed 168 days, split the Democratic party, and helped forge the
  conservative Southern Democrat–Republican coalition that blocked domestic
  reform from 1938.
- enabled: the constitutional revolution of 1937 nonetheless went Roosevelt's
  way — after the switch and Van Devanter's retirement, the Court never again
  struck down a major New Deal statute, and Roosevelt appointed eight justices
  by 1943. Whether the bill's pressure *caused* the switch is disputed
  (Roberts's *Parrish* vote was cast in conference before the bill was
  announced).
- followed_by: the failed 1938 primary "purge" of conservative Democrats,
  completing the exhaustion of the domestic reform phase.

## Actors

[[roosevelt-franklin-d]] · the Supreme Court (Hughes, Roberts, Van Devanter) ·
Senate Democratic leadership (Robinson; opposition led by Burton Wheeler) ·
[[new-deal]] agencies whose statutes hung on the outcome.

## Contemporary detail (Flynn 1948, attributed)

Flynn's account (Book Two ch. 2) adds contemporary texture, sourced mainly to
Farley and press records: the bill was "cooked up" by Roosevelt, Attorney
General Homer Cummings, and Donald Richberg with no other cabinet or
congressional consultation — House Judiciary chairman Hatton Sumners, leaving
the 5 February briefing, told reporters "This is where I cash in my chips";
the docket-congestion pretext (which Flynn attributes to Rosenman) was
demolished by the Hughes letter; Sumners had already arranged, and told
Roosevelt, that Justices Van Devanter and Sutherland would retire under a
full-pay retirement act, meaning a liberal majority was available without the
bill; and the death of the fight was marked on the Senate floor by Hiram
Johnson's "Glory be to God!" when Logan confirmed the Court was out of the
recommitted bill. These specifics carry Flynn's attribution and should be
checked against Leuchtenburg's fuller scholarly reconstruction where they are
load-bearing.

## Historiography

Mainstream accounts (Leuchtenburg, the fullest student of the episode; Smith,
*[[smith-fdr-2007|FDR]]*) treat it as Roosevelt's worst self-inflicted domestic
defeat, redeemed by the jurisprudential outcome: "he lost the battle but won
the war." Revisionist accounts (Flynn, *[[flynn-roosevelt-myth-1948|The
Roosevelt Myth]]*, Book Two ch. 2, "War on the Courts") read the same facts as
intimidation that succeeded where legislation failed — a deliberate assault on
judicial independence whose defeat in the Senate did not prevent its object
from being achieved. The "switch in time" causation question (external pressure
vs. internal doctrinal evolution) remains a live scholarly debate. See
[[roosevelt-and-new-deal-revisionism]].
