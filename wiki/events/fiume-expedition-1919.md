# The Fiume Expedition (Sept 1919 – Dec 1920)

## Narrative

[[dannunzio-gabriele|Gabriele D'Annunzio]]'s seizure of the disputed Adriatic
port of Fiume with ~2,500 legionaries on 12 September 1919, in defiance of
the Paris peace settlement and the Italian government. Fiume became the
meeting ground of interventionists of Right and Left (socialists, liberals,
and Catholics had negligible influence); the national syndicalists of the UIL
backed the venture immediately. [[de-ambris-alceste|Alceste De Ambris]]
arrived at the end of 1919 — carrying a letter from
[[mussolini-benito|Mussolini]] advising D'Annunzio *not* to march on Rome —
and was appointed secretary for civil affairs on 13 January 1920. Giolitti
ended the "Italian Regency of the Carnaro" by force in December 1920
("Bloody Christmas"); Mussolini, never enthusiastic about the venture,
backed the Treaty of Rapallo and abandoned the cause.

## The Carta del Carnaro

Promulgated 28 September 1920: drafted by De Ambris ("Materially, it was I
who prepared it, but under the inspiration and direction of the
Commandante"), reviewed by D'Annunzio, who added his "philosophic-aesthetic
interpretation of life." Corporatist productionism married to aestheticism:
work as constitutional right and duty; the corporation as bridge between
producer and state; only producers as full citizens; owners confined to
their own corporation. For the national syndicalists it served a dual
purpose — catalyst of national revolution and bulwark against a Soviet-style
one — and it is "claimed by many to be one of the main prefigurations of
Fascist corporatism." It never passed beyond the project stage. After the
promulgation De Ambris wrote to D'Annunzio that "act three of the drama
Fiume had now begun but that the final act would have to take place in Rome."

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

- caused_by: the Adriatic settlement dispute and the interventionist
  "mutilated victory" grievance.
- enabled: [[march-on-rome-1922]] — Fiume rehearsed fascism's style (balcony
  dialogue, Roman salute, legionary ritual) and its corporatist
  constitutional imagination; its failure, with the 1920 factory
  occupations, split [[national-syndicalism]] and pushed fascism rightward.
- contributed_to: De Ambris's break with Mussolini and the
  "two faces of fascism" analysis.

## Historiography

Sternhell/Sznajder treat Fiume as the constitutional laboratory of
[[national-syndicalism]] rather than mere D'Annunzian theatre; other
scholarship (e.g., on D'Annunzio as "first Duce") emphasizes the aesthetic-
performative inheritance over the doctrinal one. Both readings agree the
episode prefigured fascism while remaining outside Mussolini's control.
Griffin ([[griffin-nature-of-fascism-1991]]) goes further than either: it
was D'Annunzio, not Mussolini, who first created "a fully fledged fascist
movement out of Italy's post-war crisis" — the Regency as "a microcosm of
the New Italy," with the Carta del Carnaro's tenth corporation left empty,
"dedicated to the unknown genius, to the appearance of the New Man
(*nuovissimo uomo*)" — palingenetic myth in constitutional form. The
expedition's end on 25 December 1920 is the "Christmas of Blood."

## Griffin (2007): Fiume as Modernist State-as-Art

*Modernism and Fascism* reads D'Annunzio's Fiume (September 1919 to the "Christmas of Blood," December 1920) as the prototype of politics conducted as total work of art: patriotism, militarism, De Ambris's syndicalist corporatism, poetry, music, and spectacular "happenings" fused into a micro-state that dedicated one of its corporations to "il nuovissimo uomo," the newest man. The energy of "combattentismo" it released was ultimately captured by Fascism, not Dannunzianism. (Source: [[griffin-modernism-and-fascism-2007]].)
