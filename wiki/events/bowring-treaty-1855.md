# The Bowring Treaty (1855)

The **Bowring Treaty** (formally the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of Friendship and Commerce, 1855) was the unequal commercial treaty negotiated between Sir John Bowring, British Superintendent of Trade at Hong Kong, and the Siamese court of Rama IV (Mongkut). It opened Siam to British free trade on terms that eliminated Siamese control over customs revenues and abolished most royal trading monopolies. It was the pivotal moment in Siamese integration into the world economy and the trigger for the Chakri monarchy's modernization programme. Source: *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia, Vol. 2*, ch. 3 ([[tarling-cambridge-history-southeast-asia-v2-1992]]).

## Narrative

Siam in the early nineteenth century had managed Western commercial pressure with deliberate wariness. Rama III allowed individual Western merchants to operate and in 1826 made some limited concessions to Henry Burney's mission on behalf of the East India Company. But the predominant Siamese response was not capitulation; it was a determined effort to expand production and commerce for the royal elite's own benefit, co-opting immigrant Chinese — free of corvée obligations — as commercial intermediaries and labourers.

By the 1850s, British free-trade pressure intensified. Bowring arrived in Bangkok in 1855 with a mandate to open Siamese trade on British terms, backed by the implicit threat of the naval power that had humiliated Burma and China. The Siamese court negotiated but ultimately yielded. The resulting treaty fixed import duties at 3 per cent and export duties at graduated low levels, abolished internal taxes on goods in transit, and granted British nationals extraterritorial jurisdiction (exemption from Siamese courts). Similar treaties quickly followed with France and the United States, establishing a de facto treaty-port system in all but name.

## Consequences

The treaty's economic consequences were immediate and massive. Siamese rice exports grew more than twenty-five times between about 1860 and 1930; the area under rice cultivation in the central plain tripled, with Chinese wage labourers and Siamese smallholders together reclaiming and cultivating vast new areas. The Chinese, already welcome in Siam as commercial intermediaries, continued to handle rice trading, transport, and milling; the export business was shared between Western and Chinese brokerage firms. Unlike the more polarized rice frontiers of Lower Burma and Cochinchina, Siam's expansion proceeded with relatively less absentee landlordism, partly because indigenous capital rather than foreign investment drove it and partly because development remained slower and more contiguous with existing settled areas.

Politically, the treaty triggered the Chakri monarchy's characteristic response: internal centralization and administrative modernization to compensate for external concession. Chulalongkorn (Rama V, r. 1868–1910) used the pressure of unequal treaties as a lever to eliminate domestic rivals — hereditary court officials and provincial *chao muang* — and to construct a centralized, Bangkok-based bureaucracy on colonial lines. The *Cambridge History* argues this makes Siam "an extreme example of indirect rule": like a Malay unfederated state, it accommodated Western economic and juridical penetration while retaining formal sovereignty and using modernization to consolidate royal power.

## Historiography

David Wyatt's *Thailand: A Short History* (1984) and *The Politics of Reform in Thailand* (1969) remain the standard accounts. The *Cambridge History* chapter on commerce emphasizes the Bowring Treaty as the inflection point between the pre-colonial and colonial economic orders in mainland Southeast Asia — the moment when the Siamese elite lost the ability to manage the terms of commercial engagement and had to accept integration into the world economy on Western terms. Hong Lysa's *Thailand in the Nineteenth Century: Evolution of the Economy and Society* (1984) provides essential economic depth.
