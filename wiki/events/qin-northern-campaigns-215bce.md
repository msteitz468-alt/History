# Qin Northern Campaigns and Ordos Occupation (215 BCE)

## Narrative

In 215 BCE, the recently unified Qin empire under Qin Shi Huang dispatched General Meng Tian northward to seize the Ordos region — the territory within the great bend of the Yellow River — and expel or subdue the steppe peoples, principally the Xiongnu and allied pastoral groups (Lin Hu, Lou Fan), who had occupied that territory or been driven there by earlier Warring States expansions. The campaign resulted in the military occupation of the Ordos, the establishment of new northern commanderies, and the linking of pre-existing fortification lines — built by the Warring States kingdoms of Ch'in, Chao, and Yen — into the unified barrier that later tradition would call the "Great Wall" (ch'ang ch'eng).

This campaign did not arise in a political vacuum. It was the direct culmination of a century-long pattern of northward expansion by the three northern Chinese states. Ch'in had already pushed into the territory within the Yellow River's Great Bend earlier under King Chao-hsiang (306–251 BCE), after Queen Dowager Hsüan engineered the destruction of the Yi-ch'ü Jung kingdom. Following that conquest, Ch'in had "built a long wall (ch'ang ch'eng) to guard against the Hu [nomads]," extending from the eastern part of the Yellow River loop (Shang commandery) southwest to southern Kansu (Lung-hsi commandery), a total length later estimated at approximately 1,775 kilometers. This earlier Ch'in wall, whose terminal point (at modern T'o-k'e-t'o, Inner Mongolia) controlled a strategically important ford where the Ta-hei River joined the Yellow River, was itself an instrument of occupation: Chinese troops were garrisoned in a territory whose archaeological remains are entirely those of non-Chinese pastoral cultures.

The Meng Tian campaign of 215 BCE extended and completed this project at the imperial scale. The Ordos, the richest horse-breeding ground in Inner Asia within reach of Chinese state power, was militarily seized and colonized. The wall system was then extended and unified across the northern arc from Kansu in the west through Inner Mongolia to Liao-tung in the northeast, incorporating and fencing off territory that had been the homeland of pastoral peoples.

## Causal Analysis

Di Cosmo's revisionist analysis of Chapter 4 of *Ancient China and Its Enemies* directly addresses the causal logic of the Qin northern campaigns. The conventional narrative — that the Qin expedition was a defensive response to nomadic aggression that forced an offensive reversal of prior defensive posture — is rejected on both textual and archaeological grounds.

Textually, the sources record that the northern walls of Chao, Yen, and Ch'in were built not as responses to nomadic attacks on Chinese agricultural settlements, but as infrastructure erected *after* military conquests of northern territory: "the states of Ch'in, Chao, and Yen needed to protect themselves from the nomads only after they had taken large portions of territory from other peoples and had chased the nomads away from their homelands." Di Cosmo states explicitly: "Ch'in's expedition to the north was not a reversal, but a continuation of a policy of colonization and militarization of the north, culminating in the unification of all the extended fortifications built earlier on."

Archaeologically, the territories through which all three Warring States walls ran were inhabited exclusively by non-Chinese pastoral peoples. The Ch'in wall traversed regions whose cultural remains — animal-style bronze ornaments, horse-sacrifice pits, Northern Zone metalwork — belong unambiguously to steppe nomadic traditions. Chinese material culture appears only at the wall fortifications themselves, showing troops stationed in an "otherwise alien cultural environment." The walls "had not been built 'in between' Chinese and nomads, but instead ran through an alien land inhabited by alien groups."

Economic motives reinforced military ones. The drive to control the north was inseparable from the need for horses: Warring States cavalry had created an insatiable demand for the animals that only the pastoral north could supply in quantity. The Ordos in particular was the single most productive horse-breeding landscape accessible to Chinese power. Ssu-ma Ch'ien records that the merchant Wu-chih Lo made a fortune trading silk for horses and cattle under the First Emperor, and that the northern borderlands were "rich in horses, cattle, sheep, felt, furs, tendons and horns" — the commercial vocabulary of an already-integrated frontier economy that the military occupation sought to capture and control.

## Consequence Analysis

The most historically significant consequence of the Qin northern campaigns was arguably unintended: the catalysis of Xiongnu political consolidation. Di Cosmo argues that "the military pressure exercised on the borders by various Chinese generals, such as Ch'in K'ai of Yen, Li Mu of Chao, or Meng T'ien of Ch'in, posed a territorial threat in response to which the nomadic aristocracy was able to increase its social prestige and political power." The Qin campaign was "at the root of the formation of the Hsiung-nu empire." The rapid rise of Modun (Modu) and his creation of the first steppe empire in world history beginning in 209 BCE — just six years after Meng Tian's campaign — is consistent with this reactive-consolidation thesis.

The unified fortification system created by the Qin northern campaign also established the infrastructure that later Han emperors would rely on, develop, and eventually contend with. The commandery system established in the Ordos provided administrative templates for subsequent frontier governance. The long walls became both a physical barrier for future defensive use and, in Chinese historical memory, a symbol of the northern frontier as a primordial civilizational boundary — a framing that, as Di Cosmo notes, accumulated anachronistic defensive meaning during the Ming dynasty and has distorted historical interpretation ever since.

## Actors

**Qin Shi Huang** ([[qin-shi-huang]]) — First Emperor of a unified China, who ordered the Ordos campaign as part of the consolidation of imperial frontiers.

**Meng Tian** — the Qin general who commanded the northern expedition and oversaw the construction or unification of the long wall system. A key figure in the historiographic tradition linking wall construction to defensive imperial order.

**[[qin-dynasty]]** — the political actor whose century-long northward expansion climaxed in the 215 BCE campaign.

**Xiongnu** ([[xiongnu]]) — the pastoral confederation displaced and pressured by Ch'in and Qin expansion; this pressure catalyzed their political unification under Modun.

## Historiography

The interpretation of the Qin northern campaigns has been dominated by the "defensive Great Wall" paradigm, according to which Qin Shi Huang sent Meng Tian northward in response to Xiongnu aggression, and the wall constructed thereafter represented a defensive barrier protecting Chinese civilization from nomadic incursion. This interpretation was already embedded in Ssu-ma Ch'ien's *Shih chi*, written roughly two centuries after the events, at a time when the Han dynasty was experiencing severe Xiongnu pressure — a context that, Di Cosmo argues, projected a binary Chinese/nomad opposition backward into the Warring States period.

Arthur Waldron's work on the Great Wall helped "demythologize" the wall as an eternal Chinese fixture, showing that the continuous barrier attributed to Qin Shi Huang was a political construction joining previously separate Warring States fortifications. Di Cosmo extends this revisionism by arguing not merely that the unified wall was a later construction, but that the individual Warring States walls themselves were offensive occupation infrastructure rather than defensive barriers. The relevant passage of the *Shih chi* — that Ch'in "built a long wall to guard against the Hu" — does not, on Di Cosmo's reading, indicate that nomadic attacks motivated wall construction. Rather, the sequence of events consistently shows walls erected *after* military expansion drove nomadic peoples from their lands: "the logical sequence that can be inferred from the historical events directly related to the walls supports the notion that it was the military expansion by Chinese states that led to the incorporation of new lands, driving out the nomads, after which the walls were built and commanderies established."

Di Cosmo further challenges the "trade and raid" theory often used to explain nomadic pressure on China — the notion that nomadic economic dependence on Chinese grain drove raids that necessitated defensive walls. He argues that archaeological evidence shows farming continued in northern China outside Chinese political control throughout this period, that nomads incorporated cultivators within their own political structures (as among the Scythians), and that it was Chinese states, not nomads, that had stronger commercial interest in the northern pastoral zone. The "trade and raid" model "simply cannot be endorsed on the basis of the available evidence."

The question remains partially open: the specific size and composition of Meng Tian's force, the precise sequence and routing of the 215 BCE campaign, and the degree of resistance encountered are topics where the *Shih chi* remains the primary — and biased — source.
