# The Battle of Hattin (1187)

## Narrative

At the **Horns of Hattin**, west of Tiberias in Galilee, on 4 July 1187,
**[[saladin]]** destroyed the field army of the **Kingdom of Jerusalem**.
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Hans Eberhard Mayer, Ch. 21) treats it as the
catastrophe that ended the first era of the [[crusader-states|crusader states]]. Having
united Egypt and Syria, Saladin invaded the kingdom and besieged Tiberias to draw the
Franks into battle. Against the advice of Raymond III of Tripoli, King **Guy of
Lusignan** led the army on a waterless summer march to relieve the town; harried and cut
off from water, the exhausted Frankish host was surrounded and annihilated on the arid
plateau. The king and most of the nobility were captured (Saladin executed the
Templars, Hospitallers, and Reynald of Châtillon), and the **relic of the True Cross**
was lost.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` **Saladin's unification** of the Muslim Near East and his proclamation of
**jihad** to recover Jerusalem, which for the first time confronted the crusader states
with a single overwhelming power.
`caused_by:` the **internal divisions** of the kingdom and the disastrous decision to
march the entire field army across waterless terrain.
`contributed_to:` the chronic **strategic over-extension** of a Frankish ruling minority
dependent on a single army it could not afford to lose.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **destruction of the kingdom's army**, leaving its cities and castles
defenceless.
`produced:` the **fall of Jerusalem (October 1187)** and the collapse of nearly all the
crusader strongholds, reducing the Latin East to a few coastal points.
`produced:` the **Third Crusade (1189–92)**, which recovered a coastal rump (Acre) but
not the Holy City.

## Significance

Hattin is the decisive military verdict on the twelfth-century crusader states: a single
day that undid ninety years of Frankish conquest and made the recovery of Jerusalem the
recurring, and largely unattainable, goal of crusading thereafter. It marks the
transition from the era of Latin expansion in the Levant to the long defensive struggle
that ended at Acre in 1291.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] embeds Hattin in Saladin’s post-unification politics. By spring 1186 Saladin’s sultanate (Euphrates to Nile) was divided among ambitious kinsmen; *jihad* against Franks had been sidelined so long that even advisers had misgivings — “dusting off” the *mujahid* title served to assert authority over kin and keep troops busy. May 1186 Saladin went to Damascus; Frankish border raids broke an inconvenient four-year truce. Frankish succession (Baldwin IV dead; Sibyl and **Guy of Lusignan** crowned summer 1186) marginalized **Raymond of Tripoli**, who opened Tiberias lands by treaty with Saladin. **Reynald of Châtillon** — Red Sea raids on Mecca-bound pilgrims, Karak caravan attacks during truce — was the hard-liner enemy; when he seized a large Egyptian caravan and refused release, Saladin concentrated forces.

By late June 1187 Saladin at **Kafr Sabt** controlled water and the Saffuriya–Tiberias road; he besieged Tiberias to draw the Franks. His army is “soberly estimated at about **thirty thousand**,” grossly outnumbering the combined Frankish host (medieval numbers “hard to swallow” — hedge). The battle became “at base, a battle about water.” On **4 July** Guy directed the army toward springs near Hattin; trapped at the Horns, only Raymond III and a few were allowed through Muslim lines. Al-Afdal’s eyewitness account records Saladin’s charges, the fall of Guy’s tent, and the sultan dismounting to prostrate and weep for joy.

Aftermath per Cobb: captives ransomed or sold (anecdote: a prisoner traded for a shoe); Guy and many lords ransomed; Reynald refused conversion and was **personally executed** by Saladin (“this man had crossed the line”); Templars and Hospitallers executed as too dangerous (Saladin ransomed them from his own men to kill them); Turcopoles executed as traitors; the **True Cross** captured, paraded upside-down in Damascus, sent by al-Afdal to Baghdad, and later lost (presumed Mongol sack of 1258). A “Dome of Victory” at Hattin was later ruined. Cobb later cautions that Hattin was not Saladin’s finest hour of pure genius — Frankish bungling mattered as much — even as the victory enabled the recovery of Jerusalem and most of Palestine after nearly a century of Crusader occupation (prologue estimate of followers at Hattin: ~**20,000-odd** — hedge against the “thirty thousand” operational figure).

## Historiography

Latin and Arabic sources agree on the catastrophe’s scale while differing on motive and character: Guy’s folly vs Saladin’s *jihad*; Reynald as provocateur or martyr. NCMH IV (Mayer) treats Hattin as the end of the first crusader-state era. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] adds the pre-history of truce politics, Reynald’s Red Sea and caravan provocations, water logistics, al-Afdal’s tent anecdote, and the True Cross’s subsequent itinerary — while hedging army sizes and later arguing Saladin’s post-Hattin military record was often poor even as Jerusalem’s reconquest remained his greatest achievement.
