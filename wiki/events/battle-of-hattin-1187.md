# The Battle of Hattin (1187)

## Narrative

At the **Horns of Hattin**, west of Tiberias in Galilee, on 4 July 1187,
**[[saladin]]** destroyed the field army of the **Kingdom of Jerusalem**.
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (Hans Eberhard Mayer, Ch. 21) treats it as the
catastrophe that ended the first era of the [[crusader-states|crusader states]]. Having
united Egypt and Syria, Saladin invaded the kingdom and besieged Tiberias to draw the
Franks into battle. Against the advice of Raymond III of Tripoli, King **Guy of
Lusignan** led the army on a waterless summer march to relieve the town; harried and cut
off from water, the exhausted Frankish host was surrounded and annihilated on the arid
plateau. The king and most of the nobility were captured (Saladin executed the
Templars, Hospitallers, and Reynald of Châtillon), and the **relic of the True Cross**
was lost.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` **Saladin's unification** of the Muslim Near East and his proclamation of
**jihad** to recover Jerusalem, which for the first time confronted the crusader states
with a single overwhelming power.
`caused_by:` the **internal divisions** of the kingdom and the disastrous decision to
march the entire field army across waterless terrain.
`contributed_to:` the chronic **strategic over-extension** of a Frankish ruling minority
dependent on a single army it could not afford to lose.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **destruction of the kingdom's army**, leaving its cities and castles
defenceless.
`produced:` the **fall of Jerusalem (October 1187)** and the collapse of nearly all the
crusader strongholds, reducing the Latin East to a few coastal points.
`produced:` the **Third Crusade (1189–92)**, which recovered a coastal rump (Acre) but
not the Holy City.

## Significance

Hattin is the decisive military verdict on the twelfth-century crusader states: a single
day that undid ninety years of Frankish conquest and made the recovery of Jerusalem the
recurring, and largely unattainable, goal of crusading thereafter. It marks the
transition from the era of Latin expansion in the Levant to the long defensive struggle
that ended at Acre in 1291.
