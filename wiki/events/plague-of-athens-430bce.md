# The Plague of Athens, 430–426 BCE

## Narrative

The **Plague of Athens** was the epidemic that struck the city in **430 BCE**, in the second
year of the [[archidamian-war|Archidamian War]], and recurred until **426**. It is known
almost entirely from **[[thucydides]]**, himself a survivor, who described its symptoms with
clinical precision expressly so that it might be recognized if it ever returned — "classical
Athenian literature is nearly silent about it." [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 9,
Lewis) treats it as "the disease which changed Athenian history," and judges it unprofitable
to fix a modern label on it, given diseases' ability to change character.

## A consequence of strategy

The plague's virulence owed much to **[[pericles|Pericles']] strategy** itself: the whole
rural population of Attica had been brought inside the Long Walls, packing the city and
Piraeus into improvised shelters in temples and vacant ground — ideal conditions for
contagion arriving by sea. The disease thus struck hardest precisely because of the
evacuation that was meant to preserve the city.

## Scale and effects

Lewis accepts a **death-rate of the order of a quarter** of those affected: Hagnon's force
lost **1,050 hoplites out of 4,000 in forty days**, and Thucydides' figures from the official
lists (**4,400 hoplites and 300 cavalry** dead over the period) justify his view that
"nothing affected Athenian strength so much." The consequences ran deep:

- **Manpower** — a permanent reduction in front-line strength, visible in Athens' later
  reliance on allied land-power.
- **Pericles' death** — the plague carried off **Pericles in autumn 429**, removing the one
  leader Thucydides thought could control the Assembly's moods.
- **Morale and morality** — Thucydides attributes to the plague a **general breakdown of
  moral standards** (men spent freely and lawlessly, expecting to die), and it turned the
  Athenians, for a time, against both the war and Pericles, who was fined and briefly
  deposed.

## Significance

The plague is the great **contingency** of the Archidamian War — the accident that crippled
Athens at the outset and, in removing Pericles, helped open the way to the more volatile
leadership of demagogues like **[[cleon]]** and the eventual disasters of the
[[peloponnesian-war]]. Thucydides' account is also a landmark of **rational, observational
description** of disease and of social collapse.
