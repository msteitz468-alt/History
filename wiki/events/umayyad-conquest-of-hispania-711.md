# The Umayyad Conquest of Hispania, 711

The rapid Arab-Berber conquest of the Visigothic kingdom of Spain (begun 711), which destroyed Latin
Christian rule over most of the Iberian Peninsula, created **[[al-andalus|al-Andalus]]**, and set the
frame for nearly eight centuries of Christian–Muslim contest.

## Narrative

In **711** a largely **Berber** army under **Tariq ibn Ziyad**, dispatched from Umayyad North Africa,
crossed the strait (Gibraltar — *Jabal Tariq*) and destroyed the army of the Visigothic king
**Roderic** at the **battle of the Guadalete (Transductine Promontories)**, where Roderic was killed —
his support fatally weakened by the faction of his rival Wittiza's heirs. The Visigothic state, already
split by a disputed succession, **collapsed with astonishing speed**: Tariq and then the governor
**Musa ibn Nusayr** took **Toledo**, Córdoba, Mérida and Zaragoza within a few years, and by c. 716
most of the peninsula was under Muslim control. Only the mountainous north escaped, where surviving
Christians would found the kingdom of **Asturias**.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the momentum of **[[umayyad-caliphate|Umayyad]]** and Berber expansion across North Africa,
which brought a conquering army to the strait. contributed_to: the **internal division of the
Visigothic kingdom** — a contested succession between Roderic and the partisans of Wittiza, whose
defection at the Guadalete is blamed by the chronicles for the rout — and the brittle, narrow military
base of the Visigothic monarchy, which could not recover after one lost battle and the death of the
king.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **end of the Visigothic kingdom** and the creation of **[[al-andalus|al-Andalus]]**, the
westernmost province of the Umayyad world. enabled: the survival of a Christian rump in **Asturias** and
thus the centuries-long **[[reconquista|Reconquista]]**; and, by the mid-eighth century, the foundation
of the independent **Umayyad emirate of Córdoba** after the Abbasid revolution. The conquest also
brought the Latin Christians of Spain into the first sustained contact with Islam, recorded in the
chronicles of the period.

## Historiography

The conquest is poorly documented from the Christian side: the fullest near-contemporary Latin account
is the **Chronicle of 754** (the "Mozarabic Chronicle"), now ingested in
**[[wolf-conquerors-chroniclers-spain|Conquerors and Chroniclers of Early Medieval Spain]]**, written by
a Christian under Muslim rule who treats the event relatively soberly as a change of regime. Later
**Asturian** chronicles (and the rich but late **Arabic** tradition) reshaped it into a providential or
legendary narrative — the treachery of Count Julian, the rape of Florinda, the conquest as divine
punishment for Gothic sin. Modern debate concerns the scale of the "conquest" (mass invasion vs. a small
force exploiting a collapsing state and negotiating capitulations) and the speed of Islamization.

**Wickham, *Framing* (2005).** For social-economic structure, **711 is not the primary turning-point** in Iberia: ceramic and settlement evidence place deeper simplification earlier (e.g. end of major fineware systems around 500; coastal contraction), and regional difference within the peninsula outweighs the conquest as a single socio-economic hinge. Al-Andalus later shows tax capacity, but that does not restore a late-Roman Mediterranean world-system. Source: [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005]] Chs. 4, 11.
