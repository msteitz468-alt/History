## Narrative

The Treaty of Waitangi (signed 6 February 1840 at Waitangi) is the foundational document of New Zealand's constitutional relationship between the Crown and Maori. Its contemporary political significance is as a framework for redressing historic grievances rather than as a simple historical event.

By the late twentieth century, "About 70 per cent of Maori now live in urban areas, locked into a vicious cycle of poor social conditions, low educational achievements, lower participation in skilled jobs, high unemployment, lower incomes than pakeha, and high crime rates, as well as low self-esteem and poor health." In this context, "the acknowledgment of Maori rights guaranteed in the Treaty of Waitangi resurfaced as a major political issue" — the core source for this account being van Meijl's contribution to [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]].

## The Waitangi Tribunal

"As a result of the increasing politicisation of Maori in the 1960s, partly inspired by the American civil rights movement, the New Zealand government responded in 1975 with the Treaty of Waitangi Act establishing the Waitangi Tribunal." The Tribunal could receive claims from any Maori "prejudicially affected" by Crown policy "inconsistent with the principles of the Treaty" — but initially could only make recommendations, not compel redress, and could not address pre-1975 grievances.

A 1983 ruling vindicated Maori faith in the Tribunal's moral force, finding that the Treaty obliged the Crown to protect Te Ati Awa of Taranaki from industrial pollution of their traditional fishing grounds. In 1985 the Lange Labour government extended the Tribunal's jurisdiction retrospectively to 6 February 1840 — "this clause opened up an important avenue for the redress of grievances."

## Major Settlements

**Sealord deal (1992):** The government paid the Maori $NZ150 million over three years as part of Sealord Products Ltd (largest fishing company in the country), plus 20 per cent of fish species not yet in New Zealand's quota system — in exchange for extinguishing all Maori commercial fishing claims. The deal was declared "the 'full and final settlement' of Maori fishing claims" but was controversial because Maori tribes could not agree on the internal distribution of the settlement.

**Tainui land settlement (1995):** A comprehensive settlement signed in May 1995 on the eve of the 29th anniversary of Queen Te Atairangikaahu's coronation. It included "a formal apology from the Crown, acknowledging that it dealt unjustly with the King Movement in 1863, and it provides for the return of 15,790 hectares of Crown land. This amounts to about 2 per cent of the lands originally confiscated." Value of restored lands estimated at approximately $NZ170 million.

## The King Movement and Tribal Structure

Queen Te Atairangikaahu is "the first woman to lead the King Movement" (Kingitanga). The King Movement's tribal structure facilitated treaty negotiations — "without an effective land-based local organisation, other indigenous peoples — from Hawaiians to Aboriginal Australians — have had difficulties presenting a united front to federal administrations."

## 'Retribalization'

Counter to 1950s predictions that the Maori tribe was "an anachronism [that] would gradually cease to have any effective function," the opposite has occurred: tribes have become "more active and solidary in politics and even in urban social organisation — a phenomenon that Roger Maaka describes as 're-tribalisation'." Some urban Maori living outside their tribal lands have established "socially active tribal satellites in another tribe's territory."

- produced: Waitangi Tribunal as permanent quasi-judicial body

- enabled: Maori retribalization — strengthening of tribal organisation as a political force
- contrasts_with: Australian terra nullius doctrine (overturned by Mabo judgement 1992) — Maori tribal structure provided more effective negotiating basis

## Historiography

The section in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] (van Meijl) represents a mid-1990s snapshot when several major settlements were either just completed or pending. The author treats the treaty process as unfinished, noting that "the Tainui claim is merely the tip of the iceberg of Maori claims. Only the future will tell whether these will be settled to the satisfaction of the Maori population." Subsequent history (the Foreshore and Seabed controversy, the 2007 UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, and multiple further settlements) lies beyond the source's coverage.

## Fischer (2002): the two texts and the long aftermath

[[fischer-history-pacific-islands-2002|Fischer (2002)]] underscores the fatal discrepancy between the treaty's English and Māori versions — the Māori text's *te tino rangatiratanga* promised continued chiefly authority where the English ceded full sovereignty — so that the 45 chiefs who signed at Waitangi "were being deceived, as the British government subsequently enacted only the English-language version," and the 500-plus eventual signatories were likewise unaware (L4518–4524). Hobson proclaimed sovereignty over the North Island "by virtue of cession" and the South Island "by right of discovery" (L4525–4528). Fischer carries the consequences forward: the Northern War (1845–46, Hōne Heke and the felled Russell flagstaff), the New Zealand Wars (1860–81, ending with the guerrilla leader Te Kooti), Chief Justice Prendergast's dismissal of the treaty as "a simple nullity" (L4595–4596), and the colonists' ownership of "22 of the 26 million hectares of New Zealand" by 1890 (L4631–4632) — with only ~45,000 Māori left by 1900 (L4636), even as New Zealand became the first country to grant women the vote (1893, L4661).
