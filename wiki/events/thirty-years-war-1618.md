# The Thirty Years War (1618–1648)

> **⚔️ Military analysis:** see **[[hubs/warfare/early-modern/thirty-years-war/thirty-years-war|Thirty Years' War — military/operational analysis (the Swedish intervention & the military revolution)]]** in the warfare hub.

The **Thirty Years War** was the great central-European conflict of 1618–48 — at once the
last and worst of the European wars of religion and the first general war of the modern
**state system** — fought mainly on the soil of the Holy Roman Empire and ending in the
**Peace of Westphalia (1648)**. It devastated Germany, confirmed the decline of Habsburg
Spain, and is conventionally taken to inaugurate the order of sovereign states. From
[[new-cambridge-modern-history-v4|*The New Cambridge Modern History*, Vol. IV]].

## Narrative

The war grew from the confessional and constitutional tensions left unresolved by the 1555
Peace of Augsburg. It is conventionally divided into **four phases**:

- **Bohemian (1618–25):** the **Defenestration of Prague** (1618) and the Bohemian revolt
  against the Habsburg **Ferdinand II**; the Protestant Frederick V ("the Winter King")
  crushed at the **Battle of the White Mountain (1620)**, followed by the re-Catholicization
  of Bohemia.
- **Danish (1625–29):** Christian IV of Denmark's intervention defeated; the imperial general
  **Wallenstein** dominant; the sweeping **Edict of Restitution (1629)** alarms even Catholic
  princes.
- **Swedish (1630–35):** **Gustavus Adolphus** of Sweden (subsidized by France) wins at
  Breitenfeld (1631) but dies at Lützen (1632); the war turns to stalemate.
- **French (1635–48):** **France** under Richelieu enters openly against the Habsburgs,
  turning the war into a Bourbon–Habsburg power struggle that drags on to exhaustion.

The **Peace of Westphalia (1648)** ended it: a comprehensive settlement recognizing Calvinism
alongside Lutheranism and Catholicism, confirming the near-sovereignty of the German princes,
and acknowledging Dutch and Swiss independence.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **confessional division** of the Empire and the failure of the Augsburg
  settlement to accommodate Calvinism or contain Habsburg Counter-Reformation ambition.
- `caused_by:` the **Bohemian revolt (1618)** against Ferdinand II's centralizing, Catholicizing
  rule — the immediate trigger.
- `contributed_to:` the wider **Habsburg–Bourbon rivalry** (and the Spanish–Dutch war it
  merged with), which turned a German religious war into a European great-power conflict.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the catastrophic **devastation of Germany** (large regional population losses
  from war, famine, and disease — long taken as up to a third in the worst areas).
- `produced:` the **Peace of Westphalia** and, in the conventional reading, the **sovereign-
  state ("Westphalian") international order** — territorial sovereignty, non-interference, and
  the legal equality of states (a framing later scholarship qualifies).
- `produced:` the confirmed **decline of Habsburg Spain** (exhausted, and beset by the 1640
  revolts of Catalonia and Portugal) and the **rise of France** to continental pre-eminence.
- Entrenched **confessional pluralism** and the effective end of the dream of a unified
  Catholic Christendom.

## Actors

- **Emperor Ferdinand II** and the **Catholic League** (Bavaria, Tilly) and imperial general
  **Wallenstein**; the **Protestant Union**, Bohemia, and the Palatinate; **Denmark**
  (Christian IV); **Sweden** (Gustavus Adolphus); **France** (Richelieu); and **Habsburg
  Spain** and the **Dutch Republic**. See [[habsburg-empire]], [[military-revolution]].

## Historiography

Long read as the nadir of German history and the birth of the "Westphalian system," the war
is now treated more cautiously: historians debate the scale of the demographic catastrophe,
question whether 1648 really created modern sovereignty (vs. a gradual development), and stress
its place in the [[seventeenth-century-general-crisis|seventeenth-century general crisis]] and
the [[military-revolution]].
