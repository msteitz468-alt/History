# Assassination of Ōkubo Toshimichi (May 14, 1878)

The assassination of **[[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]]** on the morning of May 14, 1878, by six samurai from Kanazawa was the defining political shock of the late 1870s. It removed the most powerful single figure in the Meiji government, produced the first state funeral in Japanese history, and — through the manifesto the assassins left with his corpse — forced the oligarchy to confront the charge that it governed autocratically and without popular sanction.

## Narrative

The six assassins were members of the **Sankō-ji faction** in Kanazawa (Ishikawa Prefecture), a small, undisciplined group of former samurai who had sympathized with [[saigo-takamori|Saigō Takamori]] during the [[satsuma-rebellion-1877|Satsuma Rebellion]] and blamed Ōkubo specifically for both the 1873 rejection of the Korea invasion and the suppression of Saigō's revolt. Their leader, **Shimada Ichirō** (1848–1878), had traveled twice to Kagoshima to study at Saigō's private schools.

The plot's intellectual architect was **Kuga Yoshinao** (1843–1916), a leader of the *Chūkoku-sha* populist movement who shared the assassins' antipathy to Ōkubo but favored popular-rights rather than samurai violence. He drafted the manifesto that the assassins attached to Ōkubo's body — a document that combined samurai grievances with democratic demands in a hybrid that reflected the political confusion of the moment.

Shimada left Kanazawa for Tokyo on March 25, 1878, composing farewell poems for his wife and children before departing. He was sufficiently certain of his own death to treat the poems as his testament. The conspirators spent weeks in Tokyo studying Ōkubo's schedule, his route to the palace, and the distinguishing features of his carriage. They chose a narrow street he habitually used to avoid crowds.

On the morning of May 14, Shimada sent Ōkubo a warning letter — in keeping with Japanese assassin tradition, he felt unable to act without informing his victim. Ōkubo apparently ignored it.

That morning, Ōkubo had his carriage cleaned in preparation for a dinner reception at the Chinese legation that evening. His usual pistol, normally kept in the carriage, was left behind with a subordinate. This may have cost him his life.

"The assassination was carried out with the smoothness born of intensive preparations. Two men maimed the front legs of the two horses, and the other four killed one of the coachmen before dragging Ōkubo out of the carriage and killing him with singular brutality. The coup de grâce was delivered to Ōkubo's throat with such savagery that the point of the sword penetrated the earth below. After arranging their weapons neatly on the ground beside the corpse, the six men went to the nearby palace to turn themselves in" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

When asked if they had other accomplices, they replied: "Yes, every one of the 30 million Japanese, except for the officials, are our accomplices."

## The Manifesto

The statement the assassins submitted to the police was a remarkable political document. It began with the charge that "the administration and laws neither originate with imperial wishes nor do they stem from public discussion by the people. They are determined exclusively by the assumptions and unilateral decisions of a handful of influential officials." It listed five specific offenses against Ōkubo:

1. Autocratic control of government by halting public discussion and suppressing civil rights
2. Arbitrary issuance of laws and widespread use of intimidation
3. Depletion of public funds for unnecessary construction and useless decoration
4. Internal dissension caused by neglecting patriotic samurai and suspecting loyal subjects
5. Loss of national prestige through mistaken methods of dealing with foreign countries

Keene notes that "on examining the statement left by the assassins, they could not help but agree, however, that at present the laws neither originated with the emperor nor resulted from the deliberations of the people" — the response of Emperor Meiji's jiho (personal advisers) who found themselves in the uncomfortable position of sharing the assassins' diagnosis while deploring their methods ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

## Consequences

The shock of the assassination triggered a significant political response:

**Immediate governance**: Ōkubo was posthumously promoted to minister of the right. **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** was appointed interior minister the following day, ensuring that Ōkubo's developmental and modernizing policies continued without interruption.

**The emperor's engagement**: The emperor was receiving a lecture on the *Analects* from Motoda Nagazane when word arrived. He was "severely shaken." In the weeks that followed, his jiho pressed him toward more personal governance, and he responded: "he would no longer seem indifferent to the running of the state." Among his first acts was a statement of dissatisfaction with the Satsuma-Chōshū monopoly on government positions and an order that "well-qualified men in all parts of the country" be employed.

**State funeral**: The first state funeral in Japan was held for Ōkubo. It was conducted entirely according to Shintō rites — a marker of the prevailing anti-Buddhist sentiment in official circles.

**Constitutional acceleration**: The combination of the assassins' manifesto, the jiho's remonstrances, and the emperor's new attentiveness contributed to the environment in which constitutional reform moved forward. The Meiji Constitution was issued in 1889.

**Long-term structural shift**: Ōkubo's death left a power vacuum that no single figure filled. The oligarchy became more collegial, eventually evolving into the *genrō* system where a small group of elder statesmen advised the emperor collectively. This was a different kind of concentrated power than Ōkubo had exercised, but it was no less concentrated.

## Historical Significance

Ōkubo's assassination is among the most consequential political killings in modern Japanese history. He was killed at the peak of his power, before the institutional legacy he was building (the Meiji Constitution, the cabinet system, the modern bureaucracy) was complete. His death at 47 removed the most coherent strategic thinker from the oligarchy at a critical moment. Itō, who inherited his position and his program, was talented but operated within a more complex, more collegial political environment.

The assassins' manifesto is historically significant not because it was accurate (the charges were partially true at best) but because it articulated the critique of the [[meiji-oligarchs|Meiji oligarchy]] in terms that could not be entirely refuted — and that the oligarchs themselves found uncomfortably resonant. That the critique was shared, in different forms, by samurai reactionaries (who wanted the clock turned back) and populist-rights advocates (who wanted constitutional democracy) reveals the breadth of the opposition the oligarchy had generated by 1878.
