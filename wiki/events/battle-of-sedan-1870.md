# Battle of Sedan (1 September 1870)

## Narrative

A classic day-long pitched battle with an apparently perfect verdict: Moltke's
armies encircled MacMahon's Army of Châlons at Sedan, and by evening Napoleon III
had personally surrendered — "My brother, having failed to die at the head of my
troops, I lay my sword at the feet of Your Majesty. — Napoléon" — to King Wilhelm
of [[prussia|Prussia]]; French prisoners ultimately exceeded 300,000 under a
formal capitulation that, by the international law of the day, should have ended
the war. Tactically it was "one of the rare examples in military history of a
successful encirclement" (Fontane: Moltke "drew the great circle around Sedan"),
compared by contemporaries to Cannae. The *Quarterly Review* called it "perhaps
the greatest event of modern history"; Wilhelm I himself termed it a
"world-historical act," and 2 September was celebrated for two generations as
"Sedan Day" — the German Empire dating its founding, uniquely, to a pitched
battle (against America's legal document and France's insurrection).

**The verdict refused.** The defeat was received as "a defeat for the emperor,
not a defeat for France": Paris rioted, Jules Favre demanded *résistance à
outrance*, and the Third Republic was proclaimed within days; the Government of
National Defence fought on. The war dragged through months of
horror — the sieges of Paris, Strasbourg, and the provincial cities; a bitter
franc-tireur [[guerrilla-warfare|guerrilla campaign]]; German reprisals — ending
only when Moltke had, in Whitman's phrase, "marched all the way to the sea" like
Sherman before him.

## Causal analysis

- part_of: the Franco-Prussian War — itself, for Whitman, "the greatest of
  duel/point-of-honor wars," sparked by the Hohenzollern candidacy for the vacant
  Spanish throne and Bismarck's edited Ems Dispatch (1870); caused_by the
  operational encirclement of the Army of Châlons.

## Consequence analysis

- produced: fall of the Second Empire; capture of the emperor; ultimately German
  unification (January 1871, Versailles).
- For Whitman ([[whitman-verdict-of-battle-2012]]) Sedan is the decisive proof of
  the [[death-of-pitched-battle]]: a battle wearing *all* the legal trappings of
  decision — capitulation, surrendered sovereign, proofs of victory — whose
  verdict a sovereign *people* simply refused to accept. A monarch could cede a
  wager; a nation could not. The contrast with Waterloo (1815), its formal twin,
  marks the death of the [[jus-victoriae]].

## Actors

Moltke and Wilhelm I ([[prussia]]) vs. Napoleon III and MacMahon (France).

## Historiography

Military historiography treats Sedan as the triumph of Prussian operational art
(railways, general staff, envelopment). Whitman's legal reading shifts attention
to its *failure*: the first great European battle whose formally perfect verdict
did not bind. Michael Howard's *The Franco-Prussian War* is the standard account
of the people's-war phase that followed; compare [[peoples-war-clausewitz]] and
[[american-civil-war-1861-1865]].
