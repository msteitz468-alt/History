## Narrative

The Indonesian Revolution began with the proclamation of independence by [[sukarno]] and Mohammad Hatta on 17 August 1945 — two days after Japan's surrender. It ended with Dutch recognition of Indonesian sovereignty at the Round Table Conference (December 1949). In between lay four years of armed struggle, diplomatic negotiation, internal political conflict, and international pressure.

The revolution was, in the *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia*'s formulation (Tarling, ed., Vol. 2), "a seamless web of political and armed struggle." Unlike the Vietnamese revolution, it was completed "at one go" — there was no pause with an expectant sequel. "A sovereign Republic of Indonesia — the antithesis of the Netherlands East Indies — was firmly established."

**Independence declaration**: The proclamation came in the interval between Japan's surrender and the arrival of Allied forces. This window was critical: by establishing the Republic of Indonesia as a political fact before the Dutch returned, the nationalists forced any subsequent negotiation to begin from the premise of an existing government rather than a colonial territory awaiting restoration.

**The Sukarno-Hatta partnership**: The revolution's political coherence rested on the complementarity of its two leaders. Sukarno — the orator, mobilizer, and mass-politics specialist — concentrated on propaganda, guerrilla warfare encouragement, and maintaining revolutionary fervor. Hatta and Prime Minister Sjahrir — the diplomat and economist — handled negotiations with the Dutch and the British occupation forces, concluding the Linggajati Agreement (1947) and the Renville Agreement (1948). The *Cambridge History* notes that Sukarno's collaboration with the Japanese occupation had "tainted" him in international eyes, making it "not practical for Sukarno to deal with foreign powers."

The partnership also bridged Indonesia's social divisions: "Sukarno represented the Javanese syncretistic religious strain; Hatta was Sumatran and more emphatically a Muslim." Their co-operation was itself "a significant political structure that fashioned consensus during the revolution."

**Dutch Police Actions**: The Dutch launched two military strikes against the Republic. The first Police Action (July 1947) seized major economic resources in Java and Sumatra. The second Police Action (December 1948) was more ambitious — it resulted in the arrest of Sukarno and Hatta and the capture of the Republican capital Yogyakarta. The second Police Action backfired: it provoked international condemnation, triggered American threats to withhold Marshall Plan aid to the Netherlands, and demonstrated that the Dutch lacked the political will to sustain a prolonged colonial reconquest. Guerrilla resistance continued under army commander General Sudirman.

**Internal challenges**: The revolution faced multiple internal challenges simultaneously. A communist coup attempt occurred in 1946. The PKI launched the Madiun Revolt in east Java in September 1948 — the *Cambridge History* describes this as "a stab in the back of the republic at its hour of need" that inflicted casualties on the Indonesian army and produced the army's "extremely unfavourable image of militant Islam." The Darul Islam movement launched a rebellion in west Java to establish an Islamic state; this was not crushed until 1962. Social revolutions occurred in parts of northern Sumatra and Java, where traditional aristocrats who had previously enjoyed Dutch support were overthrown.

**Resolution**: International pressure, particularly from the United States, forced the Netherlands to the negotiating table. The Round Table Conference of November–December 1949 resulted in Dutch recognition of full sovereignty over all of the former Netherlands East Indies except West New Guinea (West Irian), which the Dutch retained until 1963.

## Causal Analysis

The revolution was directly caused by the Japanese occupation's dismantling of Dutch colonial authority and creation of indigenous military capacity. The occupation had recruited, armed, and trained Indonesian paramilitaries (PETA); it had imprisoned Dutch civilians; and it had punctured the myth of European racial superiority. The power vacuum of August 1945 was caused_by the abrupt Japanese surrender.

The revolution's success owed much to the Cold War context: American hostility to prolonged Dutch colonialism (which was seen as weakening the Netherlands within NATO and creating conditions favorable to communist organization) contributed_to the diplomatic pressure that ended Dutch resistance.

## Consequence Analysis

The revolution produced the Republic of Indonesia as a sovereign state — the fourth most populous country in the world. Its political consequences extended beyond formal sovereignty:

1. **Army as political actor**: The *Cambridge History* notes that "in the dark days of 1948, during the Second Police Action, the army was the only source of hope and national authority in the republic." This established the army's self-conception as the guardian of the nation — a political role (*dwifungsi*) that would ultimately produce Suharto's New Order.

2. **Unresolved Islamic question**: The Muslim concession on Pancasila (accepting "belief in one God" rather than explicit Islamism) resolved the constitutional question but not the underlying political tension. The Darul Islam rebellion represented the irreconciled alternative.

3. **Model for decolonization**: The Indonesian revolution demonstrated that a colonial war could be won through a combination of armed resistance and international diplomacy. It preceded the Bandung Conference (1955) by six years and became a model for non-aligned anti-colonial movements globally.

## Historiography

The Indonesian revolution has a rich historiography. Benedict Anderson's *Java in a Time of Revolution* (1972) is the foundational English-language study, emphasizing the pemuda (youth) revolutionary movement and the social transformations of the Japanese occupation period. The *Cambridge History* (1992) focuses on political structures — the Sukarno-Hatta partnership, the army's emerging political role, the PKI's positioning — rather than social history. Indonesian nationalist historiography has emphasized the revolution's heroic dimensions; critical scholarship has attended to social violence, the coercion involved in maintaining revolutionary unity, and the political consequences of the communist suppression of 1948.

**Collection coverage**: The *Cambridge History of Southeast Asia* (Vol. 2) provides the most systematic coverage in the Bibliotheca Alexandrina for this event. Benedict Anderson's *Java in a Time of Revolution* is referenced but not yet ingested.
