# The Sicilian Expedition, 415–413 BCE

## Narrative

The **Sicilian Expedition** was the Athenian invasion of Sicily (415–413 BCE) that ended in
total disaster — the turning point of the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]].
[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 10, A. Andrewes) follows Thucydides (Books VI–VII), who
made it the centerpiece of his history. With the **Peace of Nicias** unravelling, an appeal
from **Segesta** (against Selinus and Syracuse) gave Athens a pretext; the Assembly, urged by
**[[alcibiades]]** and against the warnings of the cautious **Nicias**, voted a great
armament under three generals — **Nicias, Alcibiades, and Lamachus**.

## The recall of Alcibiades

On the eve of departure the affair of the **mutilation of the Hermae** and the alleged
profanation of the **Mysteries** convulsed Athens. Alcibiades, implicated, sailed with the
fleet but was soon **recalled to stand trial**; instead he **fled to Sparta**, where he
advised the very measures that would ruin Athens — the sending of a Spartan general to
Syracuse and the fortification of **Decelea** in Attica. The expedition thus lost its most
energetic commander and gained a formidable enemy.

## The siege of Syracuse

The Athenians besieged **[[syracuse]]**, building walls of circumvallation across the heights
of **Epipolae**. They came close to sealing the city off, but Nicias' hesitation — and the
death of the aggressive **Lamachus** — gave Syracuse time. The Spartan **Gylippus** slipped
in with reinforcements, built a **counter-wall** that broke the Athenian siege, and turned
the tide. A despairing letter from Nicias (pleading illness and asking either recall or
massive reinforcement) brought a **second great armament** under **Demosthenes and
Eurymedon** (413).

## The catastrophe

Demosthenes' bold **night assault on Epipolae** failed in confusion; thereafter Athenian
fortunes collapsed. The Syracusans, coached by Gylippus and the Corinthians, **refitted their
fleet** with reinforced rams for the confined waters of the **Great Harbour** and won a
series of naval battles. A **lunar eclipse** (27 August 413) made the superstitious Nicias
delay the retreat fatally. With the harbour blocked, the final attempt to break out failed,
and the army — some 40,000 — fled overland, harried at every step. **Demosthenes
surrendered**; **Nicias** surrendered at the slaughter in the bed of the river **Assinarus**.
Both generals were **executed** against Gylippus' wishes; the survivors were packed into the
Syracusan **stone quarries** to die of exposure, thirst, and starvation.

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: the collapse of the Peace of Nicias, Athenian over-ambition, and Alcibiades'
advocacy. produced: the **destruction of a vast fleet and army** and the treasury that
funded them; the loss of Athens' aura of invincibility; the **revolt of the allies** and the
intervention of Persia; and the **occupation of Decelea**, which strangled Attica. It is the
hinge after which, Andrewes notes, Athens' chance of outright victory "virtually disappeared"
— the road to the **[[fall-of-athens-404bce|fall of Athens]]**.

## Historiography

Thucydides made the expedition his tragic masterpiece; his analysis blames the **quarrels at
home** (the exile of Alcibiades) and the people's hope of "large funds for public pay" as
much as the field commanders. The Melian Dialogue immediately precedes it in his text, and
critics have long debated whether he intended the juxtaposition of **[[siege-of-melos-416bce|
Melos]]** and Sicily as moral commentary (see [[classical-greek-historiography]]).
