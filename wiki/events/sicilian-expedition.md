# The Sicilian Expedition, 415–413 BCE

## Narrative

The **Sicilian Expedition** was the Athenian invasion of Sicily (415–413 BCE) that ended in
total disaster — the turning point of the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]].
[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 10, A. Andrewes) follows Thucydides (Books VI–VII), who
made it the centerpiece of his history. With the **Peace of Nicias** unravelling, an appeal
from **Segesta** (against Selinus and Syracuse) gave Athens a pretext; the Assembly, urged by
**[[alcibiades]]** and against the warnings of the cautious **Nicias**, voted a great
armament under three generals — **Nicias, Alcibiades, and Lamachus**.

## The recall of Alcibiades

On the eve of departure the affair of the **mutilation of the Hermae** and the alleged
profanation of the **Mysteries** convulsed Athens. Alcibiades, implicated, sailed with the
fleet but was soon **recalled to stand trial**; instead he **fled to Sparta**, where he
advised the very measures that would ruin Athens — the sending of a Spartan general to
Syracuse and the fortification of **Decelea** in Attica. The expedition thus lost its most
energetic commander and gained a formidable enemy.

## The siege of Syracuse

The Athenians besieged **[[places/syracuse]]**, building walls of circumvallation across the heights
of Epipolae. They came close to sealing the city off, but Nicias' hesitation — and the
death of the aggressive Lamachus — gave Syracuse time.

## The besiegers besieged (414–413)

**[[actors/nicias|Nicias]]** moved the Athenian base to Plemmyrium for sea escape after Labdalum fell. Gylippus pressed the counterwall using the Athenians' own stones and forced battle. The decisive engagement on Epipolae saw Syracusan cavalry and javelin men rout the Athenian left; "the Syracusans built their counterwall across the line of the Athenian siege wall." The Corinthian squadron slipped in unopposed, supplying over 2,000 men. "All hope of enclosing Syracuse and starving it into surrender with their present forces was now gone."

Nicias sent his letter to Athens in fall 414. He described the reversal without accepting responsibility and warned the Athenians “must either recall the force that is here or reinforce it with another just as large, infantry and a fleet and a great deal of money” (7.15). He cited fleet deterioration and supply vulnerability but omitted his own delays, failure to intercept Gylippus, neglect of Epipolae, and move to untenable Plemmyrium. Kagan judges the letter self-serving: the lion’s share of responsibility rested with “Nicias’ lethargic, overconfident, and careless leadership.”

The Athenians confounded expectations by voting massive reinforcements while retaining Nicias (Menander and Euthydemus as temporary colleagues; [[actors/demosthenes-athenian-general|Demosthenes]] and Eurymedon as regular generals). They acted with “constancy and determination to carry through what they had begun.” Nicias was viewed as a “talisman of victory” — dignified, prudent, never defeated, his name linked to nike.

Meanwhile Sparta, freed of treaty guilt by Athenian raids on Laconia, sent King Agis to fortify Decelea (March 413): “Instead of a city Athens became a garrisoned fortress” (7.28.1). Twenty thousand slaves deserted; supplies rerouted expensively. Gylippus captured Plemmyrium in a combined land/sea assault (naval diversion drew the fleet; land forces took the forts). Athenians won at sea but lost critical supplies and morale: “the loss of Plemmyrium brought bewilderment and discouragement to the army” (7.24.3). Almost all Sicily now joined Syracuse.

Syracusans adopted reinforced catheads (epotis) for head-on ramming in confined waters. In the Great Harbor they won a key victory through surprise re-attack while Athenians were ashore eating; javelin-throwers in small boats under the oar banks; “dolphin” cranes sank pursuers. Seven Athenian ships sunk; Syracusans dominated the harbor.

**[[actors/demosthenes-athenian-general|Demosthenes]]** arrived with ~73 ships and ~5,000 hoplites. He planned an immediate night assault on the Epipolae counterwall (early August). Initial success: Euryalus fort surprised and taken, sections of counterwall torn down. Pursuit in moonlight led to chaos. Unfamiliar terrain, indistinguishable Dorian paeans, and Boeotian counter-attack turned victory to rout. “Finally, when they had once been thrown into confusion, they attacked one another in many different parts of the battlefield, friends against friends and citizens against fellow citizens... they even came to blows” (7.44.8). Two to two-and-a-half thousand Athenians killed — “the greatest disaster yet suffered by the Athenians.” All hope of quick victory abandoned.

## The catastrophe and destruction (413)

Demosthenes urged immediate withdrawal while naval superiority lasted. Nicias refused, citing possible Syracusan money exhaustion (2,000 talents already spent) and a supposed internal party for surrender. His true motive: fear that returning soldiers would accuse the generals of bribery and that he would “be put to death unjustly on a disgraceful charge by the Athenians.” He preferred to “meet his own death himself at the hands of the enemy” (7.48.4). A compromise move to healthier ground was rejected.

On 27 August 413 a total lunar eclipse occurred. The soothsayer required waiting “thrice nine days.” Nicias accepted the omen as unfavorable and refused further discussion of departure.

Syracusans blocked the Great Harbor entrance with chained boats and boards. The Athenian breakout attempt in the final naval battle failed in the confined space. Grappling “iron hands” were countered by hides; missile fire was inaccurate from tossing ships; noise drowned commands. The fleet was routed. Morale collapsed; the men refused to re-embark and insisted on escape by land.

About 40,000 (half soldiers) marched for Catana. “They looked like nothing more than a city, and one of considerable size, sneaking away in flight after being reduced by a siege” (7.75.5). Constant harassment by cavalry and light troops; blocked at the Acraean Bald Rock ravine and Monte Climiti. Hermocrates’ trick (false warning via pretended traitors) plus Athenian fear delayed the march, allowing full blocking of routes.

Demosthenes’ division was trapped in an olive grove and surrendered (~6,000); he was prevented from suicide. Nicias’ force reached the Assinarus. Discipline dissolved in thirst and missiles: “Since they were forced to go forward in a close mass they fell on top of and trampled one another; some were killed immediately, impaled on their own spears... The Syracusans stood along the opposite bank of the river, which was steep, and threw missiles down on the Athenians below, most of them drinking greedily and heaped together in disorder in the hollow bed of the river. The Peloponnesians also came down and butchered them, especially those in the river. And the water immediately became spoiled, but it was drunk, nonetheless, though it was muddy and full of blood, and most of them even fought over it” (7.84). “All that remained of the great Athenian army was destroyed at the Assinarus.”

Nicias surrendered to Gylippus. The generals were executed. Survivors (over 7,000) were crowded into Syracuse’s stone quarries on minimal rations; most died or were sold after months of suffering. Thucydides called it “the greatest action of all those that took place during the war... the most glorious for those who won and the most disastrous for those who were defeated... total destruction... only few of many came back home” (7.87.5-6).

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: the collapse of the Peace of Nicias, Athenian over-ambition (urged by [[actors/alcibiades]] despite [[actors/nicias]] warnings), and Nicias' repeated failures to press the siege or intercept relief. produced: the **destruction of a vast fleet and army** (over 200 triremes lost overall; ~40,000 men in the final phase) and the treasury that funded them; the loss of Athens' aura of invincibility; the **revolt of the allies** and the intervention of Persia; and the **occupation of Decelea**, which strangled Attica. It is the hinge after which Athens' chance of outright victory "virtually disappeared" — the road to the [[fall-of-athens-404bce|fall of Athens]]. Kagan stresses that only Nicias’ failed rhetorical escalation of the original modest force and his later refusal to withdraw turned a containable reverse into total disaster.

## Historiography

Thucydides made the expedition his tragic masterpiece; his analysis blames the **quarrels at home** (the exile of Alcibiades) and the people's hope of "large funds for public pay" as much as the field commanders. [[actors/kagan-donald|Kagan]] provides detailed causal reconstruction emphasizing individual agency: Nicias' timidity, overconfidence, and reputation-driven refusal to retreat after the 414 reversal and the Epipolae defeat; the decisive role of Syracusan cavalry and tactical adaptation in confined waters; and the fatal delay from the eclipse. Kagan's narrative underscores that the Athenians showed determination rather than democratic fickleness in reinforcing, yet Nicias' personal calculations doomed the force. Contrasts with Thucydides' more structural/tragic framing.

## Kagan Analysis (range 17001-21000): Causes, Planning, Home Front, Early Operations

**Causes and Athenian Intentions (Kagan):** The impetus came from Segesta and Leontini appeals against Selinus and Syracuse. Segestans argued Syracuse would dominate Sicily and aid Peloponnesians: “If the Syracusans, who had depopulated Leontini, were not punished and, after destroying their allies who were still left, took power over all of Sicily, there was the risk that at some time in the future, as Dorians to Dorians and as kinsmen and colonists of the Peloponnesians, they might send them help with a great force and help destroy the power of Athens” (6.6.2). Thucydides saw pretext: “The truest explanation” was that “they longed for the rule of the whole island” (6.6.1). Kagan counters that Athenians showed caution: “They carefully sent ambassadors ‘to see if the money was there... and, at the same time, to discover how the war against the Selinuntians was going’ (6.6.3).” The 60 talents convinced them; initial vote for 60 ships matched the 424 expedition, indicating “limited intentions.” Growth of Syracusan power may have broadened goals, but “An expedition of the scope voted by the assembly, in any case, could not lead to the kind of catastrophe that might threaten Athenian survival.”

**The Debate and Force Expansion (Planning):** In March 415 assembly voted 60 ships under Alcibiades, Nicias, Lamachus “to settle affairs in Sicily in whatever way they judged best for Athens” (6.8.2). Nicias opposed: “We must not... reach out for another empire until we have made the one we have secure” (6.10.5). He attacked Alcibiades personally. Alcibiades defended policy and life, arguing Sicily unstable, Spartans weak (“Nothing short of an enormous disaster at sea could change the strategic balance”), necessity to aid allies, and Athens’ active nature: “A city that is active would quickly be destroyed by a change to passivity” (6.18.7). He claimed victory in Sicily would bring control of all Greece. Nicias, seeing eagerness grow, exaggerated requirements to deter: proposed “one hundred triremes, five thousand hoplites, and a proportional number of light-armed troops.” The assembly accepted and gave “full powers.” Kagan: “Against his intentions, Nicias managed in the second assembly to convert an expedition of moderate size with limited goals and liability into a vast armada burdened with great ambitions and expectations, whose failure could bring disaster... Without his intervention they would undoubtedly have sailed against Sicily in 415, but there could have been no chance of their embarking on a major catastrophe.”

**Home Front and Sacrilege:** Mood was “A passion came upon all of them equally to sail off...” (6.24.3). On June 7, 415, Hermae mutilated across city (faces and phalluses). “It seemed to be an omen for the voyage and to have been done on behalf of a conspiracy to make a revolution and destroy the democracy” (6.27.3). Profanation of Eleusinian Mysteries also charged against Alcibiades and friends. Investigation produced panic; torture of citizens authorized. Informers included Teucrus (named others, not Alcibiades), false Diocleides (300 conspirators claim), Andocides (his hetairia responsible for Hermae under Euphilitus/Meletus). Kagan: “It can be no coincidence... that the deed was executed just before the expedition to Sicily was set to depart, and there is no doubt that it was politically motivated.” Likely aimed at Nicias’ superstition to halt voyage. “The unexpected additional outrages destroyed whatever chances the bizarre plot may have had for success.” Alcibiades sailed but was later recalled by Salaminia; fled at Thurii. Convicted in absentia, property confiscated, name cursed.

**Early Operations and Strategy (415):** Fleet left mid-June: “the most expensive and glorious armament coming from a single city with a purely Greek force that put to sea up to that time” (6.31.1). 134 triremes (60 Athenian), ~5,100 hoplites (1,500 Athenian), ~1,300 light-armed, only 30 cavalry horses. At Corcyra divided command; crossed to Italy. Rhegium declared neutrality, allowed only beach camp and market: “The likeliest explanation is their perception of the vast size of this second expedition, which made it seem that the Athenians had come to conquer...” Segesta produced only 30 talents. At Rhegium, strategy council: Nicias proposed minimal show at Selinus/Segesta then home (“they should not endanger the state by spending its own resources” 6.47). Alcibiades: win Sicilian Greek cities and Sicels as allies, then attack Syracuse/Selinus. Lamachus: sail directly to Syracuse, fight unprepared or seize supplies outside walls; occupy Megara Hyblaea as base. “Lamachus... lent his support to the scheme of Alcibiades, which became the Athenian strategy.” 

Naxos received them; Catana taken by trick. Ten ships scouted Syracuse harbor (empty fleet), issued ultimatum. Syracusans debated: Hermocrates urged allies from Carthage, Corinth, Sparta and fleet to Italy; Athenagoras claimed Athenians not coming or plot to overthrow democracy. Syracusans unprepared. Athenians raided Syracusan territory; some light troops killed by cavalry “an omen for the future.”

**First Attack on Syracuse (415 Battle) and Early Siege Preparations:** To confront, Athenians tricked Syracusans into marching to Catana. Landed unopposed south of Anapus River opposite Olympieum temple. Fortified position using terrain vs cavalry. Next day battle: Athenians ~8 deep (Argives/Mantineans right, Athenians center, allies left), reserve hollow square. Superior discipline prevailed despite Syracusan depth and 1,500 cavalry; rain/thunder aided. Argives and Athenians broke enemy; pursuit checked by cavalry. “For the Athenians it was a tactical victory without strategic result.” They set trophy, returned dead, sailed back to Catana. Thucydides: lateness of season, need supplies, money, “send to Athens for cavalry” (6.71.2). Contemporaries blamed “Nicias-delays.” Kagan: “Nicias’ caution in response to the lack of cavalry was not unreasonable... But... Cavalry was essential to the capture of Syracuse. Had it been available from the first, the Syracusans would have been forced to surrender... The lack of provisions for cavalry is particularly astonishing in that Nicias himself had emphasized its importance...” (6.20.4). He omitted cavalry from force list and never remedied.

Syracusans reformed: armed poor as hoplites, compulsory training, reduced generals to 3 (incl. Hermocrates) with full powers; extended walls, sent for Peloponnesian help. Athenians wintered at Naxos (failed at Messina due to Alcibiades’ warning). Diplomatic efforts (Camarina neutral due to size fears; some Sicels joined; Carthage appeal failed). 

**414 Early Siege:** Cavalry + money arrived spring 414. Athenians seized Epipolae (unguarded), built Labdalum fort, then “The Circle” at Syce. Began northern wall to Trogilus. Syracusans built counterwall of stone/timber; Athenians destroyed it in midday surprise assault by 300 hoplites + light troops (Nicias/Lamachus followed). “The pursuing forces managed to get in through the gate, but there were too few of them...” Counterwall demolished. Then southern wall toward Great Harbor begun. In marsh battle for it, Lamachus killed while steadying line (“The brave and bold Lamachus... died fighting”). Nicias ill. Syracusans briefly captured undefended Circle section but repulsed by fire and fleet arrival. Athenians neared completing double wall; Syracuse “no longer thought they could win the war” (6.103.3). Rumors of surrender.

Gylippus (with Gongylus) arrived via Himera with ~3,000 infantry + 200 cavalry (Syracusans had been about to surrender; news reversed it). Gylippus seized Labdalum, began counterwall north. Nicias, “Sick and in pain... worried chiefly about the safety of his forces and their escape,” moved base to Plemmyrium forts south of harbor entrance (disadvantages: water/firewood distant; “the crews began to deteriorate”; divided forces). “The time and manpower used on the second southern wall... were resources the Athenians could not afford...”

Kagan judges Nicias tactically sound on cavalry delay post-battle but strategically at fault for never securing cavalry despite warnings, overconfidence vs Gylippus (“Nicias did none of these things [intercept, complete walls, guard Euryalus], with disastrous results”), and Plemmyrium move.
