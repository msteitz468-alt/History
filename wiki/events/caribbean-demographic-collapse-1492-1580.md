## Narrative

The native populations of the Caribbean were the first peoples of the Americas to bear the full weight of European invasion. From Columbus's landfall in October 1492 through the end of the sixteenth century, the indigenous societies of the Greater Antilles, the Lesser Antilles, and the adjacent mainland (Tierra Firme) were engulfed in a catastrophe with few parallels in recorded history. Within a single lifetime, the densely settled islands of the Greater Antilles were effectively depopulated; the Lesser Antilles and mainland littoral suffered severe loss and forced transformation; and the entire sociopolitical order of the pre-Columbian Caribbean was destroyed or irreversibly altered.

Neil Whitehead (Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas, Vol. III) frames the period as one of "crises and transformations": not simply destruction, but the emergence of radically new — and deeply constrained — historical trajectories for surviving Amerindian societies.

### Hispaniola (Española), 1492–1548

The catastrophe unfolded fastest on Hispaniola. Columbus's second voyage (1493–94) inaugurated a colonial regime that escalated into brutal military campaigns against indigenous polities. The decisive moment came in 1495 at the valley of Magua (La Vega Real), the largest and most densely populated native province on the island. As Las Casas recorded:

> "The Christians fell upon them suddenly, at midnight, for the Indians do nothing by night, neither attack or even prepare for war — they did not even have time to get their weapons. All in all, they could do nothing against the Spaniards, who did them great damage. The Christians captured the King Guarionex and many others; they killed many of the captured leaders . . . burning them alive."

The paramount cacique Guarionex briefly served as a mediator of Spanish demands for food, labor, and gold; by 1497, amid famine and the first epidemic outbreaks, resistance collapsed and the caciques were crushed or executed. By 1500 the complex native polities of Hispaniola had ceased to operate autonomously. The *repartimiento* of 1514 found perhaps 30,000 indigenous people remaining from a pre-contact population estimated at one million or more. A contemporary chronicler observed that by 1548 barely 500 persons remained.

### The Other Greater Antilles, 1508–1530s

The pattern repeated itself across the island chain with merciless speed:

- **Puerto Rico**: Juan Ponce de León's reconnaissance of 1508 was followed by occupation; native military resistance was fierce but short-lived, supported by caribes from the Lesser Antilles. The indigenous population was virtually gone from the colonial record by the 1530s.
- **Jamaica**: Diego Columbus and Juan de Esquivel completed occupation in 1509. Las Casas reported the natives were put to hard labor producing foodstuffs, cloth, and hammocks without evangelization; the royal factor Pedro de Mazuelo complained by 1515 that barely any natives remained.
- **Cuba**: Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar received a license in 1511 and overran the island by March 1512. The rapid conquest replicated the devastation of Hispaniola.
- **Lucayas (Bahamas)**: The islands' population, lacking natural refuges and apparently offering no violent resistance, was simply "harvested" by slavers between 1509 and 1512. The subsequent "discovery" of Florida was, as Whitehead puts it, "little more than an extension of slave hunting beyond the empty islands."

### Tierra Firme — The Pearl Coast, Venezuela, and Panama

On the mainland, the timescale was longer and the outcomes more varied, but the destruction was equally real in the zones of intensive Spanish activity:

- The Cubaguan pearl fisheries (discovered 1512) consumed Lucayan and local indigenous divers; overexploitation exhausted the beds by 1534–37.
- The Venezuelan littoral was subjected to repeated declarations of caribe status, enabling slaving raids. Las Casas described the region from Paria to the Bay of Venezuela: "the Spaniards committed most wonderful depopulations; for they gave themselves wholly to their wanton robberies, enslaving also infinite numbers of men."
- On the Panamanian isthmus, the governorship of Pedrarias Dávila (from 1514) was described by both Las Casas and Oviedo — otherwise at odds — as uniformly brutal. A pre-contact population estimated at over 2 million on the isthmus had been reduced to a fraction by 1607, when the Audiencia of Panama reported a desolate scene. The dominant Cuna (Cueva) people retreated inland as the coast and hinterland reverted to dense forest, "where once the savannahs and fields of native agriculture had dominated."

### Epidemics and Ecological Disruption

Old World diseases — smallpox, measles — struck populations with no prior exposure. Whitehead emphasizes that "epidemics proved lethal to the biologically pristine human populations of the New World and encouraged a rapid and widespread migration away from the epicenters of disease dispersion." Crucially, disease "effects" outran "cause": the spread of epidemic disease along regional trade and alliance networks affected populations before any direct European contact reached them.

Ecological disruption compounded biological catastrophe. Introduced livestock — cattle, sheep, goats, rats, dogs — transformed island ecologies, destroying native species habitats. On Margarita, introduced livestock caused "a dramatic change in the vegetation cover through the persistent browsing on the young flower-bearing shoots" which was "then filled by cactus-like plants." On the mainland llanos, cattle displaced native deer and peccary. As indigenous populations died, migrated, or were relocated, their agricultural systems collapsed, allowing forest encroachment that further reduced habitat for species central to native subsistence.

## Causal Analysis

### Immediate Causes

The collapse resulted from the convergence of multiple forces that were individually devastating and mutually reinforcing:

1. **Epidemic disease** (`caused_by: [[columbian-exchange-1492]]`): Smallpox and measles against populations with no acquired immunity. Particularly virulent on islands where geographic circumscription prevented flight; disease spread through regional networks faster than the colonizers themselves moved.

2. **Military conquest** (`caused_by:` Spanish military campaigns): Armored cavalry, steel weapons, firearms, and attack dogs gave Spanish forces decisive technological and tactical superiority. The 1495 battle of Magua demonstrated the annihilating effect of organized European military force against even numerically overwhelming indigenous resistance.

3. **Encomienda and forced labor** (`caused_by: [[encomienda]]`): The repartimiento distributed native peoples — not land — to encomenderos. The *mita* (labor parties rented by encomenderos) was particularly subject to lethal abuse. On Hispaniola the gap between formal "protection" of natives and actual conditions of labor was catastrophic.

4. **Slave-raiding** (`contributed_to:`): The juridical manufacture of the *caribe* category (see below) licensed open slave-hunting. The Lucayas were depopulated entirely by slavers. Venezuelan coastal populations were repeatedly toggled between protected *guatiao* and enslaveable *caribe* status according to Spanish economic need.

### Enabling Conditions

The vulnerability of Caribbean societies to the pace of Spanish conquest was shaped by island geography (circumscription limiting flight options), concentration of population in visible coastal settlements, and the absence of the large military states (like the Aztec or Inka empires) that elsewhere slowed and complicated conquest. On the mainland, population density and geographic extent gave somewhat more scope for flight and reorganization.

## The Colonial Manufacture of the Caribe/Cannibal Category

A distinctive feature of the Caribbean collapse — and of its historiography — is the role of legal categories in driving enslavement. Whitehead documents the mechanism with precision.

Columbus inaugurated an ethnological dualism distinguishing the "tractable" *aruaca* or *guatiao* (later retroactively called [[taino]]) of the Greater Antilles from the "fearsome" *caribes* of the Lesser Antilles. The term **taíno** itself, as an ethnic designation, was coined only in the nineteenth century by antiquarian C. S. Rafinesque — it does not appear in sixteenth-century sources — lending spurious coherence to what were in reality diverse, named polities.

By royal decree of Queen Isabella in 1504, *caribes* were juridically redefined as any native population that opposed Spanish occupation:

> "If such Cannibals continue to resist and do not wish to admit and receive my Captains and men who may be on such voyages by my orders, not to hear them in order to be taught our Sacred Catholic Faith, and to be in my service and obedience, they may be captured and are to be taken to these my Kingdoms and Domain and to other parts and places to be sold." *(Colección de Documentos Inéditos, Vol. 31)*

The crucial legal mission of licenciado Rodrigo de Figueroa (1520) was dispatched to formally classify native populations across the Caribbean and Tierra Firme as *caribe* or not. Whitehead shows Figueroa's depositions were "deeply political": populations were assigned *caribe* status in ways that "directly reflected the interests of the planters and slavers of Española." Figueroa's own criterion:

> "All of the islands which are not populated by the Christians, except the islands of Trinidad, the Lucayas, Barbados, Gigantes, and Margarita they declare themselves to be and I declare them to be caribes and barbarous people, enemies of the Christians, repudiating their conversion, and such that they eat human flesh." *(Colección de Documentos Inéditos, Vol. 1)*

Trinidad was declared *caribe* in 1511, reversed in 1518 (on Las Casas's intervention), and reversed again in 1530 to permit its occupation. The pearl-fishing populations near Cubagua were classified as *guatiao* to preserve labor in situ — then reclassified as *caribe* in 1519 once commercial extraction was underway. The term "cannibal" itself derived from Spanish renderings of *caribal* / *caníbal* from native speech, but was extended through this political logic to cover all resistance.

Anthropophagy, where it existed, was embedded in native ritual war practices connected to elite exchange networks, not the gastronomic atrocity of European representation. In some cases, the "captive women" on islands like Guadeloupe appear from early sources to have been primarily textile producers for elite exchange — not victims of cannibalism — a reading that diverges sharply from the colonial account.

## Consequence Analysis

### Demographic Annihilation

The scale is unambiguous even where precise numbers are contested. On Hispaniola alone, the indigenous population declined from c. 1 million (the most common early Spanish estimate, though disputed) to ~30,000 at the 1514 repartimiento and to barely 500 persons by 1548. By the 1530s the Greater Antilles as a whole had been virtually cleared of their pre-contact populations. The Panamanian isthmus (pre-contact estimate: 2+ million) was similarly devastated by 1607.

### Acceleration of the Atlantic Slave Trade

With the indigenous labor supply of Hispaniola and Cuba destroyed within a generation, the Spanish "had to turn quickly to the coast of west Africa to ensure a new supply of labor to support the mining and sugar growing that formed the basis of the island economy." The Caribbean demographic collapse thus `produced: [[atlantic-slave-trade]]` as a direct structural response, linking Caribbean catastrophe to the broader hemispheric trauma of African enslavement. This is one of the clearest causal chains connecting the events of 1492–1530 to the subsequent history of the Atlantic world.

### Social Dissolution and Tribalization

Surviving native societies underwent what Whitehead calls "tribalization": formerly complex chiefdom-level polities broke down into "increasingly closed and endogamous social groups" with new forms of hierarchical leadership. Marriage-exchange networks that had cemented elite alliances collapsed; the *encomienda* deliberately undermined native marriage practices "through a requirement for settlement endogamy, a valorization of patriliny over matriliny, and intermarriage with non-natives." New political leaders emerged from demographic catastrophe — often based on competence in trade and war rather than dynastic genealogy — as elite families were physically exterminated.

The small-scale, linguistically homogeneous, endogamous communities familiar from later ethnographic records of Caribbean and northern South American peoples are in large part products of this colonial tribalization, not survivals of pre-Columbian baseline societies.

### Cosmological Crisis

Native shamanic authority was undermined by the succession of epidemics: "indigenous theories and beliefs about death and contagion were proved inadequate or irrelevant." Shamans who survived retreated from large-scale political ordering into narrower roles of curing and healing. No millennial resistance movement emerged in the Caribbean (unlike coastal Brazil or Peru), partly because autonomous native society was so severely curtailed so quickly.

## Actors

- **[[taino]]**: The Taíno-speaking chiefdom societies of the Greater Antilles bore the first and most total impact. The paramount cacique Guarionex of Hispaniola served briefly as intermediary, was captured after the 1497 conspiracy, and died by shipwreck en route to Spain in 1502. The cacica Anacaona of Hispaniola was among the last prominent leaders; she was executed by the Spanish.
- **[[island-caribs]]**: The "Island Caribs" of the Lesser Antilles — themselves a colonial category partly constructed through political process — persisted far longer, raiding Puerto Rico and the Venezuelan coast throughout the sixteenth century. Their resistance was enabled by geography (wooded islands unsuitable for cavalry), external alliances (with French and English from the seventeenth century), and the commercial utility of trade in foodstuffs, water, and wood with Atlantic shipping.
- **Bartolomé de Las Casas**: Spanish cleric and primary chronicler of atrocities; his *Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias* (1542) and *Historia de las Indias* anchored the "Black Legend" of Spanish colonial brutality. Las Casas intervened to reverse Trinidad's *caribe* classification in 1518. His writings are both the most detailed contemporary account and a polemical document shaped by his advocacy agenda.
- **Rodrigo de Figueroa**: The *licenciado* dispatched by the Spanish crown in 1520 to classify Caribbean populations; his depositions formalized the caribe/cannibal category and directly served planter and slaver interests.
- **Pedrarias Dávila**: Governor of Castilla del Oro (Panama) from c. 1514; his administration was described as uniquely brutal even by contemporaries who disagreed on almost everything else. Under his rule the Panamanian isthmus was effectively depopulated.
- **[[encomienda]]** (institution): The legal-economic mechanism distributing indigenous peoples to Spanish colonists, nominally for "protection" and Catholic instruction, in practice a system of coerced labor prone to lethal abuse.

## Historiography

### Population Estimates: The Scale Debate

The precise pre-contact population of the Caribbean remains contested. The range in scholarly literature is wide. Las Casas cited figures of one million or more for Hispaniola; modern demographic historians (Henige, "On the contact population of Hispaniola," *Hispanic American Historical Review* 58, 1978) have challenged the reliability of all early Spanish counts, arguing the issue is "probably intractable" given the nature of surviving documentation. Whitehead's position is that "the point is perhaps less the absolute starting figure for the Amerindian population before contact than the unchallengeable figures of the size of the remnant population and the virulence with which disease struck in known contexts." The scale of loss is not disputed; the starting baseline is.

### Disease vs. Deliberate Violence

Early historiography (and Las Casas himself) emphasized the deliberate brutality of Spanish policy. Later twentieth-century historiography — following Alfred Crosby's *The Columbian Exchange* (1972) — shifted weight toward epidemic disease as the primary mechanism. Whitehead insists both were causally necessary: "it is not sufficient to explain all population loss from given locations by this means." Disease, military conquest, encomienda labor, and slave-raiding all operated simultaneously and reinforced each other; assigning primacy to any single factor is an analytical oversimplification.

### The Taíno/Carib Distinction as Colonial Construct

A major contribution of late twentieth-century scholarship (represented by Whitehead himself, Jalil Sued-Badillo's *Los Indios Caribes: Realidad o Fabula?*, 1978; Peter Hulme, *Colonial Encounters*) is the demonstration that the ethnological dualism of *taíno* vs. *caribe* was a colonial political product, not an accurate reflection of pre-contact ethnic reality. The term *taíno* was coined in the nineteenth century; the *caribe* category was legally manufactured to justify enslavement. Modern archaeological and linguistic studies attempting to operationalize this dualism have produced, as Whitehead notes, "wildly contradictory results." The critique has not fully displaced the older schema: some scholars continue to maintain the "Island Carib" presence in the Lesser Antilles as extraneous to the islands' aboriginal societies.

### The Black Legend and Its Critics

Las Casas's writings anchored an anti-Spanish, anti-Catholic historiography that was eagerly adopted by Spain's Protestant rivals (England, Netherlands, France). Gonzalo de Oviedo's *Historia General y Natural de las Indias* (1535) offered a competing, more sanguine view of Spanish policy and detailed native society. The debate between these poles — and the use of both by subsequent polemicists — has shaped Caribbean historiography to the present.

### Transformation vs. Extinction

Whitehead frames his chapter explicitly against a pure "extinction" narrative. The [[island-caribs]] of Dominica and St. Vincent demonstrate "transformation, rather than extinction" — persisting through adaptation, alliance with African cimarrones, and strategic accommodation with rival European powers. The "Black Caribs" of St. Vincent (Garifuna) are a product of this process. The category of the "pristine" or uncontacted indigenous Caribbean, in any form, is, Whitehead argues, an ethnographic fiction created by the very colonial processes this chapter documents.
