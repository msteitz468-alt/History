# The Hundred Years War (1337–1453)

The **Hundred Years War** was the long, intermittent conflict between the kings of **England**
and **France** that dominated the political life of late-medieval western Europe.
[[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (W. Mark Ormrod, Ch. 11a, for the opening phase) shows how the war
"created virtually unprecedented military, administrative and fiscal burdens which brought the
English state and its subjects into more regular contact and more frequent political conflict." This
page covers the war as a whole; its opening and English-victory phase here, with the French
recovery treated in the volume's French chapters.

## Origins

The war's roots lay in the **unresolved business of the thirteenth century**: the anomalous status
of **Aquitaine/Gascony**, which the English king held as a fief of the French crown (the friction
that had already produced [[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV's]] and [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I's]]
Gascon war); the Anglo-French rivalry over **Flanders** (England's wool market) and **Scotland**
(France's ally, the "Auld Alliance"); and, when the direct Capetian line failed in 1328,
**[[edward-iii-of-england|Edward III's]] claim to the French throne** through his mother Isabella.
Philip VI's confiscation of Aquitaine (1337) opened the war.

## The English-Victory Phase (1337–1360)

Edward III, who "excelled at the arts of war," won a string of spectacular victories that overturned
the assumptions of chivalric warfare through the use of the **longbow** and **defensively sited
dismounted men-at-arms** — the tactical lesson the Scots had taught at
[[battle-of-bannockburn-1314|Bannockburn]] and Courtrai. The naval victory at **Sluys (1340)**,
the crushing of the French chivalry at **Crécy (1346)**, the capture of **Calais (1347)**, and the
capture of the French king John II at **Poitiers (1356)** by the Black Prince forced the **Treaty of
Brétigny (1360)**, which gave Edward an enlarged Aquitaine in full sovereignty in exchange for
renouncing the French crown.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **dynastic claim** to France and the **feudal anomaly** of Aquitaine.
- `contributed_to:` the **commercial and strategic rivalry** over Flanders and the Scottish front;
  the failure of the Capetian line (1328) and the Valois succession.

## Consequence Analysis

The war's effects were structural and lasting:

- **State-building through war finance.** It drove "unprecedented... fiscal burdens" in both
  realms — the regular taxation, war loans, and (in England) the maturing of **[[english-parliament|parliament]]**
  as a taxative and legislative assembly; in France the *aides*, the *gabelle*, and the estates.
- **National feeling.** The long enmity sharpened **English and French national identity** (and the
  rise of [[rise-of-vernacular-literatures|written English]] in government).
- **Devastation of France.** The *chevauchées* and the free companies ravaged the French
  countryside, compounding the [[late-medieval-crisis|crisis]] of plague and famine.
- **The later phases.** English fortunes were reversed by the French recovery under **Charles V**
  (Cycle 4), revived under Henry V (Agincourt 1415, the Treaty of Troyes 1420), and finally lost —
  all but Calais — by 1453, by which time the war had helped forge two consolidated national
  monarchies. (The fifteenth-century close belongs to NCMH VII.)
