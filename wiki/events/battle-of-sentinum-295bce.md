# The Battle of Sentinum

## Overview

The Battle of **Sentinum** (295 BCE) was, in Cornell's assessment, "the greatest
military engagement that had ever taken place in Italy" — the decisive battle of the
**Third Samnite War** (298–290 BCE) and the event that effectively sealed the fate
of the Italian peninsula. Roman victory over a combined Samnite-Gallic force ended
the last serious challenge to Roman hegemony in peninsular Italy.

## Context: The Grand Coalition

The Third Samnite War had begun in 298 BCE when Rome allied with the Lucanians
against the Samnites, provoking a Samnite response. The Samnite general **Gellius
Egnatius** made the key strategic move: leading a Samnite army northward into Etruria
to unite with the Etruscan cities that had been chafing under Roman pressure since
the Etruscan wars of 311–308 BCE. By 296 BCE, a grand coalition had formed:
**Samnites, Etruscans, Umbrians, and Gauls** (the Senones of the Po valley, the same
people whose ancestors had sacked Rome in 390 BCE).

The coalition's combined manpower would have been overwhelming. Rome responded with
an unprecedented **concentration of military resources**: multiple prorogations, the
use of former magistrates (*privati*) with military *imperium*, and the simultaneous
deployment of six legions.

## The Battle

The decisive engagement at Sentinum in Umbria was fought in **295 BCE** by the
consuls **Q. Fabius Maximus Rullianus** and **P. Decius Mus** — both among the most
experienced Roman commanders of the generation. Their task was to engage the Samnites
and Gauls before the full coalition could concentrate. The Etruscans and Umbrians
were successfully drawn away from the main battlefield when Roman reserve forces
moved against Clusium (Etruscan), forcing a split in the coalition.

**Scale.** The Romans fielded **four legions plus substantial allied contingents** —
Livy notes that the allies outnumbered the Roman citizen troops (Livy X.26.14), making
the total probably over 36,000 men. The enemy's size is unknown; ancient sources give
the fantastic figure of 650,000 (rejected by Cornell); Duris of Samos, a Greek
*contemporary*, reported 100,000 killed. Livy's figures (8,700 Roman dead; 25,000
enemy) are more plausible and may be based on authentic records.

**The devotio.** At a critical moment in the battle, with the Roman line wavering,
**P. Decius Mus** repeated the sacrifice his father had performed at the Battle of
Veseris (340 BCE): he consecrated himself and the enemy to the gods of the underworld
(*devovere*) and charged headlong into the Gallic ranks, dying in battle. Livy's
account of the devotio is detailed; Cornell regards this as "undoubtedly historical"
and a turning point in the engagement.

**Q. Fabius Maximus Rullianus** commanded the other wing and won the battle; he
returned to Rome in triumph "with an assured place in the Roman tradition as the hero
of the Samnite wars."

## Consequences

**Immediate.** The Samnite-Gallic army was destroyed; Gellius Egnatius was killed.
The Etruscans and Umbrians, whose forces had been diverted, were dealt with separately
in 294 BCE (Rusellae captured; Volsinii, Perusia, and Arretium forced to terms).
Samnite resistance continued but was progressively ground down in 294–290 BCE; the
Samnites surrendered in 290 BCE.

**Strategic.** Sentinum "sealed the fate of Italy" (Cornell). After the battle the
process of conquest accelerated rather than slowed: in 290 BCE the consul M'. Curius
Dentatus conquered the Sabines and extended Roman territory to the Adriatic; by 264
BCE the entire peninsula from the Alps to the toe was under Roman control or in the
Roman alliance.

**Institutional.** The multiple prorogations of 296–295 BCE — including four
*privati* holding military *imperium* — marked a transition in Roman military
appointments. The practice of *iteration* (holding the same senior magistracy
repeatedly, which had characterized Camillus and the great commanders of the
fourth century) ended after the 290s; and the military dictatorship declined
as a regular institution, appearing again only in 249 BCE and after Trasimene
(217 BCE). The promagistracy became the normal mechanism for extended military
command.

## Source Quality

Sentinum is unusually well-attested for an early Republican battle:
- Livy (Book X) provides a detailed account, which Cornell regards as containing
  "probably for the first time" authentic elements
- **Duris of Samos** — a Greek historian who was a *contemporary* — mentioned the
  battle (reported 100,000 dead); Roman survivors of the battle could speak to
  Fabius Pictor a generation later
- The triumphal fasti confirm the victory

The scale and drama of the battle make it unlikely to have been significantly invented;
the main uncertainties concern the precise tactics and the numbers of casualties.
