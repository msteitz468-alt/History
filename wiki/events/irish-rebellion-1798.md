## Narrative

### Context and Origins

The rebellion of 1798 grew directly out of the radicalization of Irish politics after 1791. The Society of United Irishmen, founded in Belfast on 18 October 1791 and in Dublin on 9 November, had initially been a constitutional reform society pressing for parliamentary reform and Catholic inclusion. [[wolfe-tone]]'s pamphlet *An Argument on Behalf of the Catholics of Ireland* had set the terms: "an edifice of freedom" could never be built "on a foundation of monopoly." The government — alarmed by the combination of radical ideology and Catholic mobilization — declared the United Irishmen illegal in 1795, forcing them underground. This drove a transformation: the society reconstituted as a secret oath-bound republican organization committed to separation from Britain through French military assistance.

Dublin in this period was a city of clubs, and the United Irishmen exploited this: cockfights, horse races, Masonic lodges, turf-cuttings, and dinner parties all served as covers for recruitment and organization. Government reports noted with alarm that United Irish orders forbidding excessive drinking were "largely obeyed," confirming that their discipline was good. The loyalist MP Sir Richard Musgrave "became increasingly anxious in 1798 precisely because the people seemed to be remaining sober." As Patrick Geoghegan notes ([[jackson-oxford-modern-irish-history-2014]], Ch. 25), it has been estimated that Dublin had as many as 1,300 pubs in the 1790s, and these too served as recruiting grounds.

The government's response to the emerging threat was counterproductive as much as effective. After the **Bantry Bay expedition** of December 1796 — in which a French fleet of 35 ships with approximately 6,400 soldiers actually reached Bantry Bay but was prevented from landing by severe storms — the Irish administration was given a free hand "to terrorize the country into tranquillity." In 1797–98, Camden's policy of flogging suspects, burning houses, and administering oath searches in Ulster may have broken United Irish organization in the province while simultaneously provoking many into rebellion. The big interpretive question that "has still not been satisfactorily answered" is whether this terror *provoked* the country into mass uprisings or *limited* the scale of the rebellion.

### The Rising (May–September 1798)

The rebellion broke out in May 1798, but the counties did not act in unison — they were "easily sealed off" by government forces. The most dramatic and prolonged rising was in **Wexford**, where a largely Catholic insurgency under local leaders (including some priests, notably Fr John Murphy) held out for weeks against government forces. The Wexford rising produced the most contested episodes of the entire rebellion: the massacres at Scullabogue (where loyalist prisoners were burned alive by rebels) and at Wexford Bridge, and the counter-atrocities committed by the Yeomanry against civilian Catholics.

In **Ulster**, where the Presbyterian radical core of the original United Irishmen was concentrated, the rising was limited and quickly suppressed. The dynamic of the Ulster rising differed markedly from Wexford: it was more ideologically coherent but militarily weaker, and its suppression had been more thorough in the preparatory terror of 1797.

A French force under General Humbert landed at **Killala Bay** in Connacht in August 1798 with about 1,000 troops. Joined by local rebels, they won an initial engagement (the "Castlebar Races") before being surrounded and forced to surrender. Before news of Humbert's surrender reached France, Tone set out with a smaller French force for Ulster. His ship, the *Hoche*, arrived at **Lough Swilly** in Donegal three weeks late, after a six-hour sea battle in which Tone refused offers to escape. He was captured, brought to Dublin, tried by court martial, and sentenced to hang. He died in custody on 19 November 1798 after cutting his own throat.

### Religion and the Rebellion

The role of religion in 1798 "is an area which still divides." In the nineteenth century, the "faith and fatherland" interpretation — treating the rebellion as a Catholic religious uprising, with priests leading the people — competed with arch-loyalist accounts that emphasized its sectarian violence. For the 1998 bicentenary, nationalists downplayed clerical involvement, noting that only 70 of 1,800 priests had sided with the rebels. But as Geoghegan notes, "given that the rebellion was confined to a relatively narrow geographical area, this figure of seventy could be considered remarkably high." The Catholic majority *as a whole* sided with the government — a fact that complicates any simple reading of the rebellion as a Catholic national rising.

The rebellion is better understood as a "double conflict, superimposed on a single island: it was both a civil war, with loyalists fighting against nationalists, and also part of an international conflict, with Britain and France continuing their titanic conflict on Irish soil." The "real story was lost" in the subsequent nationalist-revisionist argument over atrocities.

## Consequence Analysis

The immediate consequence was the [[act-of-union-1801]]. The rebellion convinced the British government and Dublin Castle that the Irish Parliament, as constituted, was too unstable an instrument: it could neither prevent sedition nor manage a crisis. The Union was the solution — drawing Ireland directly into the Westminster system and denying future rebels a separate institutional target. Castlereagh and Cornwallis managed the passage of the Union through a combination of patronage, compensation for borough proprietors, and a covert slush fund that secretly diverted money from the civil list — the last a scandal documented only when a "second set of secret service records" was discovered in the 1990s.

The failure to deliver Catholic Emancipation with the Union — King George III refused, forcing Pitt's resignation — meant that "the Union came into effect on 1 January 1801, but far from being the unifying security mechanism that Pitt had intended, it became the focus of further division." Catholic Emancipation became the dominant issue of the next generation, ultimately won by [[daniel-o-connell]] in 1829.

The longer-term legacy was the crystallization of Irish republicanism as a tradition with Tone as its founding martyr — "without him, republicanism in Ireland would virtually have no tradition" (Ó Faoláin). Every subsequent generation of Irish republicans — the Fenians, the IRB, the IRA — invoked Tone.

## Historiography

The 1798 rebellion has been one of the most contested events in Irish historiography. The classic nationalist account — Thomas Pakenham, *The Year of Liberty* (1969) — presented a heroic narrative of popular resistance. The revisionist critique, most sharply articulated in the 1990s, emphasized the sectarian violence of the Wexford rising (at Scullabogue and Wexford Bridge) and challenged the idealized nationalist reading. As Geoghegan notes, "nationalist historians attempted to downplay the rebel atrocities" while "revisionist historians for their part overplayed the significance of these atrocities." The bicentenary of 1998 generated a substantial historiographical reckoning.

The ongoing debates concern: (1) the role of religion — how far was 1798 a Catholic religious rising or a nonsectarian republican one? (2) the counterfactual of the French landings — how close did Ireland come to an independent republic? (3) the role of government terror in provoking rather than preventing the rebellion; (4) the relative weight of the Wexford rising versus the Ulster rising in defining the rebellion's character.

Marianne Elliott's *Partners in Revolution* (1982) remains the standard account of the United Irishmen and France. Nancy Curtin's *The United Irishmen* (1994) reconstructed the organization's social base. Thomas Bartlett, David Dickson, Dáire Keogh, and Kevin Whelan edited the bicentenary collection *1798: A Bicentenary Perspective* (2003). The Wexford rising has its own literature: Daniel Gahan, *The People's Rising: Wexford, 1798* (1995); Kevin Whelan, *Fellowship of Freedom* (1998).
