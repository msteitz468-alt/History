# Battle of Verdun (21 February – 20 December 1916)

## Narrative

The Battle of Verdun was the longest and one of the costliest battles of the First World War, a deliberate German campaign of attrition aimed at "bleeding white" the French army. The Germans chose Verdun because French political and military leaders would never willingly abandon the historic fortress city, and because excellent rail communications allowed rapid concentration of German artillery and troops close to the front.

The German bombardment began unevenly on 21 February 1916. The heaviest fires fell on XXX Corps east of the Meuse. After initial gains, Fort Douaumont fell on 25 February in the confusion of command changes. French forces were driven back several kilometers in the first days.

Joffre initially viewed the attack as a diversion and expected the main German effort elsewhere. On 25 February he sent Castelnau to assess the situation and then assigned Philippe Pétain and Second Army headquarters to command at Verdun with the mission to "rally the troops... if they are compelled to withdraw to the west bank; (2) Prevent the enemy from crossing the [Meuse] river." After Briand's intervention at GQG, the decision was affirmed to hold the east bank "no matter what the cost."

Pétain organized the defense in depth with groups echeloned, created the "noria" rotation system to relieve divisions before they were destroyed (ultimately 68–78 divisions rotated through), and sustained the front via the narrow-gauge railway and the "Sacred Way" road from Bar-le-Duc (1,700 trucks/day at peak, a vehicle every 25 seconds). French artillery on the west bank enfiladed German advances on the east.

The Germans shifted to the west bank in March to silence French batteries, seizing ground toward Hill 304 and Mort Homme but failing to take the crests after a month. On the east bank they captured Fort Vaux in June and pushed a salient toward Souville. Nivelle's famous order "Comrades, they will not pass!" came during the crisis after Fleury fell.

Political pressure led to removal of Dubail and de Langle de Cary in late March; Pétain was promoted to command Central Army Group in May, with Nivelle replacing him at Second Army / Verdun. Pétain continued to advocate limited, artillery-heavy actions and protested levies of troops and guns for the Somme.

As the Somme offensive drew German reserves away, French counterattacks intensified. Nivelle and Mangin recaptured Fort Douaumont on 24 October 1916 with a short, violent artillery preparation and rolling barrages (infantry following 50–75 m behind 75 mm fire). Fort Vaux was evacuated by the Germans and reoccupied on 2 November. A further push in mid-December drove the Germans back to lines 7.5 km from the citadel.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[events/first-world-war-1914]]` and the failure of earlier rupture attempts in 1915. Falkenhayn's explicit goal was not territorial gain but to force France to "impale itself on Germany's sword" at Verdun, compelling repeated offensives and suffering immense losses. Joffre's prior policy of treating forts as non-independent positions and stripping artillery, combined with diversion of labor to the autumn 1915 Champagne offensive, left Verdun's defenses incomplete ("third position almost nonexistent"). Joffre's underestimation ("I ask only one thing, that the Germans attack me and... that it be at Verdun" yet "most likely be a diversion") delayed reinforcement until the attack was underway. `contributed_to:` the noria system and methodical defense that turned the battle into a prolonged mutual attrition rather than quick German victory.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` enormous French casualties (Second Army reported 89k by end March, rising to 377k+ claimed by December; British figures like Churchill's 442k include extraneous losses). `produced:` Pétain's reputation as saver of Verdun and developer of rotation, depth defense, and economical use of infantry. `produced:` Nivelle's prominence through limited but successful October–December counteroffensives using concentrated artillery and rolling barrages, setting up his 1917 command. `enabled:` relief of pressure at Verdun once the Somme drew off German artillery and reserves; by late summer the Germans abandoned the "bleed France" priority. `concurrent_with:` the Somme offensive, which Joffre used to justify limiting Verdun commitments while Pétain begged for an earlier start. The battle exhausted French reserves and contributed to political disillusion with Joffre's broad-front attrition methods on the Somme compared to Nivelle's "quick thrusts." Overall French 1916 deaths were lower than 1914 or 1915 despite Verdun and Somme.

## Historiography

Doughty provides the definitive archive-based account of French high command decision-making, drawing on AFGG, SHAT records, and memoirs. He corrects myths of irrational French bloodletting by showing pragmatic adaptation (Pétain's rotation vs. earlier "hold every inch"), the role of artillery dominance, and the political-military tensions (Gallieni report, Briand intervention, removal of army-group commanders). French official history and Pétain's own account emphasize the "noria" and logistics; German accounts stress initial successes and later shift under Hindenburg. The battle remains central to debates on attrition vs. breakthrough, command responsibility, and the "pyrrhic" nature of French 1918 victory. Doughty notes French casualties at Verdun were severe but lower overall in 1916 than in the mobile 1914 or rupture attempts of 1915.
