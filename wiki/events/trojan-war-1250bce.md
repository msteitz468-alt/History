# Trojan War

The Trojan War is the conflict, remembered in Greek epic as a coalition of
[[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] kingdoms besieging and destroying the city of
[[troy|Troy]] in northwest Anatolia, and debated by historians as a possible
historical kernel behind [[homer|Homer]]'s *Iliad*. CAH II.2 (chapter XXVII)
treats it within the recession of Mycenaean civilisation, asking how much real
Late Bronze Age history the epic tradition preserves; chapter XXXVI (Hammond)
returns to it from the side of the migration legends.

> **Dating.** No date is secure. Archaeologically the candidate destruction is
> **Troy VIIa**, burned c. 1250–1180 BCE; the later Greek chronographers placed
> the fall variously, Eratosthenes' **1184 BCE** being the canonical ancient
> figure. The filename uses c. 1250 BCE (the Troy VIIa horizon as understood at
> the time of the volume); the event sits at the very end of the
> [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze Age]], overlapping the
> [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|collapse]].

## The Question of Historicity

CAH II.2 frames the problem soberly. Three independent bodies of evidence bear on
whether a historical war underlies the legend:

1. **Archaeology of [[troy|Troy/Hisarlık]].** Schliemann's and Blegen's
   excavations established a long sequence. **Troy VI** was a prosperous,
   well-fortified Late Bronze Age citadel destroyed c. 1300 BCE — but the evidence
   (Blegen) pointed to **earthquake**, not war. **Troy VIIa**, a poorer
   reoccupation with crowded houses and storage jars sunk in the floors (a city
   under siege?), was destroyed by fire later in the 13th century. Blegen
   identified VIIa as "Priam's Troy"; the identification rests on the destruction
   by fire and the date, not on direct evidence of Greek attackers.
2. **The Catalogue of Ships (*Iliad* II).** The Catalogue lists the Achaean
   contingents and their home cities. CAH II.2 notes that it preserves a
   geography of Late Bronze Age Greece more accurate than the Greece of Homer's own
   day — naming centres (Mycenae, Pylos) that were great in the Bronze Age and
   insignificant or deserted afterward. This is among the strongest arguments that
   genuine Mycenaean-age memory survived in the tradition, even if much else is
   anachronistic.
3. **Hittite records and [[ahhiyawa-question|Aḫḫiyawa]].** The Hittite archives
   document **Aḫḫiyawa** — plausibly the Achaeans / a Mycenaean power — active on
   the western Anatolian coast, and a place **Wiluša** (≈ Ilios) ruled by an
   **Alakšandu** (≈ Alexandros/Paris). These tantalising correspondences suggest
   real Mycenaean involvement in conflicts over northwest Anatolia, without
   confirming the specific war of legend. The identification is itself disputed
   (see [[ahhiyawa-question|the Aḫḫiyawa question]]).

### The migration legends and Mopsus (CAH II.2, Ch. XXXVI)

Hammond, treating the literary tradition, dates the fall of Troy to **c. 1200 BCE**
(compatible with the Egyptian Sea-Raids of the 1190s–1180s and with Thucydides) and
shows the post-war *Nostoi* shading into the **migration legends** of the eastern
Aegean and Anatolia. The pivotal case is **Mopsus**: a seer who, after the sack of
Troy, led "peoples" across the Taurus into Pamphylia and on to Cilicia, Syria, and
Phoenicia. The legend — long known only from late authors — was **strikingly
confirmed** by the bilingual **Karatepe inscription** (8th c.), which names the
"house of *Mukshush*" (Hittite) / *Mupsh* (Phoenician), i.e. Mopsus, and a king "of
the *Danuniyim*" (≈ the Danaoi). A Hittite tablet from Boğazköy also links *Mukshush*
to a "man of Aḫḫiyawa." Mopsus is thus, in R. D. Barnett's phrase, "the first figure
of Greek mythology to emerge into historic reality" — independent, non-Greek
corroboration of the post-Trojan-War Greek diaspora into the Levant.

## Causal Analysis

If a historical conflict lies behind the legend, the volume's framing points to
material rather than romantic causes:

- `contributed_to:` competition for control of the **Hellespont / Dardanelles**
  and the Troad — the gateway between the Aegean and the Black Sea and its trade.
- `contributed_to:` the pattern of [[mycenaean-civilization|Mycenaean]] raiding and
  expansion along the Anatolian coast attested in the Aḫḫiyawa texts.
- The epic *casus belli* (the abduction of Helen) is the kind of personalising
  legend the tradition supplied; it is not treated as historical cause.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the Greek **epic tradition** — the matter of Troy became the
  organising memory of the heroic age, transmitted orally through the
  [[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]] and crystallised in [[homer|Homer]] (see
  [[homeric-poems-as-history|the Homeric poems as history]]).
- `produced:` (in legend) the **Nostoi** — the disrupted homecomings of the
  victors — which the later Greeks linked to the very migrations and disturbances
  of the [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|collapse]] period.
- `concurrent_with:` the destruction of [[troy|Troy VIIa]] and the broader
  unravelling of the Late Bronze Age Aegean.

## Historiography

The volume occupies a careful middle ground: it neither dismisses the war as pure
fiction nor accepts the *Iliad* as reportage. The decipherment of Linear B (1952)
had recently shown the Mycenaeans to be Greeks, lending plausibility to a Bronze
Age kernel; the Hittite Aḫḫiyawa texts pointed the same way. The Catalogue of
Ships is the load-bearing argument for genuine survival of Bronze Age memory.
Against historicity stand the epic's manifest anachronisms (mixing Bronze Age and
Iron Age realia), its oral-formulaic composition centuries after the events, and
the inconvenient fact that the better-fortified, grander city (Troy VI) fell to
earthquake. Modern excavation (Korfmann's work at Hisarlık from the 1980s) has
enlarged the lower city and revived the case for a substantial city worth fighting
over, keeping the debate alive. The matter is treated here as
**date_precision: disputed**; this page records the arguments and does not
adjudicate. See also [[ahhiyawa-question|the Aḫḫiyawa question]].
