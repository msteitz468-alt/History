## Narrative

In October 1572 [[takeda-shingen]] left his capital of Ko-fu with approximately twenty thousand of his own troops and two thousand Hojo men, marching into Tōtōmi province while his general Yamagata took five thousand to make a diversion in eastern Mikawa. Taking advantage of the season's snows that would keep his northern rival Uesugi Kenshin immobilized at home, Shingen advanced steadily through the province. A Tokugawa reconnaissance force of three thousand under Okubo Tadayo and Honda Tadakatsu had difficulty withdrawing and was saved only by Honda's skillful management of the retreat. Shingen captured Ieyasu's stronghold of Futamata on the road to his capital of Hamamatsu.

Three thousand Nobunaga troops under Sakuma Nobumori, Hiraide Norihide, and Takigawa Kazumasa arrived in Hamamatsu in response to Ieyasu's request for help. A council of war unanimously advised remaining on the defensive; Nobunaga's generals explicitly brought advice from their lord to do the same. Ieyasu rejected all this: "To let an enemy come marching up to your castle without shooting an arrow at him is not to be a man" (Sadler). He ordered his generals to lead out their combined force.

Ieyasu drew his men up in "stork's wing" formation on the plateau of Mikata-ga-hara, about three-quarters of a mile north of Hamamatsu, with himself commanding the center slightly behind the wings. On his right was Nobunaga's contingent under Sakuma, Hiraide, and Takigawa; Sakai Tadatsugu held the extreme right flank; the left wing was commanded by Ogasawara Nagayoshi, Matsudaira Ietada, Honda Tadakatsu, and Ishikawa Kazumasa. It was late afternoon in a snowstorm when the battle opened.

Shingen's advanced troops were arranged in echelon and nearly matched the entire Tokugawa force in numbers, with the fifteen-thousand-strong main body behind them. The Takeda attacked both wings simultaneously with great vigor. The Mikawa men on the left initially drove back their opponents; Nobunaga's men on the right had no stomach for the fight and collapsed quickly. Takigawa and Sakuma "lost their heads and bolted" (Sadler). Hiraide stood firm and fought well but was overwhelmed and killed. Two fresh Takeda divisions moved up to continue the assault on the left as the first two rested.

Sakai Tadatsugu on the extreme right, now "left in the air," fought with great tenacity but was forced back onto the main body. Shingen then ordered a flanking movement against the Tokugawa left rear. As twilight approached, with snow still falling and his men exhausted and without reserves, Ieyasu's line broke. "They abandoned their positions and retreated as best they could" (Sadler).

Ieyasu was nearly surrounded while attempting to assist Mizuno Masashige on his exposed flank. Natsume Jurozaemon Masayoshi, captain of the Hamamatsu garrison, rode out with twenty-four men, ordered Ieyasu's escort to get him to the castle, grabbed his bridle, struck his horse's rump with a spear-haft, and shouted for his squire to ride hard. The horse, "a spirited one," quickly outdistanced the pursuers. Ieyasu made it back to Hamamatsu with five men, having along the way deflected an enemy archer with the help of Amano Yasukage kicking the bow from horseback and put an arrow into a spearman himself.

En route back to the castle, Ieyasu ordered a captured enemy head proclaimed as Shingen's — a ruse to calm the anxious population of Hamamatsu before the reality of the defeat became clear.

## The Night After the Battle

Ieyasu reached the castle and, famously, "lay down and immediately his loud snores resounded through the apartment" (Sadler) after eating three bowls of boiled rice. He ordered the castle gates left wide open and great flares lit both inside and out — reasoning that open gates and light would make it easier for his own scattered men to find their way back, while puzzling the enemy. The Takeda vanguard commanders Yamagata Masakage and Baba Nobuharu, approaching the castle, saw the open gates and hesitated: "Tokugawa Dono is renowned as the greatest general on the Tokaido coast. You may be sure he is up to some cunning trick or other" (Sadler).

That night a volunteer detachment of sixteen matchlockmen and about a hundred infantry under Okubo Tadayo and Amano Yasukage slipped out and attacked the Takeda bivouac on the plateau, using their knowledge of the terrain to get close before firing. They caused a minor panic, inflicted some casualties, and intensified Shingen's uncertainty about Ieyasu's remaining strength and intentions.

Shingen's council of war concluded it was unwise to remain: overcoming the Mikawa men would take time, Nobunaga might strike their rear, and Uesugi could move into Kai if they stayed too long. They withdrew, intending to return the next year with a larger force.

## Shingen's Death

In January 1573 Shingen returned to Mikawa and besieged the fortress of Noda. After the garrison surrendered from starvation, Shingen fell ill — or, in the more colorful account, was shot in the head by a sharpshooter while listening to flute music played by the besieged garrison drinking up their last provisions before surrendering. He died in April 1573. "A great load was lifted from the shoulders of Nobunaga and Ieyasu" (Sadler).

## Causal Analysis

The deeper cause of the battle was Shingen's strategic plan to neutralize Ieyasu before advancing on the capital, exploiting the winter season when Uesugi Kenshin could not intervene. The immediate cause on Ieyasu's side was his refusal to accept Nobunaga's advice to retreat — a decision that nearly destroyed him. His inspector of forces Torii Mototada had argued for attacking Shingen's rear as he marched past rather than meeting him frontally; Ieyasu rejected this as well, saying Torii was "possessed by the Spirit of Timidity."

The outcome was determined by the disparity in troop quality between the Takeda veterans and Nobunaga's Omi levies, who had "no heart for the fight." Had Nobunaga's contingent held, the battle might have been more contested.

## Consequence Analysis

The defeat was Ieyasu's most serious military reverse. Its consequences were mitigated almost entirely by Shingen's death the following spring. Shingen's withdrawal spared Hamamatsu castle, and his subsequent death meant that the Takeda under the less capable Katsuyori would never again threaten Ieyasu with the same combination of numbers, military skill, and strategic coordination. The episode also generated one of the most celebrated anecdotes of Ieyasu's character — the open gates, the snoring, the rice — that later became central to his carefully curated reputation for composure.

## Historiography

Sadler's account is drawn principally from the *Mikawa Go-Fudoki* and Tokugawa chronicle tradition, with the self-evident interest of maximizing the drama and ultimate significance of Ieyasu's recovery. The anecdote of the open gates (and the sleeping general) is widely repeated in Japanese sources and has the quality of deliberate legend-making. Sadler himself notes that Shingen's death by sniper fire while listening to the flute is "picturesque though not necessarily more trustworthy" than the less colorful account of illness. Modern scholarship on the battle has focused particularly on whether Nobunaga's advice to retreat was strategically correct, and on the detailed reconstruction of Takeda formations.
