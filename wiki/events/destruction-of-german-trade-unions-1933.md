# Destruction of the German Trade Unions (2 May 1933)

The overnight elimination of Europe's most powerful trade union movement, the
day after the regime had granted labor's oldest demand — May Day as a paid
national holiday — as a trap. Source: Evans
([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]], Part 5).

## Narrative

By 25 March 1933 union offices had been occupied by SA, SS, or police in 45
towns. Theodor Leipart's leadership tried appeasement: on 21 March the unions
declared themselves apolitical, ready to serve "whatever the kind of state
regime"; on 28 April they agreed a unification framework with the Christian
and Liberal unions under the "new state"; they endorsed Goebbels's "Day of
National Labour," and officials — including textile-union president Karl
Schrader — marched under the swastika on 1 May. The Tempelhof rally packed
over a million people into twelve military-style squares under glowing
swastika fireworks — "a proletarian counterpart of the ceremony held for the
upper classes at Potsdam" (Evans) — though attendance was coerced: state
workers threatened with dismissal, Berlin industrial workers' timecards
confiscated and returnable only on the field.

Goebbels's diary, 17 April: "On 1 May we shall arrange May Day as a grandiose
demonstration of the German people's will. On 2 May the trade union offices
will be occupied... There might possibly be a row for a few days, but then
they will belong to us." On 2 May brownshirts and SS stormed every Social
Democratic-oriented union office, newspaper, and union bank branch in
Germany; Leipart and the leadership went into "protective custody," many
beaten and humiliated; in Duisburg stormtroopers beat four union officials to
death in the cellar of the trade union building. On 4 May the Christian
unions submitted unconditionally. "The once-powerful German trade union
movement had disappeared without trace virtually overnight." Goebbels, 3 May:
"We are the masters of Germany."

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: the seizure-of-power logic — with the KPD destroyed and the
  [[enabling-act-23-march-1933|Enabling Act]] in force, the unions were the
  labor movement's last organized mass base.
- Enabling context: the Nazi factory cell organization had polled only single
  figures in the early-1933 works-council elections (better only at Krupp,
  chemicals, some steel, Ruhr mines) — the takeover had to be by force, not
  capture; remaining elections were postponed indefinitely.
- The unions' appeasement (Evans, attributed) reflected the memory of
  institutional survival strategies and hopes in Nazi job-creation promises;
  it bought nothing.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: the German Labour Front absorbed the assets and membership
  (managed initially by the Nazi factory cell organization).
- enabled: the [[dissolution-of-german-parties-1933|destruction of the SPD]] —
  Goebbels: once the unions were taken, "the other parties and organizations
  will not be able to hold out for much longer."
- contrasts_with: [[kapp-putsch-1920]] — the general strike that saved the
  Republic in 1920 was impossible in 1933 amid mass unemployment (millions of
  potential strikebreakers), a contrast Evans draws explicitly.

## Actors

- [[nazi-germany]] / [[sturmabteilung-sa]] / [[goebbels-joseph]]
- Theodor Leipart, Wilhelm Leuschner (ADGB leadership; Leuschner was later

## Historiography

Evans's account stresses the trap structure (May Day gift → 2 May seizure)
and the failure of institutional appeasement as a survival strategy — a
pattern repeated by the Centre Party weeks later.
