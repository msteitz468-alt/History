# Proclamation of Neutrality (1793)

On April 22, 1793, President George Washington issued the Proclamation of Neutrality, declaring that the United States would "pursue a conduct friendly and impartial toward the belligerent Powers" in the war then raging in Europe.

## Narrative

The proclamation came in response to the outbreak of war between the French Republic and a coalition including Great Britain. It announced that the United States would not take sides and warned American citizens against committing acts that would violate the country's neutral obligations.

Thomas Jefferson, as Secretary of State, had significant responsibility for implementing and interpreting the policy on a day-to-day basis, including issues of treaties with France, the reception of the new French minister Edmond Genet, and responses to British and French violations of American neutral rights.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The European war that began in 1793 after the execution of Louis XVI and French revolutionary expansion.

**contributed_to:** Washington's determination (supported by Hamilton) to keep the fragile republic out of war it was unprepared for.

**enabled:** Jefferson's efforts to maintain commercial relations with both sides while upholding treaty obligations to France that were not inconsistent with neutrality.

## Consequence Analysis

The policy of neutrality became a foundational American tradition for more than a century. It immediately created friction with France (which expected American support under the 1778 treaties) and with domestic opponents who viewed it as overly favorable to Britain.

It also intensified the emerging party divisions, with Republicans generally more sympathetic to France and Federalists to Britain and order.

## Actors

- **Primary**: George Washington (issued the proclamation), Thomas Jefferson (chief diplomatic officer implementing it), Alexander Hamilton (strong advocate for strict neutrality and pro-British tilt in practice).
- **Secondary**: John Adams, Edmund Randolph.

## Historiography

Malone's account in *Jefferson and the Ordeal of Liberty* emphasizes Jefferson's technical competence and patience in carrying out a policy he had doubts about but supported once decided, while noting the personal and political strain. Later scholarship has examined the legal and constitutional dimensions (Pacificus-Helvidius debate) and the long-term success of the neutrality policy in allowing the United States to grow stronger before later engagement in European affairs.
