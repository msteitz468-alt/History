# The Ustashe Genocide (1941–1945)

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]]: Ante Pavelić's "Independent State of Croatia," proclaimed 10 April 1941 under German protection, launched a systematic campaign of murder and forced conversion against its 2 million Serbs, ~45,000 Jews, and ~30,000 Roma. Decrees from 17 April 1941 sanctioned killing for "offending the honour of the Croat nation" — past, present, or future — alongside Aryan-model racial laws.

## Narrative

The violence was intimate and demonstrative. At **Glina** (July 1941) ~300 Serbs were shot; a subsequent "amnesty" lured 250 villagers to a sham conversion ceremony in the Orthodox church, where Ustashe militia forced them to lie down and beat their heads in with spiked clubs. Across Croatia in summer–autumn 1941 villagers were burned alive in churches; mutilations (eyes gouged, women's breasts cut off) were widespread enough to shock German observers. The camp law of 26 June 1941 built a national network explicitly for exterminating minorities: at **Jasenovac** more than 20,000 Jews died of disease, starvation, and nightly hammer-beatings by militia "egged on by Franciscan friars"; at Loborgrad 1,500 Jewish women were subject to systematic rape by camp staff; Stara Gradiška's administrator deliberately seeded typhus into the Djakovo camp. Serbs were offered conversion to Catholicism or death; Archbishop Alojzije Stepinac, who had initially endorsed action against Orthodox "schismatics" and arranged Pavelić's private papal audience (18 May 1941), condemned forced conversion only from 1942 and deportations from 1943.

Toll: **~300,000 Serbs**, ~30,000 Jews (a further 5,000+ deported to [[auschwitz]] at German request in 1942–43), and most of Croatia's Roma. Even German and Italian officials complained the terror was inflaming the Serb risings; the refugee flood fed both the Chetniks and Tito's Partisans.

## Historiography

Evans presents the Ustashe killings as an autonomous, inward-directed genocide — enabled and sponsored by, but not commanded from, Berlin — and elsewhere (2015) contrasts it with the Holocaust's world-enemy construction. Victim numbers, especially for Jasenovac, remain politically contested between Serbian and Croatian national historiographies (postwar Yugoslav figures were inflated; 1990s Croatian revisionism deflated); the ~300,000 Serb figure represents the scholarly middle ground Evans adopts.
