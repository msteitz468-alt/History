# The Peace of Callias, c. 449 BCE

## Narrative

The **Peace of Callias** was the settlement, traditionally negotiated at **Susa** by the
Athenian **Callias son of Hipponicus**, that ended the half-century of war between the
Greeks and **[[achaemenid-empire|Persia]]** begun by the [[ionian-revolt-499bce|Ionian
Revolt]]. After [[cimon|Cimon's]] last campaign in **Cyprus** (where he died besieging
Citium, 450), the Persian king sued for terms. The reported conditions: the **Greek cities
of Asia were to be autonomous**; Persian **satraps were not to come within three days'
journey of the sea**; **no Persian warship** was to sail west of Phaselis and the
Bosporus; and the **Athenians were not to attack** the King's territory.

## Why it matters

[[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 6, D. M. Lewis) treats the Peace as the moment the
**[[delian-league|Delian League]] abandoned its founding purpose** — war against Persia —
and so completed its transformation into the **Athenian empire**. Lewis argues the decisive
evidence is **circumstantial but conclusive**: in **447/6** Athens began the
**[[parthenon|Parthenon]]**, financed (the building accounts show) from the **appropriated
League treasury** and conceived "with something of the nature of a victory dedication." A
state would not pour 3,000 talents of allied tribute into a victory monument unless
"confident that the Persian War was over, by mutual consent." The war did end; "to fuss
about the details... involves the danger of losing sight of the main fact."

## Historiography — the authenticity debate

The Peace is the single most debated topic in fifth-century history because **neither
Herodotus nor Thucydides records it**. Its existence rests on fourth-century Athenian
orators (Isocrates, 380, who could "read" a text) and on Ephorus (via Diodorus); the terms
vary in the telling, and **Theopompus denounced the inscribed text as a forgery** (it was
in the Ionic lettering of 403/2). Modern scholars have variously denied it, dated it to the
460s (after the Eurymedon), or — with Lewis (following Eduard Meyer) — accepted it as a real
cessation of hostilities around 449 whatever the uncertainty over its formal terms and
oaths. The silence of the two great historians is itself a lesson in the limits of the
source tradition (see [[classical-greek-historiography]]).

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the formal end of the **Greco-Persian Wars** and the autonomy (in principle) of
the Asian Greeks; the **moral and institutional completion of the empire** — tribute now
flowed to Athena and the building program, not to a Persian war; and a half-century of
relative peace with Persia broken only by satrapal intrigue, until Persia re-entered Greek
affairs during the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]].
