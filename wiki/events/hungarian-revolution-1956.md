# Hungarian Revolution of 1956
## Narrative
In October 1956, inspired by political concessions in Poland (the return of Gomulka), Hungarian students and workers staged mass demonstrations in Budapest, demanding democratic reforms, freedom of the press, and the withdrawal of Soviet troops. The protests quickly escalated into a full-scale revolution. The Hungarian army largely joined the rebels, and the hated political police (AVH) were targeted. A revisionist communist, Imre Nagy, became Prime Minister and formed a multi-party coalition government, subsequently declaring Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. On November 4, 1956, Soviet tanks and troops stormed Budapest, crushing the revolution after fierce street fighting. Nagy was arrested and later executed, replaced by Soviet-loyal Janos Kadar.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` **Khrushchev's de-Stalinization campaign** (the Secret Speech in February 1956) which shattered the absolute authority of Stalinist regimes; **popular hatred of the repressive AVH** (Hungarian secret police); and the **Suez Crisis**, which distracted the Western democracies, split the Western alliance, and prevented any Western counter-measures.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the **brutal military suppression of Hungary**, resulting in thousands of deaths and the flight of over 200,000 Hungarian refugees to the West.
- `produced:` the **death of Imre Nagy**, who was arrested, tried in secret, and executed by the Soviet-installed Kadar government.
- `produced:` a **profound moral shock for international communism**, leading to widespread defections from communist parties in Western Europe and North America (Line 6713).
- `enabled:` the **restoration of Soviet dominance in Eastern Europe**, demonstrating that Khrushchev would not tolerate any satellite state leaving the Warsaw Pact or abandoning single-party rule.

## Actors
- **Nikita Khrushchev**, who ordered the invasion; **Imre Nagy**, the revisionist communist leader who declared neutrality; and the **Hungarian People** who fought the Soviet troops. See [[khrushchev-nikita]].

## Historiography
Riasanovsky highlights that the Soviet intervention in Hungary shattered the illusion of a peaceful transition to democratic socialism under Khrushchev, exposing the limits of the Khrushchev thaw.

From [[leffler-westad-cold-war-v1]] (Békés chapter): The 1956 events in Hungary (and parallel Polish October) were the product of de-Stalinization directed from Moscow, "predominantly political actions" in contrast to earlier 1953 economic-driven crises. "The radically diﬀerent means of handling the two crises illustrated the real boundaries of Soviet tolerance: Polish reforms preserving Communist rule and the unity of the Soviet alliance system were still tolerable, but the Hungarian revolution, which was rightly seen as the transformation of the regime into a Western-type democracy, had to be crushed." 

Detailed causal sequence (verbatim-grounded): Post-Stalin New Course imposed after 1953 crises (Plzeň, East German Berlin uprising of ~half million across 560 cities, crushed by Soviet tanks); "New Course" features included consumer goods priority, amnesties, reduced terror, slowed collectivization, collective leadership. De-Stalinization after Feb 1956 20th CPSU Congress Secret Speech (sent to allies; published in Poland June) emboldened reformers. Poznań June 28 1956 uprising (73 civilians +8 forces killed) prompted Soviet political intervention. In Hungary: Rajk reburial early Oct drew 100k+ silent protest; students issued "sixteen points" Oct 22 (Nagy as PM, troop withdrawal, multiparty elections, freedoms); Oct 23 demonstration escalated to armed revolt; initial Soviet intervention requested by Hungarian leaders but "radicalized the Hungarian population"; Nagy appointed PM Oct 24 but argued for placing party at head of the "broad national movement"; Oct 28-30: Moscow agreed program ("broad national movement" not "counterrevolution"), troops withdrew from Budapest, Oct 30 declaration promised "new foundation for relations ... based on mutual equality and noninterference" and to "consider the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Hungary entirely"; Chinese delegation influenced initial non-intervention decision. Reversal Oct 31 after reports of multiparty system, AVH dissolution, party collapse: "Communist power in Hungary was on the verge of collapse"; massive second invasion Nov 4 crushed revolution in days; Kádár installed; Nagy executed; 2,700 killed during revolution; 200,000 fled; post-reprisals 13k detained, >20k imprisoned, 230 executed.

Legacies (Békés): "the Western states, by not intervening, had proved once and for all their unconditional acceptance of the postwar European status quo." "the Hungarian revolution largely contributed to the success of the eﬀort to build a post-Stalinist Communist order in the Soviet Union and throughout East Central Europe." "ended any hopes in East Central Europe that the Soviet yoke could be thrown off by active revolt." "The Polish example demonstrated that a limited campaign of moderate reforms which did not imperil the political system, or threaten the security of the Eastern military bloc, could be implemented even against the will of the Soviet leadership."

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] add precise scale figures for
the October 23 trigger — some 200,000 protestors, mostly students, gathered in Budapest
chanting Petőfi's "We shall never again be slaves," in solidarity with Gomułka's Polish
reforms, after authorities banned the demonstration. They frame Nagy's withdrawal from
the Warsaw Pact and declaration of Hungarian neutrality as the fatal escalation — "going
too far" for Khrushchev, who used the international distraction of the `concurrent_with`
Suez Crisis to prepare the invasion while ostensibly removing garrisons (not a causal
link — the crises were simultaneous, not connected). Their invasion figures: ~2,500
Soviet tanks; ~4,000 Hungarian and 700 Soviet dead (note: this Hungarian death toll is
higher than the 2,700 given above from Békés/Leffler & Westad — both figures are
preserved here as a factual discrepancy between sources rather than resolved). Nagy took
refuge in the Yugoslav embassy, was kidnapped by Soviet special forces, and was tried and
executed two years later (1958). Post-invasion repression, per Paxton & Hessler: nearly
300 executions, at least 22,000 interned, and roughly 200,000 refugees (2% of the
population) fled west — broadly consistent with the reprisal figures above. They note
the long-term irony that Kádár's regime, installed by Soviet force, later became "the
most liberal, prosperous, and reform-minded of all the people's democracies" — the
"goulash communism" of subsequent decades (see [[brezhnev-stagnation]]).
