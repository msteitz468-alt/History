# The Gallic Sack of Rome

## Narrative

In the summer of **390 BCE** (by the Varronian reckoning; **387/386 BCE** by the
Greek synchronism derived from the Peace of Antalcidas), a war-band of Senonian
Gauls from the Po valley crossed the Apennines into northern Etruria and marched
southward. Their ultimate destination was probably Campania or Magna Graecia —
they appear to have been moving as a large raiding party or a mercenary force for
**Dionysius I of Syracuse**, who was active in Italy at precisely this period.

When the Gauls passed through Clusium (Chiusi), the Clusines appealed to Rome.
Roman envoys were sent — and violated diplomatic convention by taking up arms
alongside the Clusines. The Gauls, outraged, broke off negotiations and marched on
Rome. The Romans **hastily assembled an army and marched north** to intercept them
without the preparation such a force required.

The encounter at the **River Allia** (a small northern tributary of the Tiber,
approximately 11 miles north of Rome) on 18 July was a **catastrophic Roman
defeat**. The Roman army was routed and scattered; the day became the *dies Alliensis*,
the most inauspicious day in the Roman calendar. The road to Rome was undefended.

The Gauls entered Rome. Most of the civilian population fled; the **Capitol was
defended** by a garrison. The city itself was occupied and reportedly burned or
pillaged. After some weeks — the tradition says seven months, which is implausible
for a mobile war-band without a logistical base — the **Gauls accepted a ransom**
and departed. The tradition preserved the cry of the Gallic leader Brennus: *Vae
victis* ("Woe to the conquered"), when he threw his sword onto the scale as a
counterweight to the ransom gold.

**The role of Caere.** The Etruscan city of Caere (Cerveteri), though not a full
Roman ally, sheltered the **Vestal Virgins** and the sacred objects of Rome during
the occupation. The Caeretan tradition also claims a victory over part of the
Gallic force at the "Trausian Plain" that recovered the ransom gold — a story
possibly conflated with the Camillus legend but preserving a genuine memory of
Caeretan intervention. See [[caere]].

**Camillus and the "rescue."** The tradition holds that [[m-furius-camillus|M.
Furius Camillus]], then in exile at Ardea, raised a new army and arrived to defeat
the Gauls just as the ransom was being weighed — hence the famous exchange with
Brennus. Cornell's analysis is unsparing: this story is a **late fictional accretion**
and should be entirely rejected. The Gauls departed because they were paid or because
they had achieved their objectives; the Camillus rescue narrative was constructed to
efface the humiliation of the ransom. Camillus was a real figure and was likely
active in the campaigns of the following years, but the liberation story is an
invention.

## Chronological Dispute

The **Varronian chronology** places the sack in 390 BCE; the **Greek synchronism**
(Polybius I.6.2, drawing on Philistus or Timaeus) synchronizes the sack with the
Peace of Antalcidas (387/386 BCE), making the Polybian date four years later. The
discrepancy arises from the artificial insertions in the Varronian fasti: four
"Dictator Years" (333, 324, 309, 301 BCE in the Varronian system, years in which a
dictator was apparently inserted to lengthen the chronology) and a fictitious
"five-year anarchy" during the Licinian-Sextian rogations. These were devices by
late-Republican chronographers to harmonize conflicting source-chronologies; the
actual effect was to push the Varronian sack date back four years beyond the
Greek evidence. See [[reliability-of-early-roman-tradition]].

The consequence for other dates in the fourth century: **all absolute dates from
Varronian 390 to Varronian c. 301 BCE should be understood as approximately four
years earlier in actual chronology**, though the consular year within the fasti
sequence remains reliable as a relative marker.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` The Allia disaster was caused by the Romans intercepting a Gallic
force that was probably not specifically targeting Rome, with a hastily raised and
inadequately prepared army. The decision to fight rather than defend was militarily
reckless.

`contributed_to:` The Gallic movement southward was driven by the broader
**Gallic expansion into peninsular Italy** that had been ongoing for decades:
Celtic groups from Transalpine Gaul crossed the Alps and settled the Po valley in
the fifth century, progressively displacing Etruscan settlements in the north and
then raiding southward. The Romans had no prior experience fighting massed Gallic
infantry — the psychological impact was as significant as the military defeat.

`contributed_to:` Roman diplomatic missteps at Clusium — specifically the illegal
combat participation of the Fabii sent as envoys — gave the Gauls a legitimate
grievance and made the confrontation appear inevitable. The Gauls' demand that
Rome hand over the offending envoys was rejected.

## Consequence Analysis

**What the sack did NOT do.** Cornell's most important finding: the Gallic Sack
produced **no archaeological trace of a conflagration** at Rome. The Roman literary
tradition's claim that the city was burned to the ground is contradicted by the
material record. Subsequent Roman writers used the Sack as an explanation for the
loss of early public records (*tabulae*) — a convenient hypothesis that also explains
the historical problems with the early fasti. The explanation is not false (records
may have been lost), but the scale of destruction is greatly exaggerated.

**What the sack actually produced:**

1. **The Servian Wall (c. 378 BCE).** Rome's fortification of its circuit with a
   massive stone wall — the so-called "Servian Wall" (misattributed to king Servius
   Tullius; it dates to c. 378 BCE, after the sack) — was the most concrete
   consequence. The wall enclosed approximately 426 hectares, making it the largest
   circuit wall in Italy. It was a genuine defensive response to the demonstrated
   vulnerability.

2. **Military reform.** The development of the **manipular legion** (replacing the
   Hoplite phalanx with a more flexible tactical formation) is associated by the
   tradition with the post-Gallic period; the timing is plausible given the tactical
   failures of 390.

3. **Renewed Latin alliance.** The sack strained Rome's relations with the Latin
   cities — several defected during Rome's weakness — but the demonstrated fragility
   of peninsular Italy before Gallic raids also pushed Romans and Latins toward
   tighter alliance.

4. **Prestige damage and recovery.** The Gauls continued to raid peninsular Italy
   sporadically through the fourth century (documented raids in 360, 358, 354, 349 BCE
   in the tradition). Roman recovery from the sack was rapid and complete — by the
   350s Rome had resumed aggressive expansion against the Volsci, Latins, and Etruscans.
   The sack was a trauma but not a structural disruption.

5. **The Caeretan exception.** Caere's hospitality to the Vestals and sacred objects
   was rewarded with **hospitium publicum** (public guest-friendship) — Rome's first
   public declaration of this status with an external community. Caere later received
   *civitas sine suffragio* (citizenship without suffrage), possibly as early as the
   late fourth century.

## Actors

- **[[m-furius-camillus|M. Furius Camillus]]**: Five-time dictator; present in the
  aftermath and wars of the 380s–360s; the liberation story is legendary but his
  real historical role in the recovery period is significant.
- **Brennus**: Gallic war-leader; the name may be a title (*bren*, chieftain) rather
  than a personal name. His exchange with the Romans during the ransom weighing (*Vae
  victis*) is the most famous scene; its authenticity is uncertain.
- **[[caere|Caere]]**: Etruscan city that sheltered Rome's Vestals; played the
  central supporting role in the tradition.
- **[[latin-league|The Latin League]]**: The reliability of Latin support fluctuated
  during Rome's crisis; some Latin cities took the opportunity to defect or pursue
  independent policies.

## Historiography

The Gallic Sack is one of the most thoroughly analyzed events in Roman history
precisely because it presents a **paradox**: the most catastrophic event in early
Republican history left almost no archaeological trace.

**The destruction question.** Cornell is categorical: no stratum of burning or
destruction datable to c. 390 BCE has been identified at Rome. This does not mean
the Gauls did not sack Rome — it means the destruction was limited and selective,
not the city-wide conflagration the literary sources describe. A war-band occupying
a city for weeks would have looted and burned individual buildings; a systematic
urban destruction would have left a clear material signature.

**The Camillus legend.** The tradition's most dramatic element — Camillus arriving
ex machina just as the ransom gold was weighed — has been rejected as legendary by
virtually all modern scholars. Cornell (CAH VII.2) dismisses it explicitly. The
function of the legend was to efface the ransom: Romans paid off the Gauls, and the
tradition found this intolerable.

**The "dictator years" problem and chronology.** See the Appendix in CAH VII.2
(Cornell), which demonstrates that the four "Dictator Years" (Varronian 333, 324,
309, 301) inserted into the Fasti Capitolini were late fabrications to extend the
fourth-century chronology and reconcile conflicting sources. The Varronian 390 date
for the sack should be understood as four years too early; the Polybian date of
387/386 BCE is more likely correct. This affects all dates in the Varronian
framework between 390 and c. 301.

**Source quality.** The accounts of Livy (Books V–VI) and Dionysius of Halicarnassus
(XIII–XIV) are both derived from late-Republican annalists (primarily Claudius
Quadrigarius and Valerius Antias) who had access to oral tradition, clan records,
and their own literary imagination in unknown proportions. Polybius I.6.2 provides
the crucial external synchronism. The Greek historians (Theopompus; fragments) knew
of the sack; its historicity is not in question.
