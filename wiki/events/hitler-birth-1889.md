# Birth of Adolf Hitler (20 April 1889)

## Narrative

At half-past six in the evening on 20 April 1889, an overcast and chilly Easter Saturday, Klara Hitler gave birth to her fourth child (first to survive infancy) in the Gasthof zum Pommer, Vorstadt Nr. 219, Braunau am Inn, on the Austro-Bavarian border. The child was named Adolf.

In the opening of *Mein Kampf*, Hitler later stressed the "providential" character of his birth on the border between the two countries he would seek to unite.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the marriage and family circumstances of Alois Hitler (customs official, recently widowed from second wife Franziska Matzelberger) and Klara Pölzl (second cousin and former household maid; Church dispensation required for the cousin marriage). `contributed_to:` the material stability of a lower-middle-class provincial official's household (cook, maid present). `preceded_by:` Alois's name change from Schicklgruber (1876) and social climbing in the Austrian customs service.

The immediate family context included half-siblings from Alois's prior unions and the recent deaths of earlier infants (Gustav, Ida, Otto).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the individual whose later career would reshape the twentieth century. `enabled:` the childhood moves (to Passau 1892, Linz area) and the specific formation (father's authoritarianism, mother's affection, border identity) that Kershaw traces as foundational to Hitler's worldview. The birth itself was unremarkable locally; its `scale_consequential` derives entirely from subsequent events.

`followed_by:` childhood instability, school failures, Vienna years, WWI, political entry 1919.

## Historiography

Kershaw (Range 01, Ch. 1) stresses the ordinariness of the provincial birth and the sparse, retrospective, often inaccurate record (Hitler's own *Mein Kampf* account "inaccurate in detail and coloured"; post-war family recollections and Third Reich glorification efforts are "dubious"). No contemporary portent; the "first of many strokes of good fortune" was the 1876 name change that spared the future leader the name "Schicklgruber." Family background (illegitimacy rumors around Alois's paternity, incestuous cousin ties) is treated skeptically; Hans Frank's later Graz Jew grandfather story is "valueless."

Emphasizes material comfort alongside emotional distance from father and later trauma of mother's death (1907). Links birth/border identity to Hitler's later pan-German fixation without teleology.
