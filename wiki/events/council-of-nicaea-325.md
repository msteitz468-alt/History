# The Council of Nicaea

The **Council of Nicaea** (325), summoned and presided over by [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]],
was the **first ecumenical council** of the Christian church — convened to settle the **Arian
controversy** over the relationship of Christ to God the Father and to fix the date of Easter. Treated
in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH XII]] Ch. 4 (**Averil Cameron**).

## Narrative

Having become sole emperor in 324, Constantine inherited a church split by the **Arian** dispute
(whether the Son was of the same substance as, and co-eternal with, the Father, or a created being).
Determined — after his earlier failure with the **Donatist** schism — to settle matters decisively, he
summoned the bishops (preponderantly eastern; the count is traditionally **318** but was probably
lower) to **Nicaea** in Bithynia, providing for all their needs at imperial expense. Eusebius of
Caesarea describes Constantine's entrance "like some heavenly angel of God." With "amazing speed and
harmony" the council agreed a **creed**, fixed the date of **Easter**, and produced the clinching term
***homoousios*** ("of the same substance") — said to have been the **emperor's own contribution**.
Only a few, including **Arius**, refused, and were exiled.

## Causal Analysis

The council was driven by the **Arian controversy**'s threat to church unity, and by Constantine's
conviction — now backed by the resources of the state — that it was his duty to secure doctrinal
concord in his empire.

## Consequence Analysis

Nicaea's apparent success was **fragile**. Within a short time Constantine's mind changed: the exiles
were recalled, and he was **baptized on his deathbed by the pro-Arian Eusebius of Nicomedia** (337).
The emperor left a "double-edged legacy": his three sons favored Arianism, and the reign of
**Constantius II** was punctuated by renewed conflict and the repeated exiles of **Athanasius**. But
Nicaea set two enduring precedents: the **Nicene Creed** as the touchstone of orthodoxy, and — more
broadly — the **emperor's role in defining and enforcing doctrine**, the foundation of Eusebius'
theory of Christian monarchy that would serve Byzantium for centuries.

## Significance

As the first ecumenical council held in the emperor's presence, Nicaea fused **imperial and
ecclesiastical authority** in a way that shaped the entire subsequent history of church and state. It
is the doctrinal centerpiece of the [[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization]] of the
Roman world.

## Historiography

Nicaea is among the best-documented early councils (canons, creed, later ecclesiastical historians) yet remains interpretive: was Constantine a humble “minister” of the bishops or the real director of outcomes? How far was *homoousios* a genuine majority faith rather than an imposed formula? Modern accounts (CAH XII–XIII; Arian-controversy literature) stress the unfinished character of 325 — the dispute’s real settlement came only under [[theodosius-i|Theodosius]]. Gibbon’s portrait (below) is the classic anticlerical reading of creed-making under imperial power.

## From Gibbon, Decline and Fall (1776–1788)

[[actors/gibbon-edward|Gibbon]]’s Nicaea is the first great fusion of imperial coercion and metaphysical creed-making. **Gibbon’s views.**

- **318 bishops; emperor as “minister”:** Constantine sat on a low stool without guards; “he humbly professed that he was the minister, not the judge, of the successors of the apostles,” yet influenced debates among men “established as priests and as gods upon earth.”
- **Homoousion as ambiguous glue:** Arians rejected it; the majority used “the mysterious Homoousion, which either party was free to interpret according to their peculiar tenets,” to cut off heresy while uniting Catholics who leaned toward tritheism or Sabellianism — the ball of orthodoxy “tremulous.”
- **From indifference to coercion:** moderating letter treated the dispute as trifling subtlety; then Osius’s credit and insinuation against Eusebius of Nicomedia as Licinius’s friend swung him; “those who resisted the divine judgment of the synod, must prepare themselves for an immediate exile” (opposition reduced from ~17 to 2 bishops).
- **Fifty-year contrast:** “Within the space of fifty years… Tacitus in the senate of Rome, and Constantine in the council of Nice” — both bodies degenerated, but bishops more rooted in public opinion.

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Nicaea as first empire-wide attempt to define core beliefs after Constantine’s sole rule (**324**) made unified church imperial policy (practical: who gets privileges/judicial powers; ideological: autocracy needs monolithic religion). Constantine hoped Antioch would clear doctrine so Nicaea could focus on unity, Easter date, and hierarchy — theology took precedence. Creed placed Arius beyond pale: Christ begotten from Father’s substance, not created from nothing; Hosius insisted on *homoousios*. Same council handled Melitians/Novatians in spirit of reconciliation and can. 6 Alexandrian regional authority. Failure: doctrinal controversy thicket until Theodosius’s Constantinople **381**; much “driven not by theology but by politics.” “Despite Constantine’s best efforts the decisions at Nicaea failed to resolve the dogmatic issues about the nature of Christian belief.”
