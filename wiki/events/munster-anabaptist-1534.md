# Anabaptist Kingdom of Münster (1534–1535)

Short-lived radical Anabaptist regime in the Westphalian city of Münster that established a theocratic "kingdom" under prophetic leadership, practised community of goods and polygamy, and waged a defensive holy war until its bloody overthrow. One of the most extreme expressions of sectarian apocalyptic religious warfare in the period.

## Narrative

After the city council fell under radical influence and Lutherans were expelled, Dutch and local Anabaptists led by Jan Matthijs and then Jan van Leiden (John of Leiden) took control. Declaring Münster the New Jerusalem, they instituted adult baptism, common ownership, and (after 1534) polygamy under "King" John. When besieged by the prince-bishop's forces, they framed the defence in eschatological and purgative terms. The city fell in June 1535; the leaders were tortured and executed, their bodies displayed in cages.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis (Housley framing)

Exemplifies the "sectarian apocalypticism" pattern: a group convinced it was God's elect with a mandate to prepare the way for Christ's return through sanctified violence. Closest 16th-c. parallel to early Taborite extremism (Housley). Unlike Tabor, it did not evolve into a stable community; its defeat produced a powerful negative lesson for other Anabaptist groups, accelerating the shift toward pacifism (Mennonites etc.).

**contributed_to:** Long-term marginalisation of radical Anabaptism; strengthening of confessional state control over religious expression.

## Historiography

Contemporary accounts (Catholic and magisterial Protestant) emphasised monstrosity and sexual licence. Modern scholarship places it within the broader "radical Reformation" and the social/urban crises of the 1520s–30s. Housley uses it comparatively with Tabor to delimit the most extreme form of holy war in the era — celebratory/purgative rather than merely defensive or crusading.
