# The Battle of Bannockburn (1314)

At **Bannockburn**, near Stirling, the Scottish army of **[[robert-the-bruce|Robert I (the
Bruce)]]** destroyed the great English host of **[[edward-ii-of-england|Edward II]]** — the decisive
battle of the **Wars of Scottish Independence**. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (the Scotland
chapter) ranks it "with Courtrai (1302) and Morgarten (1315) in military history, and... probably
even more significant, for it taught English commanders the value of defensively sited infantry."

## Narrative

After [[edward-i-plantagenet|Edward I's]] conquest had collapsed and Robert the Bruce had isolated
and defeated his Comyn/Balliol enemies, by mid-1314 only a few strongholds held against him —
notably **Stirling Castle**. When Edward II marched north to relieve the siege in June 1314,
"Robert's army of spearmen destroyed the English host at Bannockburn." The Scots' tightly packed
**schiltrons** (spear formations) on chosen ground broke the English cavalry and routed the army.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Wars of Scottish Independence** — Edward I's attempt to make Scotland "a mere
  adjunct of the realm of England" after adjudicating the Balliol–Bruce succession (1292) and
  imposing his overlordship, and the sustained "people's war" of resistance (William Wallace,
  Stirling Bridge 1297).
- `contributed_to:` **Robert the Bruce's rising of 1306** and his reconquest of Scotland after Edward
  I's death (1307).

## Consequence Analysis

Bannockburn **ended the English occupation of southern Scotland** (though Berwick was held until
1318) and effectively secured Scottish independence. Edward II would not recognise it, so Robert
intensified the **border raids** and launched his brother Edward Bruce's (failed) invasion of
**Ireland (1315–1318)**; English invasions were defeated by Robert's **scorched-earth strategy**.
The stalemate, and Edward II's deposition (1327), led to the **Treaty of Edinburgh (1328)**, by
which the English government confirmed Scotland's independence. Militarily, Bannockburn — with
Courtrai and Morgarten — was a landmark in the late-medieval **infantry revolution**, the humbling
of mounted chivalry by disciplined footsoldiers (a lesson the English themselves applied at
[[hundred-years-war-1337|Crécy and Poitiers]]).
