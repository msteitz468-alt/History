# The Fall of Nineveh and of Assyria (614–609 BCE)

The **fall of Nineveh** in **612 BCE** — and the destruction of the
[[neo-assyrian-empire|Neo-Assyrian Empire]] that it sealed — is one of the great
discontinuities of ancient history: the sudden collapse, "within two decades of
[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's]] death," of "the greatest empire the world had
known." [[boardman-cah-v3p2-1991|CAH Vol. III, Part 2]] (Ch. 25, J. Oates) is the
authority. It is a standing transition-page subject.

## Narrative

After [[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal]] died (627) and the Chaldaean
**[[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]]** seized Babylon (626), a decade of war wrested
Babylonia from Assyria. From **616** Nabopolassar struck north. The decisive blows
came from the **[[medes|Medes]] under Cyaxares**: in **614** they destroyed
**Ashur**, the ancient religious capital, and Nabopolassar **allied with Cyaxares**
on the battlefield. In **612**, after a **three-month siege**, the combined
Median–Babylonian armies (with the **Ummanmanda**, including
[[scythians|Scythians]]) breached the walls and **sacked Nineveh** — "turned [it]
into a ruin heap." The Assyrian king **Sin-sharra-ishkun** perished (probably in
the flames of his palace, the kernel of the Greek "Sardanapalus" legend). A
remnant under **Ashur-uballit II** held out at **Harran**, lost it in **610**, and
disappeared after a failed Egyptian rescue in **609**. Assyria was finished.

## Causal Analysis

Oates's analysis is structural, not moral:
`caused_by:` the **smallness and exhaustion of the Assyrian homeland** — its
population and agriculture could not sustain the empire its kings had built;
tribute required perpetual force, and subject peoples "had no reason to risk their
lives in its defence in times of trouble."
`contributed_to:` by [[assyrian-mass-deportation|mass deportation]], which left a
"growing legacy of subject peoples ... whose loyalty in times of weakness the
state could not ensure"; by the over-reach of the Sargonid conquests (Egypt, the
destruction of Elam, endless Babylonian war); and decisively by
[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's]] **failure to secure the succession**, which
shattered the royal authority on which the Assyrian system depended.
`caused_by:` the **[[medes|Median]]–Babylonian coalition** — the proximate
military cause, drawing on "the vast wealth of the southern tribes allied with the
successful commercialism of the Babylonian cities, which Assyrian policies had
themselves fostered."

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **end of Assyria as a political entity** — though Oates stresses it
was less a Roman-style collapse into chaos than a **transfer of power from Nineveh
to Babylon**, "an adjacent and ethnically related kingdom," with administration and
geography largely intact (later perfected by the [[achaemenid-empire|Achaemenids]],
whose imperial system descended from Assyria's).
`produced:` the brief supremacy of the **[[neo-babylonian-empire|Neo-Babylonian
Empire]]** and the **[[medes|Median]]** kingdom — the two heirs that divided the
Assyrian world.
`enabled:` the [[battle-of-carchemish-605bce|confrontation of Babylon and Egypt at
Carchemish (605)]] for the Assyrian inheritance in Syria.
The Assyrian heartland itself **depopulated**: squatters sheltered in the ruins of
Calah; when Xenophon marched past in 401 the cities were nameless ruins he took for
abandoned "Median" towns.

## Liverani: a two-phase collapse

[[liverani-ancient-near-east-2014|Liverani (2014)]] frames the fall as two phases — an Assyrian
**civil war** (~635–626 BCE; the Babylonian Chronicle calls 626 a year "without a king") then
**rapid military collapse** — and stresses its structural inevitability in hindsight:
[[ashurbanipal|Ashurbanipal's]] destruction of **Elam** had removed the buffer against the
Iranian powers, **Median** and Mannaean state-formation had been contained but not stopped, and
old rivalries (with Urartu) prevented strategic alliance against the northern threat. The
sequence (Babylonian Chronicle, his Text 31.1): the **Medes under Cyaxares** sacked **Ashur**
in 614 (arriving before [[nabopolassar|Nabopolassar]], with whom they then allied at its
ruins), the combined armies destroyed **Nineveh** after a three-month siege in 612
(Sin-shar-ishkun dying in it), and took **Harran** in 610, ending the rump Assyrian state under
Ashur-uballit II. Egypt's pro-Assyrian intervention failed at **Carchemish** (605) against
Nebuchadnezzar. See [[ancient-near-east-historiography]].

## Historiography

The chronology of the empire's last years is one of the **hardest problems in
Assyriology** (no Eponym Chronicle after 649; the reigns of Ashur-etel-ilani and
Sin-sharra-ishkun undated), and rests on Oates's reconstruction from the
**Babylonian "Fall of Nineveh" Chronicle**, the Warka King List, and economic
tablets. The biblical prophet **Nahum** exults over Nineveh's fall, recalling
Assyria's own [[sack-of-thebes-663bce|sack of Thebes]]; Greek tradition
(Sardanapalus, Ctesias) preserves a garbled romance. The tradition that the walls
were breached by **flooding the Khosr** (Nahum 2:6) is plausible but
archaeologically unconfirmed.
