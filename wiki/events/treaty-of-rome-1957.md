# Treaties of Rome and the Founding of the Common Market

## Narrative

The dismantling of Europe's colonial empires (see [[algerian-war-1954]], [[suez-crisis-1956]]) pointed toward a permanent loss of European power, but a countermovement centered on Europe itself — the drive for European unity — offered a new avenue for collective action. Two impulses drove it: the vow never again to allow European national rivalries to provoke war, and the recognition that no individual Western European state could act freely on a world stage now dominated by two superpowers.

The architects were disproportionately drawn from the Catholic Rhineland borderlands: France's Robert Schuman (a former German officer and French deputy for restored Alsace), West Germany's Konrad Adenauer (Catholic mayor of Cologne under the Weimar Republic, interned under the Nazis, first chancellor of the Federal Republic from 1949), and Italy's Alcide De Gasperi. They were joined by anti-Communist Socialists such as Belgium's Paul-Henri Spaak and by technocrats such as Jean Monnet, who worked for a unified European economy on the scale of the United States.

The first attempt at common political institutions, the Council of Europe (Strasbourg, May 1949), was blocked from acquiring supranational authority by British insistence (under both Labour and Conservative governments) on preserving national sovereignty and Commonwealth trade preferences; its main lasting achievement was the European Convention on Human Rights (1950). With the parliamentary route blocked, integration proceeded instead through limited, sector-specific supranational institutions. Schuman's 1950 proposal to place French and German coal and steel production under "a common High Authority" — explicitly framed as "the first step in the federation of Europe," making war between France and Germany "not merely inconceivable but physically impossible" — produced the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) in 1951, administered by a nine-member High Authority with genuine supranational power to set prices, levy fees, and direct investment across France, West Germany, Italy, and the Benelux states (the "Six").

The ECSC's success, coinciding with the postwar economic boom, pointed toward further integration just as the more ambitious European Defense Community (a supranational European army proposed to contain German rearmament) was defeated in the French parliament in June 1954. The immediate impetus for the next step was the European humiliation of the Suez Crisis (1956). The **Treaties of Rome**, signed March 25, 1957, and effective January 1, 1958, united the Six in two new agencies: the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom) and the **European Economic Community (EEC)** — the Common Market.

## Causal Analysis

The Treaties of Rome were `caused_by` the ECSC's demonstrated success as a model of sector-by-sector integration and `contributed_to` by the collapse of the more ambitious European Defense Community, which discredited full military-political federation as a near-term goal. They were `contributed_to` by the shared humiliation of Suez, which underscored the need for an independent European power base. They were `preceded_by`, but not directly `caused_by`, the Council of Europe, whose failure to achieve supranational authority redirected the integration movement toward economic rather than parliamentary means.

## Consequence Analysis

The Treaties `produced` a single free-trade area roughly comparable to the United States in size and population, within which goods, capital, and workers moved freely; internal tariffs among the Six were eliminated by 1968, ahead of the original 12–15 year schedule. The Common Market `enabled` sustained postwar prosperity (see the "economic miracle") and `produced` labor migration from southern Italy to France and Germany. It `enabled` — though under a French veto that slowed the process considerably under de Gaulle (the 1965–1966 "empty chair" crisis, resolved by the Luxembourg compromise) — decades of further institutional deepening: merger of the ECSC, Euratom, and EEC into the European Community (1967); direct election of the European Parliament (1979); the European Monetary System (1979); and successive enlargements (Britain, Ireland, Denmark in 1973; Greece 1981; Spain and Portugal 1986), culminating eventually in the modern European Union. See also [[german-european-integration]] for the German-specific trajectory of this process.

## Actors

Robert Schuman — French foreign minister whose 1950 plan launched supranational European integration. [[konrad-adenauer|Konrad Adenauer]] — West German chancellor and essential partner. Jean Monnet — technocratic architect of the sector-by-sector integration method. Paul-Henri Spaak — Belgian statesman, first president of the Council of Europe and later NATO secretary-general. Walter Hallstein — first president of the EEC Commission (1958–1967), an advocate of the Treaty of Rome as a "constitutional document."

## Historiography

Andrew Moravcsik, *The Choice for Europe: Social Purpose and State Power from Messina to Maastricht* (1998), and Alan Milward, *The European Rescue of the Nation State* (2nd ed., 2000), both argue integration was driven by member states pursuing national ends rather than by supranational idealism — a revisionist counter to the "Monnet method" mythology. John Gillingham, *Coal, Steel, and the Rebirth of Europe, 1945–1955* (new ed., 2004), is the standard account of the ECSC's origins. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]], Chapter 17.
