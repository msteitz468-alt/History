# Palmer Raids (1919-1920)

The **Palmer Raids** were a series of federal roundups of suspected anarchists,
communists, and radical labor activists carried out by the US Department of Justice under
Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer in November 1919 and January 1920, at the height of
the First Red Scare. The raids followed a wave of anarchist bombings in 1919, including one
that damaged Palmer's own Washington home, and were coordinated in large part by a young
[[hoover-j-edgar|J. Edgar Hoover]], newly appointed head of the Justice Department's
Radical Division, who built an extensive card-index system tracking suspected radicals by
name, organization, and ideology.

## Conduct and Aftermath

Thousands were arrested, often without warrants and with limited access to counsel; the
December 1919 deportation of 249 people, including Emma Goldman and Alexander Berkman,
aboard the ship *Buford* became a symbolic culmination of the campaign. Palmer had
predicted a major radical uprising on May Day 1920 that did not materialize, and mounting
public and legal criticism of the raids' constitutional abuses (highlighted by a group of
prominent lawyers' 1920 report, *To the American People*) discredited Palmer and ended the
campaign, though it established institutional and methodological precedents — mass
surveillance files, coordinated federal-state action against radicals — that shaped later
domestic intelligence practice.

## Historiography

The raids are generally treated by historians as a paradigmatic episode of civil-liberties
overreach driven by wartime and postwar panic, and as formative for Hoover's subsequent
career and methods (see [[political-surveillance]]), which historians have traced directly
back to lessons — both operational and reputational — drawn from 1919–1920.
