# Aksumite Decline and Capital Relocation

## Narrative

By the middle decades of the sixth century, economic decline was becoming apparent in the Aksum region. Archaeological survey (Michels 2005) shows sharp depopulation of the capital area. Grand buildings fell into disrepair and were occupied by squatters; the Gobedra quarries that supplied massive masonry were abruptly abandoned mid-extraction; gold coinage suffered accelerating debasement followed by a proliferation of tiny base-metal issues suggesting inflation.

The decline was multi-causal. Population concentration in and around Aksum during the fourth- and fifth-century peak had depleted resources — above all timber — and generated environmental deterioration (first argued by Butzer 1981; supported with revision by French et al. 2009; Sulas et al. 2009). King Kaleb's large army deployed across the Red Sea into South Arabia in the early sixth century represented a major manpower drain; Procopius records that many soldiers, particularly slaves, refused to return. This depopulation around the capital compounded the resource problem.

The structural blow, however, was the loss of the Red Sea trade axis. Control of the Red Sea had been shared between Aksum and its Byzantine ally. The **Persian seizure of Himyar in the 570s** and the subsequent **Arab conquests of the seventh century** transferred that control definitively. **Adulis** — Aksum's great port — was destroyed by Arab forces. The kingdom was cut off from the Mediterranean markets for which it had produced ivory, gold, civet, and incense for centuries. The supply of these export goods was itself diminishing: gold from near Aksum was exhausted (requiring distant expeditions, noted by Cosmas Indicopleustes); elephant herds near the capital had been protected as early as c. 530 CE (Nonnosus), suggesting ivory was already scarce.

Arabic historians writing some generations later record that Aksum ceased to be the political capital and was replaced by **Kubar** — a place whose location is unconfirmed but believed to lie to the southeast of Aksum, possibly a mobile royal court rather than a fixed city (as became standard practice in the second millennium). The relocation probably occurred in or around the early seventh century; it was complete by c. 700 CE. Aksum itself was not abandoned — ecclesiastical establishments continued — but its non-ecclesiastical buildings and monuments fell into decay. A Ge'ez inscription of late-eighth or ninth century in the name of a ruler Dana'el at Aksum indicates disputed control of the area. Later traditions record a queen "Gudit" whose depredations included toppling stelae.

In eastern Tigray, by contrast, settlement density and prosperity increased. Churches — first funerary hypogea, then the grander "Tigray cross-in-square" rock-hewn churches — were created in sequence from roughly the seventh through the tenth centuries. These represent active, prosperous Christian communities maintaining and developing Aksumite cultural forms. The capital's eastward shift and the florescence of eastern Tigray Christianity thus form one continuous transformation.

## Actors

- **Kingdom of Aksum** (`[[aksum]]`): the declining state; its last coins are those of King Armah, associated in Muslim tradition with the Aksumite king who sheltered early followers of the Prophet (first hijra c. 615 CE). Muslim shrine at Negash in eastern Tigray preserves this memory.
- **Arab Caliphate**: the external force whose Red Sea expansion destroyed the trading basis of Aksumite prosperity and severed the Byzantium–Aksum axis.

## Historiography

The timing of the capital's relocation was debated. Early scholarship placed Aksum's "terminal decline" in the tenth or eleventh century; by the early 1980s numismatic and archaeological evidence (Munro-Hay 1989a) demonstrated that decline had occurred c. 700 CE. This revision was significant: it created a three-hundred-year gap before the Zagwe dynasty that demanded reinterpretation of the post-Aksumite period. Phillipson (2012, Ch. 16) argues the decline was transformation, not catastrophe, pointing to active church-building in eastern Tigray through the ninth–twelfth centuries. Reader (*Africa: A Biography*) proposes near-total environmental collapse by c. 800 CE; Phillipson's evidence contradicts this. See `[[aksumite-decline-causes]]`.
