# Citizen Genet Affair (1793)

The arrival of Edmond Charles Genet (Citizen Genet) as the new French minister to the United States in 1793 created a major diplomatic crisis that tested the young republic's neutrality.

## Narrative

Genet arrived in Charleston, South Carolina in April 1793 and proceeded to Philadelphia. He immediately began commissioning privateers, recruiting Americans for expeditions against Spanish and British possessions, and appealing directly to the American public over the head of the government. He expected enthusiastic support from the United States under the 1778 treaties of alliance and commerce.

Jefferson, as Secretary of State, dealt with Genet extensively. While sympathetic to the French Revolution in principle, Jefferson insisted on American neutrality and worked to restrain Genet's activities. The administration ultimately requested (and received) Genet's recall by the French government. Genet chose to remain in the United States rather than return to what might have been a dangerous situation in France.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The French Revolutionary leadership's belief that the United States owed active support because of the 1778 alliance and shared republican principles.

**contributed_to:** Genet's overestimation of American public support and underestimation of the Washington administration's determination to stay neutral.

## Consequence Analysis

The affair helped consolidate the policy of neutrality. It damaged the French position in American public opinion over time and gave Federalists ammunition against pro-French Republicans. It also established important precedents regarding the reception and conduct of foreign ministers.

## Actors

- Edmond Genet (overreached and was recalled)
- Thomas Jefferson (handled much of the diplomacy while trying to preserve Franco-American relations)
- George Washington (supported firm defense of neutrality)
- Alexander Hamilton (pushed for stronger measures against Genet)

## Historiography

Malone presents Jefferson as showing high technical skill and patience in managing the crisis while under political attack from both sides. The episode is often used to illustrate the limits of revolutionary enthusiasm in the early American republic and the emergence of a more realist foreign policy.
