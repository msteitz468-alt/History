# Legislative Commission

## Narrative
The **Legislative Commission** of 1767–1768 was a grand representative assembly convened by Empress Catherine the Great to draft a new code of laws for the Russian Empire, guided by the principles of the European Enlightenment (L3340-3342).

To guide the deputies, Catherine II spent eighteen months composing the *Nakaz* (Instruction), a liberal document heavily influenced by Montesquieu and Beccaria, which denounced capital punishment and torture, though it left autocracy and serfdom legally unchallenged (L3342-3346).

The Commission opened in Moscow in the summer of 1767, consisting of 564 deputies representing various classes of the empire: 28 appointed from state institutions, 161 from the landed gentry, 208 from the townspeople, 79 from the state peasants, and 88 from the Cossacks and national minorities (L3348). Serfs, who composed half the population, were excluded (L3348). 

The deputies brought over a thousand written instructions (*nakazy*) detailing the grievances of their electorates (L3348-3350). The Commission met for a year and a half in 203 sessions (L3350). However, the debates stalled due to deep class conflicts, particularly between the gentry and merchants over trade rights and serf ownership, and between gentry and peasants over serfdom (L3350). In late 1768, Catherine II utilized the outbreak of war with Turkey as a pretext to disband the Commission (L3352).

## Causal Analysis
The commission was caused by:
* **Legal Obsolescence:** Russian law had not been codified since the Ulozhenie of 1649, before the Westernization and expansion of the state (L3342).
* **Enlightened Despotism:** Catherine II sought to demonstrate her adherence to the principles of the Enlightenment and win international prestige among European intellectuals (L3342, L3396).

## Consequence Analysis
The Legislative Commission produced:
* **Socio-Historical Records:** The compiled instructions (*nakazy*) from the electorates provided the state and future historians with a comprehensive map of the socio-economic grievances, desires, and class alignments of 18th-century Russian society (L3350).
* **Class Polarization:** The debates exposed the deep class animosities within the empire, particularly regarding the institution of serfdom, foreshadowing the social violence of the Pugachev Rebellion (L3350-L3352, L3358).
* **Administrative Reforms:** Although the Commission failed to produce a new legal code, the information gathered directly influenced Catherine II's subsequent administrative legislation, most notably the Local Government Reform of 1775 and the Charter to the Nobility of 1785 (L3352, L3374, L3382).

## Historiography
The *nakazy* (deputies' instructions) compiled during the Commission are a celebrated primary source for eighteenth-century Russian social history, extensively mined by historians for evidence of gentry, merchant, and peasant grievances — though this entry relies on a secondary synthesis rather than the instructions themselves. Scholars have long debated the sincerity of Catherine's *Nakaz*: a genuine attempt at Enlightened reform constrained by political reality, or primarily a work of self-presentation aimed at her reputation among Voltaire, Diderot, and other Enlightenment correspondents? The Commission's failure is variously read as proof that autocracy and serfdom were structurally incompatible with liberal reform, or as evidence Catherine never intended a binding legal code and used the Commission chiefly for legitimation and information-gathering. Comparative work situates it within the broader European phenomenon of "enlightened despotism," alongside Joseph II's and Frederick II's reforms.
