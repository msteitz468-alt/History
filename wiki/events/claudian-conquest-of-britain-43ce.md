# The Claudian Conquest of Britain (43 CE)

The Roman invasion that brought **southern Britain** into the empire — launched by
**[[claudius|Claudius]]** in 43 CE primarily to win the **military prestige** his insecure
accession lacked. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v10-1996|CAH X]] Ch. 5 (Wiedemann; the campaign
detail in Ch. 13e).

## Narrative

Two generations after Julius Caesar's raids and Augustus' and Caligula's abandoned projects,
Claudius ordered a full invasion. He **entrusted the campaign to Aulus Plautius**, the loyal
legate of Pannonia — a man who had stood by him during Scribonianus' revolt — with four
legions (including **Legio II Augusta** under the future emperor **Vespasian**). The Britons
"presented considerably fewer problems" than feared; Plautius secured the south-east, and
**Claudius himself came in person** for the climactic capture of **Camulodunum (Colchester)**,
capital of the Catuvellauni, before returning to Rome for a **triumph**.

## Causal Analysis

Wiedemann presents the conquest as driven above all by the **logic of imperial legitimacy**:

- `caused_by:` **Claudius' need for military *virtus*** — the cardinal qualification of a Roman
  *imperator*, which the scholar-emperor conspicuously lacked. Britain offered glory at low
  risk.
- `contributed_to:` **the symbolic prize of the Ocean** — since Caesar's day, conquering an
  island "beyond the edge of the world" signified that even lands beyond the *orbis terrarum*
  obeyed Rome.
- `contributed_to:` **a strategic by-product** — removing legions from the Rhine, where
  Caligula's new units had made the German commands "too powerful."
- A British **dynastic appeal** (the exiled Verica) supplied the pretext.

## Consequence Analysis

The conquest created the province of **Britannia** and bound the consulars who accompanied
Claudius more tightly to him (he distributed *ornamenta triumphalia* to them). It opened nearly
**four centuries of Roman Britain**. The British king **Caratacus** continued resistance until
his capture and famous display at Rome (51). The exactions of the occupation — including the
calling-in of Seneca's loans — helped provoke **Boudica's revolt** (60) under [[nero|Nero]].

## Historiography

The political framing is Wiedemann's; the military narrative depends on Cassius Dio and
archaeology (CAH X Ch. 13e). The conquest is the textbook case of an emperor manufacturing a
war for **domestic legitimacy** rather than strategic necessity — a counterpart in the West to
the propaganda conquests analysed in [[augustan-imperial-expansion]].
