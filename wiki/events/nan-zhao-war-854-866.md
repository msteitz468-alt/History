# The Nan Zhao War in An Nam (854–866)

A decade-long war fought over the Tang **Protectorate of An Nam** (the Red River lands) between the
[[tang-dynasty|Tang]] empire and the kingdom of **Nan Zhao** of Yunnan. In K. W. Taylor's account
([[taylor-history-of-the-vietnamese-2013|*A History of the Vietnamese*]]) the war is significant less
as a border episode than as an early expression of a structural fault line that would recur for a
thousand years: competition between the elites of the Red River plain and those of the southern plains
of the Ma and Ca Rivers.

## Narrative

The war grew out of decades of tension between the pro-Tang Red River elite and southern-province and
upland leaders more willing to deal with the rising power of Nan Zhao. In 854 a breakdown in the upland
horse-and-salt trade and the execution of the frontier magnate **Do Ton Thanh** — accused of dealing
with Nan Zhao — stiffened southern resistance to Tang authority. In 861 the Do family, allied with Nan
Zhao, seized the protectoral capital **Dai La**. A full-scale Nan Zhao invasion in 862–863 destroyed the
Tang forces and captured Dai La in early 863, with mass drownings of Tang soldiers fleeing across the Red
River. The Tang general **Gao Pian** reconquered the protectorate in 865, retaking Dai La by year's end
(reportedly executing some 30,000 captured defenders) and then rebuilding the city, restoring its dikes
and canals, and endowing it with roads, bridges, shrines, and a literary reputation that made him a
culture-hero in later Vietnamese tradition.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: the 854 rupture of the frontier trade order and the execution of Do Ton Thanh.
- contributed_to: the standing factional split between the Red River plain (invested in the Tang order)
  and the southern/upland leadership (open to Nan Zhao).
- enabled: the general decay of Tang imperial control in the ninth century, which left the far-southern
  protectorate exposed.

## Consequence Analysis

Tang recovery under Gao Pian is, for Taylor, striking evidence that the empire still regarded An Nam as
integral to its inheritance even as it disintegrated elsewhere. Yet Tang garrisons were withdrawn by 880
amid the dynasty's collapse, and local families governed the Red River plain thereafter — the immediate
prelude to durable Vietnamese autonomy under [[ngo-quyen|Ngo Quyen]] (see
[[battle-of-bach-dang-938]] and [[sinicization-of-vietnam]]).

## Actors

The [[tang-dynasty|Tang dynasty]] and its general Gao Pian on one side; Nan Zhao and the allied Do family
on the other. See [[sinicization-of-vietnam]] for the wider provincial era.

## Historiography

Taylor reads the war against the nationalist temptation to see all anti-Tang activity as proto-national
resistance: the conflict was as much a contest among Vietnamese-territory factions (Red River vs. southern
plains) as between "Vietnamese" and "Chinese." Gao Pian's later apotheosis as a benevolent culture-hero —
invoked by an eleventh-century Vietnamese ruler for legitimacy — illustrates how thoroughly the provincial
past was woven into later Vietnamese self-understanding rather than simply resented.
