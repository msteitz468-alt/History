# Mexican-American War (1846–1848)

The war between the United States and Mexico that resulted in the U.S. acquisition of roughly half of Mexico's territory (the Mexican Cession) via the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. In the interpretive frame of Blaakman, Conroy-Krutz, and Arista (eds.), *The Early Imperial Republic* (2023), it represents the culmination of early U.S. imperial expansion, inseparably linked to slavery's empire and the ideological ambivalences of the "empire of liberty."

## Narrative (high-level, source-grounded)

The conflict followed Texas independence and U.S. annexation. Border incidents (Thornton Affair) provided the casus belli for President Polk. U.S. forces under Taylor, Scott, and others invaded and occupied key Mexican territory. Naval operations secured California. The war ended with Mexico's capitulation and massive territorial transfer.

## Analysis per the Source

Amy S. Greenberg (Ch. 13) examines "mercenary ambivalence" and military violence in antebellum America's wars of empire, including this conflict. The volume situates the war within the longer arc of using imperial practices for continental (and projected global) growth, with slavery expansion as a major driver (see also Heerman on slave trade/Texas borderlands). Peace reformers (Minardi) debated whether "benevolent" or non-violent means could better serve imperial ends. The outcome dramatically enlarged the "slave empire" question that would explode in the 1850s.

**Causal note:** While immediate triggers were diplomatic/military, the source emphasizes structural drivers from the early imperial republic: land hunger on cleared Native territories, the need for new slave states, and the ideological comfort with empire when framed as liberty or civilization.

## From Gwynne, *Empire of the Summer Moon* (2010) — Comanche consequence

Gwynne ranks Guadalupe Hidalgo (2 Feb 1848) with Appomattox as nation-defining: ~1.2 million sq mi added (~66% increase in U.S. landmass). For the [[actors/comanches|Comanches]], the geopolitical change was decisive — from **buffer between two empires** (Spain/Mexico vs. the U.S./Texas) to **obstacle inside one federal polity** with armies, taxes, and a single Indian policy. Texas Rangers served in Mexico as irregular scouts transferring Comanche-learned mounted tactics; the army’s wartime demand for revolvers produced the **Walker Colt** (Samuel Walker + Samuel Colt redesign, 1847), a technology genealogy Gwynne ties from Ranger–Comanche combat on the Texas frontier into the Mexican War and later plains campaigns.

## Historiography

Standard U.S. accounts emphasize Manifest Destiny, Polk’s war aims, and the slavery-expansion crisis the Cession ignited. Mexican historiography stresses aggression and national trauma. Blaakman et al. (2023) reframe the war within early U.S. imperial practice rather than as a mid-century anomaly. Gwynne (2010) is not a Mexican War monograph but underlines a borderlands consequence often thin in general narratives: the war’s end enclosed the Comanche imperial range inside a single continental power, converting a multi-imperial buffer zone into a U.S. “Indian problem.”
