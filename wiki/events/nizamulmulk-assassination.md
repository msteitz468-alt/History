# Assassination of Nizam al-Mulk (1092)

Nizam al-Mulk, the powerful and long-serving Persian vizier of the Seljuk Sultans Alp Arslan and Malik-Shah, was murdered in October 1092 near Nahavand, reportedly by a member of the Nizari Ismaili movement disguised as a Sufi mystic seeking an audience — one of the earliest and most consequential killings attributed to the group later known in Western sources as the "Assassins," under the emerging leadership of Hassan-i Sabbah from the fortress of Alamut.

Nizam al-Mulk had governed the day-to-day administration of the Seljuk state for roughly three decades, founding the influential Nizamiyyah madrasas (including the one in Baghdad where al-Ghazali later taught) and authoring the *Siyasatnama* ("Book of Government"), a major work of Perso-Islamic political theory. His death, followed within weeks by that of Sultan Malik-Shah, removed the two figures most responsible for holding together the sprawling Seljuk realm, triggering a succession crisis among Malik-Shah's sons and regional governors that permanently fractured centralized Seljuk authority into competing successor sultanates.

## Causal Analysis

The assassination was **enabled by** the Nizari Ismaili movement's emerging strategy of targeted political killing against Sunni Seljuk authority figures, and occurred amid **contributing** court tensions between Nizam al-Mulk and Terken Khatun over succession arrangements favoring her son.

## Consequence Analysis

The killing **produced** an immediate leadership vacuum in Seljuk administration and, combined with Malik-Shah's death shortly after, **enabled** the fragmentation of the Seljuk Empire into rival successor states, while also **contributing to** the Nizari Ismailis' reputation and subsequent use of targeted assassination as a political tool across the following two centuries.

## Historiography

Known through Persian and Arabic chronicle traditions (including Ibn al-Athir and later Ismaili and anti-Ismaili sources), whose accounts of the precise circumstances and motive vary. Modern scholarship treats the assassination as a genuine turning point in Seljuk political history, though some accounts attributing the killing partly to court intrigue (rather than purely Ismaili initiative) remain debated.
