# The Albigensian Crusade

## Narrative

The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) was a war of conquest in **Languedoc**,
proclaimed by [[innocent-iii|Pope Innocent III]] and waged by northern French
barons against the lords of the south who tolerated the dualist heresy of the
[[catharism|Cathars]] (named "Albigensian" after Albi, an early Cathar centre).
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Bernard Hamilton, Ch. 2b) stresses that it was
**not designed to deal directly with heresy** but to make an example of Toulouse by
replacing the *fautores* — those who fostered heresy — with Catholic lords who would
cooperate with the Church.

The trigger was the assassination of the legate **Peter of Castelnau** in January
1208, blamed (probably wrongly) on **Raymond VI of Toulouse**. Innocent launched the
crusade, offering the same indulgence as a Holy Land crusade but requiring only **forty
days' service** — an attraction that, with the short distance from the Île-de-France,
guaranteed recruitment. The crusade's brutality was established at once: when **Béziers**
fell in July 1209 the entire population, Cathar and Catholic alike, was massacred;
**Carcassonne** fell in August. The legate Arnald-Amalric appointed **Simon de
Montfort**, a baron of the Île-de-France, as ruler of the conquered Trencavel lands.

The war turned on the intervention of **Peter II of Aragon** (the victor of
[[battle-of-las-navas-de-tolosa-1212|Las Navas de Tolosa]], 1212), who took Toulouse
under his protection; he was **killed at the battle of Muret** (12 September 1213),
where Montfort's heavily outnumbered cavalry won a decisive victory. The **Fourth
Lateran Council** (1215) awarded Montfort the lands of Toulouse. But southern revolts
followed; Montfort was **killed besieging Toulouse** in June 1218, and his son Amaury
lost the conquests, so that Languedoc reverted to Raymond VII. The decisive turn was the
intervention of the **Capetian crown**: Louis VIII crusaded in 1226, and after his death
the regent **Blanche of Castile** brought the war to a close at the **Peace of
Paris-Meaux (1229)**, by which Raymond VII was reconciled, agreed to enforce the heresy
laws, found a university at Toulouse, and marry his heiress daughter Jeanne to a brother
of the king — ensuring that Toulouse would ultimately **revert to the French crown**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[catharism|the entrenchment of Catharism]] in the fragmented,
  partible-inheritance society of Languedoc, where great lords (the Saint-Gilles counts
  of Toulouse, the Trencavels) tolerated *perfecti* at their courts.
- `caused_by:` the assassination of Peter of Castelnau (1208) and Raymond VI's refusal
  to suppress heresy.
- `contributed_to:` the [[papal-monarchy|developed papal claim]] (codified at Lateran
  IV, c. *Excommunicamus*) that a ruler who persistently failed to act against heretics
  could be **deposed** and his lands offered to orthodox princes.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Capetian annexation of the Midi** — the integration of Languedoc
  into the kingdom of France, one of the largest territorial gains of the
  [[capetian-dynasty|Capetian monarchy]].
- `produced:` the **[[medieval-inquisition|papal Inquisition]]**, which Gregory IX
  delegated to the [[dominican-order|Dominican]] and Franciscan friars from c. 1233 when
  it became clear that crusade and episcopal inquisition had **failed to destroy
  Catharism** (the *perfecti* had simply gone underground).
- `produced:` over the following century, the **extinction of Catharism**: the fall of
  the Cathar stronghold of **Montségur** (1244, ~200 *perfecti* burned), the
  suppression of the late revival led by Pierre Autier (executed 1310), and the burning
  of the last *perfectus* Guillem Bélibaste (1321) under the inquisitor Bernard Gui.
- `enabled:` the precedent of the **"political crusade"** against Christian lay powers,
  later turned against the [[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Hohenstaufen]].

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan names the dualists **Cathars** (from Greek *katharos*, “pure”) and **Albigensians** (from Albi). Murder of the legate **Peter of Castelnau** (January 1208), blamed on Raymond VI of Toulouse’s circle, triggered the crusade Innocent had been preparing through Cistercian legates and the barefoot preaching of Diego of Osma and [[dominic-de-guzman|Dominic]]. Logan stresses **Philip Augustus** refused to lead (preoccupied with John of England and Otto IV). Later royal armies under Prince/King **Louis** transformed crusade pretence into French expansion into the south: “The crusade now was but a pretence; the reality was a war being waged by the king of France in the Languedoc for the purpose of extending royal power into the south. … It had been a successful war for the French, but clearly not a crusade.” Peace with Raymond VII and young Louis IX at Notre-Dame Paris left ancestral territories to pass to the crown. Dominic continued preaching during the turbulence; Logan finds no evidence of Dominic’s military involvement though he was friendly with Simon de Montfort’s family. Full dualist doctrine, northern vs southern repression before 1198, and inquisitorial aftermath: see [[catharism]].

## Historiography

Hamilton (NCMH V) corrects two popular distortions. First, the **Inquisition's
reputation** for the thirteenth century is "not entirely deserved": the inquisitors
(unlike the crusade leaders) rarely conducted mass executions — Bernard of Caux
sentenced over 600 offenders in 1245–46 with none burned and only 23 imprisoned, the
rest given crosses or pilgrimages — and recorded uses of torture (licensed by Innocent
IV's *Ad extirpanda*, 1252) are very few. Second, the cult of **Montségur** as a Cathar
sacred site is a modern myth: the dualists, holding all matter evil, attached no
sanctity to any place. Hamilton also registers the scholarly dispute over **Bosnian
dualism** (Fine's reductionist view vs. Šanjek's maximalist one) and the puzzle of why
Catharism died out across western Europe so suddenly around 1320.
