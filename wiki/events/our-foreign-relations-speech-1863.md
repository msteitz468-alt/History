# "Our Foreign Relations" speech (Charles Sumner, 10 September 1863)

Charles Sumner's marathon 36,000-word oration at New York's Cooper Union Institute, the high-water mark of the transatlantic campaign to brand the Confederacy a pariah power and to shame Britain and France into ending their complicity.

## Narrative

Before three thousand listeners, Sumner excoriated the Palmerston ministry for granting belligerent rights and allowing the construction of Confederate commerce raiders. He warned that a recognized slaveholding Confederacy would dispatch "Ambassadors of Slavery" to every court and force civilized nations into moral compromise. He placed the high seas at the center: the raiders had turned the ocean into "a furnace and a melting pot of American commerce" and made Confederates "disturbers of the common highway, outlaws, and enemies of the human race."

The speech was printed as pamphlets, excerpted in newspapers, and circulated on both sides of the Atlantic.

## Significance (Bonner)

It represented the anti-Confederate case at its most comprehensive and emotionally powerful. While it did not produce a new formal rule of international law, it crystallized the moral and political obstacles to recognition that proved decisive.

## Historiography

The speech is treated in Bonner as the rhetorical summit of the anti-Confederate pariah campaign. It has received less attention in purely military histories of the war. Primary text widely reprinted in 1863 pamphlets and in Sumner's *Works*.
