# Death of Tokugawa Ieyasu (1616)

Tokugawa Ieyasu, founder of the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] and de facto ruler of
[[japan|Japan]] since the battle of Sekigahara (1600), died at Shizuoka (Sumpu) on the seventeenth
day of the fourth month of Genna 2 (April 17, 1616), aged seventy-five. His death, long rumored
abroad — the English merchant Richard Cocks noted in January 1616 "newes was brought to town that
the Emperour is dead; but I beleeve rather is a fable" — ended the career of the man who had
consolidated Japan's long [[edo-period|Edo peace]].

## Narrative

Ieyasu had been in declining health since early 1616, when he fell ill at Shizuoka after eating sea
bream cooked in sesame oil — a dish resembling what later became tempura, recommended by the
merchant Chaya Shirojiro. Though he rallied briefly with the aid of pills he compounded himself
(including his prized "Manbyo-en," or Pill for a Myriad Ills), he relapsed, and his illness is
generally attributed to cancer of the stomach. Throughout this final illness he continued to
conduct affairs of state: he reviewed the daimyo situation with his son and successor Hidetada,
summoned the Three Collateral Lords (Yoshinao, Yorinobu, Yorifusa), and instructed Todo Takatora
and Ii Kamon to have forces under arms to suppress any potential rebellion at the moment of his
death.

On the occasion of his last illness the Emperor conferred on him the title of Dajodaijin (Great
Chancellor of the Empire), the highest court rank. Ieyasu received the imperial commission dressed
in court robes, attended a Noh performance, and distributed lavish gifts — "Lower First Court Rank
and Dajodaijin, he had nothing more to attain in this world, though the latter honor was little but
a farewell present for the world to come." He composed two death verses:

> *Whether one passes on or remains behind it is all the same. / That you can take no one with you
> is the only difference.*

> *Ah, how pleasant! Two awakenings and one sleep. / This dream of a fleeting world! The roseate
> hues of early dawn!*

Konchiin Suden recorded the moment simply: "On the seventeenth day of the fourth month at noon the
Great Chancellor of the Empire of the Lower First Court Rank, Minamoto Ieyasu, passed to another
world aged seventy-five." That evening his coffin was carried up the steep hill of Kunozan by the
sea in silent procession, Honda Masazumi walking beside it in straw sandals and addressing his
master as if he still lived.

## Causal Analysis

Ieyasu's death appears to have been caused by gastric cancer, consistent with the
symptoms described in the sources. His illness was contributed_to by his age (seventy-five) and
possibly by his lifelong willingness to try novel medicines — the sesame-oil bream triggered the
initial episode. His death was not politically precipitated; by 1616 the Tokugawa settlement was
already secure, Hidetada had been Shogun since 1605, and Ieyasu had spent his final years as retired
Shogun (Ogosho) consolidating institutions rather than fighting.

## Consequence Analysis

Ieyasu's instructions before death were precise: Hidetada should keep the daimyo in Edo three years
after his death to prevent disorder; Todo Takatora and Ii Kamon should stand between any parties to
prevent collision. The succession to Hidetada was seamless, vindicating Ieyasu's two decades of
institution-building. Within a year his remains were transferred to [[tosho-daigongen-deification-1617|Nikko]]
and he was deified as Tosho Daigongen, transforming his political legacy into a religious one that
reinforced Tokugawa authority for 260 years.

Notably, Ieyasu had forbidden the practice of *junshi* (retainer suicide following a lord's death),
and in consequence there was not a single case of junshi at the deaths of either Ieyasu or
Hidetada — a deliberate institutional break with feudal custom that he had enforced since 1607.

## Actors

- **[[tokugawa-ieyasu]]** — the dying Ogosho; remained politically active to the last
- **[[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Hidetada]]** — Shogun since 1605; received final instructions
- **Honda Masazumi** — attended the deathbed and coffin procession
- **Tenkai** — Tendai monk; managed the posthumous religious program
- **Konchiin Suden** — Zen monk and bakufu advisor; left the diary record of the death

## Historiography

Sadler draws extensively on the *Tokugawa Jikki* (official Tokugawa chronicle) and on the diary of
Konchiin Suden, cross-referenced with the English merchant Richard Cocks's diary (January–April
1616), which confirms that Ieyasu's death was anticipated and rumored in advance. Sadler notes the
"complete absence of nerves" and "well-known capacity for sleep at any time" that had preserved
Ieyasu's health through decades of warfare, making the final illness the more striking. The
source — [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]] — is a 1937 narrative biography relying on
Tokugawa-period chronicles; it should be read with awareness that official chronicles had dynastic
interests in the shaping of Ieyasu's final days.
