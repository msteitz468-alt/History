# Algerian War of Independence

## Narrative

France's postwar colonial policy was shaped by wartime promises that went unkept: in 1944, de Gaulle had outlined a framework for transforming the empire into a French Union modeled on the British Commonwealth, in part to rally colonial troops (some 300,000 North African Arabs served under his command). France reneged, and the Liberation period saw brutal suppression of pro-independence disturbances, including at Sétif, Algeria, in May 1945.

Unlike the professional-army war in Indochina (1946–1954), the Algerian war mobilized French draftees, spreading the professional army's frustrations with counterinsurgency directly into French civil society. As reports of systematic torture by the French army surfaced, French public opinion split bitterly between critics of a cruel, unwinnable war and a majority — spanning both Left and Right in the late 1950s — who believed France was defending civilization against "Communist-Arab fanaticism." The presence of nearly 1 million pieds-noirs, French settlers adamantly opposed to independence, complicated any negotiated settlement.

In May 1958, fearing the Fourth Republic was losing Algeria to the FLN through indecision, army officers and settlers seized government headquarters in Algiers, took Corsica, and threatened to send paratroops to Paris. General de Gaulle announced from retirement that he was ready to save the Republic; unable to compel the army or police to suppress the rebellion, the Fourth Republic gave way to de Gaulle's more authoritarian Fifth Republic — the one Cold War change of regime in Western Europe, and a nationalist-conservative one.

The pieds-noirs who had championed de Gaulle's return were disappointed: instead of committing to French Algeria, de Gaulle moved gradually toward self-determination, framing it as the "liberating genius of France" already emancipating its sub-Saharan colonies. A January 1961 referendum gave overwhelming support to Algerian self-determination. Opponents formed the underground Organisation de l'Armée Secrète (OAS) to continue the fight through terrorism. The war ended in 1962 after a ceasefire providing for "Algerian independence in cooperation with France," approved by over 90% in referenda in both countries. Violence scarred both sides: dozens of peaceful pro-independence demonstrators were beaten and drowned by Paris police in October 1961, while an estimated 10,000 pieds-noirs and 150,000 harkis (Algerians loyal to France) were killed by the FLN after independence; harki and pied-noir survivors who relocated to France became a lasting reservoir of support for the French Far Right.

## Causal Analysis

The war was `caused_by` France's refusal to honor its wartime autonomy promises and by the FLN's turn to armed insurgency after peaceful nationalist channels were blocked. The war's prolongation and brutalization were `contributed_to` by the pied-noir settler presence and by mutual radicalization (FLN terror, French army torture and reprisal). De Gaulle's return to power was `caused_by` the May 1958 Algiers coup attempt, not by any prior electoral process; his subsequent move toward Algerian self-determination was `contributed_to` by his assessment that French power in Algeria could only be maintained through methods that destroyed its own legitimacy.

## Consequence Analysis

The war `produced` Algerian independence and the collapse of the Fourth Republic; it `enabled` the creation of the more centralized, presidential Fifth Republic that De Gaulle used to pursue an independent French foreign and nuclear policy. It `contributed_to` a broader French reckoning — particularly on the Left — with the concept of France's "civilizing mission," paralleling the corrosive effect of America's later war in Vietnam on the American self-image. It `produced` lasting demographic and political consequences in metropolitan France via the harki and pied-noir resettlement.

## Actors

[[de-gaulle-charles|Charles de Gaulle]] — returned to power amid the crisis and ultimately negotiated Algerian independence despite pied-noir expectations. The Front de Libération Nationale (FLN) — the Algerian nationalist movement that fought and won the war.

## Historiography

Alistair Horne, *A Savage War of Peace* (reprint ed., 2002), is the standard account drawing on both sides' sources; see also John Talbott, *The War Without a Name* (1980), and Anthony Clayton, *The Wars of French Decolonization* (1995). Often compared to the American war in Vietnam, though contemporaries stressed Algeria's proximity to metropolitan France and its status, in French eyes, as a civil war rather than a colonial one.
