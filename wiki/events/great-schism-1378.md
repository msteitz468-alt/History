# The Great Schism (1378–1417)

The **Great (Western) Schism** was the forty-year division of the Latin Church between rival lines
of popes — at **Rome** and **Avignon**, and later a third at **Pisa** — that shattered the unity of
Western Christendom. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (Howard Kaminsky, Ch. 16; P.N.R. Zutshi, Ch.
15) treats it as the great crisis of the late-medieval Church, the outcome of the
[[papacy|Avignon papacy's]] fiscal monarchy and the catalyst of the conciliar movement.

## Narrative

After seventy years at **Avignon**, **Gregory XI** returned the papacy to **Rome (1377)** and died
the next year. In a conclave besieged by a Roman mob "shouting for 'A Roman! A Roman!... or else
we'll kill them all!'," the cardinals elected the Neapolitan **Urban VI (1378)** — but his harsh,
erratic conduct soon alienated them. Within months the (mostly French) cardinals declared the
election void as made under duress, and elected a rival, **Clement VII**, who withdrew to
**Avignon**. Christendom now had **two popes**, each with his own curia, cardinals, and revenues,
and each excommunicating the other.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **contested conclave of 1378** — held under genuine Roman intimidation, which
  gave the cardinals a plausible (if self-interested) ground to repudiate Urban VI.
- `contributed_to:` the **Franco-Italian rivalry** built into the Avignon papacy: a largely French
  College of Cardinals chafing under a return to Rome and an Italian pope, and the **secular
  powers** ready to back whichever pope served their interests.

## Consequence Analysis

The schism **divided Europe into "obediences"** along political lines: France, Castile, Aragon,
Scotland and Naples generally followed Avignon; England, the Empire, Hungary, Poland and northern
Italy followed Rome. Each line perpetuated itself by electing successors, so the schism could not
end by simple death. Its effects were profound:

- **The discrediting of the papal monarchy.** Two (then three) men claiming to be the one vicar of
  Christ made a mockery of the [[papacy|papal-monarchy]] ideology the Avignon popes had pushed "to
  its highest pitch," and intensified calls for **reform of the Church "in head and members."**
- **The rise of [[conciliarism|conciliarism]].** When neither pope would resign (the *via
  cessionis*), theologians and canonists revived the doctrine that a **general council** could judge
  and depose popes and stood above them. The **Council of Pisa (1409)** deposed both popes and
  elected a third — making matters worse (now **three** popes).
- **Resolution at Constance.** The **Council of Constance (1414–1418)**, summoned with the Emperor
  **Sigismund's** backing, finally ended the schism: it secured the resignation or deposition of all
  three lines and elected **Martin V (1417)**. In its decree *Haec sancta* it asserted conciliar
  superiority over the pope — though the post-schism papacy would soon reassert monarchical claims.

## Historiography

Kaminsky stresses that the schism grew from the **structural** character of the Avignon papacy — its
"rampant fiscalism" and French complexion — as much as from the accident of 1378. Its lasting legacy
was to legitimise the **conciliar** challenge to papal absolutism and to feed the demand for reform
that ran on through Wyclif, Hus, and ultimately the Reformation.

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

### Scale and dual apparatus

Logan frames the Western Great Schism as beginning in **1378** and lasting almost **four decades**—unique among papal rivalries for dividing Europe largely along **national lines** into two and eventually three allegiances, casting a long shadow over subsequent church history. Both Urban VI (Rome) and Clement VII (Avignon) claimed canonical election, maintained full papal apparatus, affirmed the primacy of Rome, and differed only over who was Peter’s true successor; each quickly excommunicated the other. Logan judges neither ideal: Urban’s “apparently demented behaviour”; Clement (as Robert of Geneva) was the “**butcher of Cesena**,” who ordered the slaughter of Cesena’s inhabitants (~**3,000**) about a year and a half before his election.

Two linked questions: which claimant was true pope, and—soon paramount—**how to resolve** the schism; both camps included Christians of “unquestioned rectitude.”

### Election of Urban VI (April 1378)

Gregory died 27 March 1378; 16 cardinals at Rome (4 Italian, 11 French, 1 Spanish) elected **Bartolomeo Prignano**, archbishop of Bari, as Urban VI under Roman pressure (“Romano lo volemo”); after delay and palace invasion, aged Cardinal Tebaldeschi was mock-vested while electors fled; Prignano accepted 9 April, crowned Easter 18 April; all 16 cardinals subsequently treated him as pope. After enthronement Urban condemned absentee benefice-holders, berated cardinals, nearly assaulted Limoges, refused return to Avignon. By August 13 cardinals at Anagni issued *Declaratio* (9 Aug 1378) nullifying election for fear (*vi et metu*); 20 Sept elected Robert of Geneva as Clement VII at Fondi. Logan concludes bizarre post-Easter behaviour—not mob fear alone—turned the cardinals (“If he had behaved prudently, he could have remained pope”). Possible mental breakdown: later imprisoned/murdered five of six cardinals seeking remedy (1384); Adam of Easton saved by Richard II.

Clement created new cardinals, failed to take Rome, reached Avignon Palais des Papes **20 June 1379**; Urban created 24 new cardinals (Sept 1378)—formal dual-obedience structure.

### Canon-law gap and political map

Logan argues the schism stemmed partly from canon law’s lack of procedure to remove even an insane pope. Charles V of France recognized Clement; without Charles’s support the schism might have been a footnote. Obediences: Roman—most Italy, England, Wenceslaus/Germany, Scandinavia, Poland, Hungary; Avignon—Scotland, Savoy, Burgundy, Portugal (later switched under English pressure), Castile 1380, Aragon 1387, Navarre 1390. Decisions political, not theological—“the world was at sixes and sevens.”

### Remedies: *via cessionis*, Pisa, triple papacy

A 1384 University of Paris poll yielded *via concilii generalis*, *via compromissi*, and *via cessionis*; recommended mutual resignation; *via cessionis* became French royal policy. After Clement’s death (Sept 1394), Avignon elected **Benedict XIII** (Peter de Luna), who refused resignation unless legitimacy affirmed. Simon de Cramaud steered a 1395 Paris assembly >4:1 for *via cessionis*; France withdrew support 1398 then rescinded 1403. After failed Savona meeting, cardinals of both camps at **Leghorn** (29 June 1408) summoned the **[[council-of-pisa-1409|Council of Pisa]]** (1409), which deposed both claimants and elected **Alexander V**—producing a **third obedience**. Urban’s death (15 Oct 1389) might have enabled reconciliation, but Roman cardinals elected Boniface IX eighteen days later without waiting.

Logan’s three-obedience table: **Rome**—Urban VI (1378–89), Boniface IX (1389–1404), Innocent VII (1404–06), Gregory XII (1406–15); **Avignon**—Clement VII (1378–94), Benedict XIII (1394–1423); **Pisa**—Alexander V (1409–10), John XXIII (1410–15). Charges against Baldassare Cossa / John XXIII (piracy, seducing 200 women at Bologna, poisoning Alexander V, bribing electors) are hard to separate from enemy libel—“there may be some truth to some… but to say more than that is to go beyond surviving evidence.”

Resolution at [[council-of-constance-1414|Constance]] and election of [[martin-v|Martin V]] (1417): “The schism was truly over.”
