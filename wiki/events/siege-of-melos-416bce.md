# The Siege of Melos and the Melian Dialogue, 416 BCE

## Narrative

In **416 BCE** Athens sent an expedition against the small Dorian island of **Melos**, a
colony of [[sparta]] that had tried to stay neutral in the [[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian
War]]. When the Melians refused to submit to the [[delian-league|empire]], the city was
**walled off, besieged, and starved** into surrender that winter; the Athenians **killed the
adult men and sold the women and children into slavery**. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]]
(Ch. 10, A. Andrewes) notes that **forcible incorporation in the League was nothing new**, and
that the massacre weighed little on Athenian consciences at the time (Aristophanes jokes about
"Melian hunger" the next spring); it became notorious chiefly because of Thucydides.

## The Melian Dialogue

What made Melos a permanent emblem is **Thucydides' Melian Dialogue** (v.85–113), the
negotiation cast not as a pair of antithetic speeches but, uniquely, as a **dramatic
dialogue**. In it the Athenian envoys demand that argument proceed only on what both sides
"really think," and assert that **justice has no place between states of very unequal
power** — "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians appeal
to fortune, the gods, and Spartan help; the Athenians dismiss each in turn as empty hope. The
Dialogue is the wiki's paradigm of the **"might makes right" logic of empire** that
Thucydides elsewhere lets Athenians call a **tyranny**.

## Significance and interpretation

Andrewes stresses how much of Melos' prominence "is due to the attention Thucydides' Dialogue
has focused on it," and that the historian's **purpose is contested**:

- Some read it as illustrating the **moral decline of Athens** under the stress of war — but
  Andrewes notes the Athenians spoke in similar terms as early as 432, so there is no simple
  linear descent.
- Alternatively, Thucydides' real subject may be the **theoretical question of what, if
  anything, should limit the exercise of imperial power** — "not a defence of Athens, but nor
  a vindication of Melos."

Thucydides placed the Dialogue **immediately before the [[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian
Expedition]]**, and readers have long seen the juxtaposition as deliberate — imperial
arrogance at Melos followed at once by imperial catastrophe at Syracuse — though the historian
gives no explicit signal. The episode entered the permanent indictment of the Athenian empire.

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: Athenian insistence that no island remain neutral, and the empire's renewed
assertiveness after the Peace of Nicias. produced: the **destruction of Melos** and a lasting
symbol of imperial ruthlessness; and, through Thucydides, the West's classic text on **power
versus justice** between states.

## Historiography

Melos is known essentially only through **Thucydides**, and the source itself is the
central historiographical problem: the historian states elsewhere in his work (i.22)
that speeches represent what he judged "called for" by the situation rather than a
verbatim record, and the **Melian Dialogue**'s unique dramatic form (rather than the
paired antithetical speeches used everywhere else in the work) marks it as an
exceptionally deliberate literary and philosophical construction rather than a
transcript of an actual negotiation. No Melian voice survives independently — the
adult male population was killed and the rest enslaved — so the "Melian" side of the
dialogue is entirely a Thucydidean invention, which sharpens rather than resolves the
long-standing debate (noted above, following Andrewes) over whether Thucydides intends
moral critique of Athens, dispassionate analysis of power politics, or something more
ambiguous. Because the episode's fame rests so heavily on this one authored text, its
historical significance and its literary/philosophical significance are difficult to
disentangle.
