# Battle of Britain (July–October 1940)

The first major air campaign in history and a pivotal defensive victory for the Allies.

## Narrative

See detailed tactical analysis at [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/second-world-war/battle-of-britain-1940]].

Luftwaffe attempted to destroy RAF Fighter Command to enable invasion. RAF, with radar and home advantage, inflicted unsustainable losses on German bombers and fighters.

## Causal Analysis

German overconfidence after France, switching targets from airfields to cities (London Blitz), British radar, pilot replacement, and leadership (Dowding, Park).

## Consequence Analysis

Hitler postponed Sea Lion indefinitely. First strategic defeat for Germany; boosted morale, kept Britain in the war, forced shift to Eastern Front.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

Evans deflates the "few" mythology: in mid-August 1940 Britain had 1,379 operationally ready fighter pilots to Germany's ~870, radar, radio intercepts, and double the German rate of aircraft production. Sea Lion's 2,000 river barges were unfit for anything but flat calm, and Raeder wanted to wait until May 1941. German intelligence told commanders half the British fighters were destroyed when the real figure was ~12% (791 German losses to 169 British in the deceived accounting's window), sustaining fatal overconfidence; Halder (7 October 1940): "leadership of our air force has underestimated the British fighters by about 100%." After the RAF's Berlin raid, Hitler's 4 September Sportpalast speech threatened to "erase their cities," and the Luftwaffe shifted to London — losing a quarter of its bombers on 15 September. Hitler conceded on 14 September that "the preconditions needed for Sealion are not yet there" and shelved the invasion. ~40,000 British civilians died in the Blitz (Coventry, November 1940: 380 dead, the medieval cathedral destroyed); ~600 German bombers were lost over the whole campaign; morale did not break. Evans's verdict: Hitler "had lost a major battle" for the first time. The pilot Ulrich Steinhilfer's letters track the squadron-level disillusion ("there are only twelve left from the old crew").
