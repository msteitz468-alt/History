# Australian Imperial Force on the Western Front (1916–1918)

## Narrative

The great bulk of the Australian Imperial Force (AIF) joined the British Expeditionary Force (BEF) on the Western Front from 1916. Four infantry divisions were dispatched to France in 1916, later joined by a fifth — the 3rd Division, formed in Australia and commanded by Major General [[monash-john]]. Together they constituted "about a twelfth of the BEF's fighting strength (though a much smaller proportion of its total number, because a much higher proportion of Australia's troops were front-line soldiers)."

**1916 — Somme and Fromelles.** As a diversionary operation, the 5th Australian Division attacked German trenches at Aubers Ridge near Fromelles on 19 July 1916. "Preparations for the ill-conceived attack failed to fool the Germans, and the attackers suffered heavy losses, virtually crippling the division for the rest of the year." On the Somme proper, three Australian divisions were "successively committed to attacks around Pozières and nearby Mouquet Farm against determined German resistance based on massive artillery fire. Over 23,000 Australians were killed or wounded in seven weeks of attacks." Charles Bean described Pozières Ridge as "more densely sown with Australian sacrifice than any other place on earth."

**1917 — Bullecourt and Ypres.** In spring 1917 ill-considered experiments with new tanks at Bullecourt resulted in "a bloody repulse, the loss of 1,900 men as prisoners." A second Bullecourt battle in May brought Australian losses to 10,000 casualties without gain. In autumn 1917 all five Australian divisions were committed to the Ypres offensive; "unseasonal heavy rain" bogged down later attacks. Nearly 22,000 Australians died in 1917, making it "the year of heaviest losses of the war."

A successful attack at Messines in June 1917, involving Monash's 3rd Division, showed that advances "more economical in casualties were possible."

**1918 — The Hundred Days.** Late in 1917 the five AIF infantry divisions were united as the Australian Corps. After Birdwood, command passed early in 1918 to Monash. Under the German spring offensives Australian troops "were rushed from Belgium to the Somme to help hold the line." At Amiens in August 1918 the Australian Corps attacked alongside Canadian, British, and French formations. The Corps "played a leading part in advancing up the Somme valley," culminating in the capture of Mont St Quentin and seizure of parts of the Hindenburg Line. These advances, along with those of other Allied forces, produced the German defeat in November 1918.

**Discipline and Command.** "Australian troops soon demonstrated differences from British and other Dominion forces. Though notably competent, they also displayed poorer discipline: Australian rates of absence, crime generally and imprisonment outstripped their counterparts, until by 1918 Australian rates of absence were several times greater than comparable forces." Despite urging by senior officers, Prime Minister Hughes "refused to allow the 120 Australians sentenced to death for a variety of military offences, including desertion, to be executed."

The "Anzac illusion" — "the idea that Australia made a disproportionately strong contribution, especially on the Western Front" — is identified by historian Eric Andrews as a myth. The AIF was not the only all-volunteer force and did not suffer heavier absolute casualties than other forces, though its casualty rate was high because it was composed overwhelmingly of front-line soldiers.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: Approximately 60,000 Australian dead and 150,000 wounded across the whole war
- contributed_to: Erosion of trust in British high command at Fromelles, Pozières, Bullecourt — "the defeat undermined Australian troops' confidence in British command"
- enabled: Monash's rise to Australian Corps command
- contributed_to: Allied victory in the Hundred Days (August–November 1918)
- contributed_to: Post-war demobilisation challenge and repatriation welfare state — see [[anzac-legend]]

## Historiography

E.M. Andrews' *The Anzac Illusion* (1993) is the key critical work on the inflated claims for Australian performance, arguing that Australia made "a disproportionately strong contribution" claim was misleading. The source is careful to note the real disproportion: the AIF was front-line heavy, explaining higher casualty rates without requiring claims of unique bravery. Bill Gammage's *The Broken Years* (1974) — ordinary soldiers' diaries and letters — provides the ground-level picture. The high indiscipline rates (absence, crime) complicate simple heroic narratives. The choice not to execute soldiers despite official pressure is treated as a distinctive feature of the AIF's relationship with Australian democratic norms.
