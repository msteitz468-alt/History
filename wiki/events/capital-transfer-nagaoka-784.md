# Transfer of the Capital to Nagaoka (784–794)

The transfer of the Japanese imperial court from Nara ([[nara-period|Heijō-kyō]])
to Nagaoka in 784 was the first of two capital moves undertaken by
[[kanmu-emperor|Emperor Kammu]] and constituted an incomplete step toward the
eventual founding of [[heian-kyo|Heian-kyō]] in 794. The ten years at Nagaoka were
marked by political violence, supernatural crisis, and ultimately abandonment, making
this one of the most politically fraught episodes in early Japanese history.
[[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]]

## Decision and Site Selection

In early summer 784, Kammu directed that a site be surveyed at Nagaoka, an area
near the Katsura River about twenty miles northwest of Nara and "approximately a half
mile northwest of the point where the Meishin Highway now first crosses the Tōkaidō
Shinkansen south of Kyoto." By the end of the year he had moved into his new palace
there, moving with notable haste.

The motivations were multiple:
- **Escape from Nara's political landscape.** Nara was "the handiwork of the old imperial
  line and the stronghold of both that line's adherents and the Buddhist forces that seem
  to have very nearly succeeded in usurping imperial authority in the time of Konin's
  immediate predecessor, Empress Shōtoku (718–770; r. 749–758, 764–770)."
- **Kinship geography.** Nagaoka was "associated politically and economically with Kammu's
  family line ever since the time of his great-grandfather Emperor Tenji" and was the home
  district of his matriline (the Korean-descended Yamato and Haji lineages).
- **Communication advantages.** The new site was "more conveniently located for land and
  water communication than the capital at Nara."
- **Cost as political weapon.** The enormous personal cost the move imposed on the nobility
  increased their dependence on the imperial government.

The move's chief advocate was **Fujiwara no Tanetsugu** (737–785), Kammu's chief adviser,
whose own matrilineal family (the Korean-descended Hata) was also based in the area. Tanetsugu
owed his influence to his uncle Fujiwara no Momokawa, who had been instrumental in securing
both Kōnin's succession and Kammu's appointment as crown prince.

## Crisis: The Tanetsugu Assassination and Sawara's Death (785)

On a night in autumn 785, while Kammu was temporarily absent from the city, Tanetsugu was
murdered at Nagaoka. The chapter's sources point to multiple possible instigators:
- Entrenched interests at Nara opposed to the move
- Clans (Ōtomo, Saeki) excluded from power after the ascendancy of the Momokawa Fujiwara line
- The heir apparent, Prince Sawara — Kammu's younger full brother — who may have clashed
  with Tanetsugu over the succession and feared his own position was threatened

Sawara was implicated, deposed, and condemned to exile. He died before reaching it — "the
chronicle alleges, by starving himself to death." The *Cambridge History* notes that this
outcome served Kammu's interests so well "that one might suspect the emperor himself of
having manipulated the affair from the outset. However, no evidence supports such a view."

The deaths were followed almost immediately by a cascade of disasters: famine, devastating
floods, epidemic disease, and a series of illnesses and deaths in Kammu's family. Court
diviners interpreted these as the revenge of Sawara's angry spirit (*goryō*). Fear of that
spirit's future actions became a significant factor in the decision to seek a new capital site.

## Abandonment

After ten years at Nagaoka — described as having a "cramped and flood-prone site near
a large marsh" — Kammu accepted the recommendation of Wake no Kiyomaro (733–799),
reportedly "dismayed by the unfinished state of Nagaoka after ten years of effort and
untold expenditures," to seek a new location in the Kadono area northeast of Nagaoka.
The site in what is now the city of Kyoto was chosen in early 793, and Kammu moved
into the new palace at [[heian-kyo|Heian-kyō]] in the tenth month of 794.

## Significance

The move to Nagaoka and its failure is significant for several reasons:
1. It reveals the mixture of political calculation, kinship geography, and supernatural
   belief that shaped imperial decision-making in the early Heian court.
2. The spirit belief (Sawara's *goryō*) was itself a political and cultural force — the
   court's response to perceived supernatural vengeance had practical political consequences.
3. The abandonment demonstrated the limits of capital-building as a political strategy:
   even Kammu could not maintain a settlement he had created when inauspicious omen
   and physical unsuitability combined against it.

The move was part of "a temporally long peregrination that had taken Japanese rulers and
their courts from one site to another ever since the inception of the statutory (*ritsuryō*)
system of government in the seventh century."

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by**: Kammu's desire to break from the Buddhist and noble-clan power concentrated
at Nara; kinship and logistical attractions of the Nagaoka site; Tanetsugu's advocacy.

**produced**: Political crisis (Tanetsugu assassination, Sawara affair) that discredited
the site and accelerated the shift to the final Heian-kyō location.

## Historiography

The principal source is the *Shoku Nihongi*, the official court-compiled chronicle covering this period, which records the move, the Tanetsugu assassination, and Prince Sawara's death within the conventions of court historiography — favorable to the ruling emperor and reticent about implicating Kammu directly in Sawara's fate, as the *Cambridge History of Japan* itself notes ("no evidence supports" the suspicion that Kammu orchestrated the affair, though the chronicle's silence on the point is itself suggestive). Later Heian-period sources and religious records preserve the *goryō* (vengeful spirit) tradition around Sawara, reflecting how contemporaries and near-contemporaries interpreted the disasters at Nagaoka religiously rather than merely politically. Archaeological excavation of the Nagaoka palace site since the late twentieth century has substantially expanded knowledge of the capital's actual physical scale and state of completion, supplementing and in places correcting the textual record's impression of a hastily abandoned, unfinished project. The balance between practical (logistical, factional) and religious (spirit-appeasement) motives for the eventual move to Heian-kyō remains a matter of interpretive emphasis among historians rather than a settled dispute over facts.
