# Opening of the First Imperial Diet (1890)

The first session of the Imperial Diet (*Teikoku Gikai*) opened on 29 November 1890, marking
the beginning of constitutional parliamentary government in Japan. The ceremony was held at the
Diet building in Tokyo and presided over by [[emperor-meiji]] in a formal ceremony. Linked to
[[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]].

## Narrative

The emperor left the palace at 10:30 in the morning and proceeded to the House of Peers,
accompanied by Prince Taruhito, Interior Minister Sanjō Sanetomi, Prime Minister
[[yamagata-aritomo]], President of the Privy Council Ōki Takatō, and other high officials.
At the Diet building, the emperor was met by dignitaries, foreign legation ministers, and all
members of both Houses already assembled. In the Hall of Ceremonies, the emperor seated himself
on the throne. Prime Minister Yamagata came forward to offer the text of an imperial rescript,
and the emperor read it aloud.

The rescript expressed the emperor's satisfaction with progress toward establishing constitutional
institutions during the roughly twenty years since his accession. It voiced hope that this
progress would make known abroad the glory of Japan and the loyalty and bravery of its people,
and expressed confidence that friendly relations with foreign countries and trade would deepen.
[[ito-hirobumi]], as president of the House of Peers, came forward to receive the document from
the emperor. All present bowed deeply, acknowledged by the emperor with a nod. Then he withdrew.

The tone of the opening rescript was notably more international than the [[imperial-rescript-on-education-1890]]
issued a month earlier — though it too attributed Japan's progress to the Imperial Ancestors.

## Context

The opening followed months of last-minute institutional preparation. On June 28, the
administrative code had been approved; on June 30, the spheres of the Privy Council and the
Cabinet were defined. The [[first-general-election-japan-1890]] had been held on July 1.
On July 25 a new law simplified procedures for forming political parties and holding political
meetings, though simultaneously prohibiting women and children from attending political meetings
or joining parties, and banning outdoor gatherings within seven miles of the Diet buildings
during sessions.

The Diet building itself was new. A proposal for a government agency for Shintō worship had been
debated in the months before the opening — Sasaki Takayuki argued that such an agency was needed
to ensure Japan's national polity — but no decision was reached. The debate illustrated the
ongoing tension between the oligarchs who wanted clean separation of religion and politics (to
avoid alarming foreign powers and provoking domestic religious conflict) and conservative voices
who wanted Shintō formalized as the state religion.

## Significance

Keene's account describes the opening as "the realization of the dreams of many men, notably
Ōkuma Shigenobu," and notes that political novelists of the 1880s had written fiction in
anticipation of the day when Japan "like the advanced countries of the West, would be ruled by a
parliament that guaranteed the liberty of all Japanese and promised them a better life." Many
of those who had held such hopes would soon be disillusioned by the political squabbles of the
Diet, but the first session nonetheless represented, in Keene's words, "an immense step toward
achieving democracy, even though the path ahead was in shadows."

The first Diet session was also notable for the exercise of [[ito-hirobumi]]'s institutional
role: he had finally agreed (after many refusals) to serve as president of the House of Peers —
the upper house stocked with the new Meiji peerage — provided he could resign after the first
session. His presence helped anchor the constitutional machinery to the oligarchic governing
class that had designed it.

## Historiography

The opening of the Diet is celebrated as a milestone, but historians note the severe structural
constraints on parliamentary power built into the Meiji constitutional design: the cabinet was
responsible to the emperor, not the Diet; the budget if rejected defaulted to the previous year's
appropriation; the Privy Council and House of Peers provided upper-house checks on the elected
lower house. The opposition won the first election but could not govern. These structural
constraints shaped the entire period of Meiji and Taishō constitutional politics through 1925.
