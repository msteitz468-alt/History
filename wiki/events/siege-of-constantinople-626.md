# Siege of Constantinople (626)

The **Siege of Constantinople in 626** was a pivotal military engagement that represented the climax of the final Sasanian–Roman War (602–628). A combined army of the Avar Khaganate (with their Slavic and Gepid subjects) and the Sasanian Persian Empire besieged the Byzantine capital. The successful defense of the city prevented the collapse of the Eastern Roman Empire and marked a turning point in the war, leading directly to Heraclius's counter-offensive, the collapse of the Sasanian regime, and the disintegration of the Avar hegemony in Central and Eastern Europe.

## Narrative

By 626, the Sasanian Empire under Chosroes II had conquered much of the Byzantine Near East, including Syria, Palestine, and Egypt. While Emperor [[heraclius|Heraclius]] was campaigning with a mobile army in the Caucasus and Anatolia, the Persian general Shahrbaraz marched to Chalcedon on the Asiatic shore of the Bosporus. Simultaneously, the Avar Khagan led a massive host of approximately 80,000 warriors, including Slavic, Gepid, and Bulgar contingents, to the western land walls of Constantinople.

The city was defended by a garrison of roughly 12,000 troops (mostly cavalry), led by the regent, Patriarch Sergius I, and the patrician Bonus. The Persians on the Asiatic shore were unable to cross the Bosporus to reinforce the Avars due to the absolute naval dominance of the Byzantine navy's galleys (*monoremes* and *dromons*).

The siege began in late July 626. On August 7, the Avars launched a massive assault on the land walls using siege engines, while their Slavic allies attempted to launch *monoxyla* (dugout canoes) to ferry Persian troops across the Bosporus and launch a maritime assault on the Golden Horn. The Byzantine fleet intercepted and destroyed the Slavic flotilla, slaughtering the forces on board. Deprived of Persian support and facing heavy casualties on the land walls, the Avar Khagan lifted the siege and withdrew, burning his siege engines. Shahrbaraz and the Sasanian forces at Chalcedon subsequently retreated.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[successors-of-justinian|Sasanian-Roman War of 602–628]]`: The war reached its peak when the Persians sought to strike a lethal blow at the Byzantine capital.
- `caused_by: [[successors-of-justinian|Avar-Sasanian Alliance]]`: The siege was a coordinated, two-pronged attack meant to split Byzantine defensive forces.
- `contributed_to: [[heraclius|Heraclius's Strategy]]`: By leaving the capital's defense to the walls, the navy, and the regency, Heraclius was free to maintain his offensive in the Sasanian homeland, which ultimately broke Sasanian power.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[heraclius|Byzantine Survival and Counter-offensive]]`: The victory secured the imperial center, allowing Heraclius to launch the campaign that culminated at the [[battle-of-nineveh-627|Battle of Nineveh]].
- `contributed_to: [[empire-of-samo|Avar Decline]]`: The disaster at the walls decimated the Avar military elite and shattered the Khagan's prestige. This sparked immediate rebellions among their Slavic subjects, leading to the rise of [[empire-of-samo|Samo's Empire]] and the formation of independent Slavic tribal confederations (*Sclavinias*) in the Balkans.
- `produced: [[religious-perceptions-in-byzantium|Cult of the Theotokos]]`: The defense was attributed to divine intervention by the Virgin Mary (the Theotokos). The famous Akathist Hymn was sung in her honor, reinforcing the religious ideology of the Byzantine state.

## Historiography

The primary sources for the siege are contemporary Byzantine texts, including the *Chronicon Paschale*, the poems of George of Pisidia, and the later history of Theophanes the Confessor. Modern historians (such as Andrew Louth) note that the siege represents the first major test of Constantinople's massive Theodosian Walls against a coordinated siege by two major empires, demonstrating that the city's naval control was its ultimate security.
