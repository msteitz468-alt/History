# Prague Coup

## Narrative

Czechoslovakia was, at the war's end, the most "westernized" of the Eastern European states subject to Soviet influence and the one where the National Front coalition strategy went furthest: President Edvard Beneš had resumed his prewar post with a wartime understanding from Stalin, and the Communist Party (KSČ) won a genuine plurality of 38% in free elections in May 1946 — the only such Communist electoral success in the region. Communist secretary Klement Gottwald aimed for an outright majority (a target of 51%) at the next elections.

At the Cominform's founding meeting at Szklarska Poręba (September 1947), the KSČ was criticized for softness, and it responded with proposals — a "millionaire's tax," further nationalizations — designed to demonstrate revolutionary toughness to Moscow. Against a backdrop of severe drought and growing public dissatisfaction, tensions between the KSČ and its coalition partners mounted. A further round of Communist purges of the security police in early 1948 led twelve non-Communist ministers to resign as a body on February 20, 1948, apparently expecting the Social Democrats to follow suit and force new elections that would reduce the Communist vote share. Instead, the Social Democrats fell in line with the KSČ, while Communist-organized "action committees" and people's militias stormed public buildings, occupied factories, railroad stations, and media outlets, and staged mass demonstrations, creating a revolutionary atmosphere in the streets of Prague.

With a majority of ministers still in place, President Beneš was constitutionally obliged to allow Gottwald to fill the vacated posts with Communists, which he did on February 25, 1948. On March 10, Foreign Minister Jan Masaryk was found dead in the courtyard of the Foreign Ministry — officially ruled a suicide, though some evidence suggests he was pushed. When Beneš himself died in September 1948, no further barriers to a one-party Communist regime remained.

## Causal Analysis

The coup was `caused_by` the collision between the KSČ's Cominform-driven need to demonstrate revolutionary credentials and the coalition partners' attempt to force new elections; it was `contributed_to` by Communist control of the security police (built up steadily since 1945) and by the drought-driven economic discontent of early 1948. It is `part_of` the broader [[stalinization-of-eastern-europe|Stalinization of Eastern Europe]] and `concurrent_with` the parallel forced Social Democratic mergers occurring elsewhere in the bloc at the same time.

## Consequence Analysis

The coup `produced` a one-party Communist dictatorship that lasted until 1989, the deaths of Masaryk and (indirectly) Beneš, and Czechoslovakia's full alignment with Moscow. It `enabled` the subsequent purges of "nationalist deviationists" within the KSČ itself, culminating in the 1952 show trial and execution of Rudolf Slánský. Internationally, the coup — following so soon after the war — shocked Western opinion and `contributed_to` the acceleration of Western European defense cooperation, feeding directly into the Treaty of Brussels (March 17, 1948) and ultimately NATO (April 1949).

## Actors

Klement Gottwald — KSČ secretary who directed the coup and became president. Edvard Beneš — constitutionally obligated to accept the new government; died months later. Jan Masaryk — Foreign Minister whose death became a symbol of the coup's brutality.

## Historiography

The most dramatic single episode in the Stalinization of Eastern Europe; treated in Igor Lukeš's essay "The Czech Road to Communism" (in Naimark and Gibianskii, eds., *The Establishment of Communist Regimes in Eastern Europe, 1944–1949*, 1997) and Mark Pittaway, *Eastern Europe 1939–2000* (2004). The circumstances of Masaryk's death remain disputed. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]], Chapter 16.
