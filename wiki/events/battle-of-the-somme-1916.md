# The Somme — First Day (1 July 1916)

## Narrative

The opening day of the British Somme offensive and the bloodiest single day in British military
history. After a seven-day bombardment of some 1.5 million shells, ~60,000 men of the British Fourth
Army — 143 battalions, most of them Kitchener volunteers — advanced at **walking pace, in waves,
under ~60 lbs of kit** across no-man's-land on a fifteen-mile front. The bombardment had failed: as
[[keegan-face-of-battle-1976|John Keegan]] shows in *The Face of Battle*, only ~900 of ~21,000 tons
of shell was high explosive, the wire was often **uncut**, and the German machine-gunners survived in
deep dug-outs to **win "the race for the parapet."** The walking waves were cut down in the open —
~**21,000 killed, ~60,000 casualties**, "most in the first hour… perhaps the first minutes." On most
of the front the breakthrough failed utterly; the offensive then ground on attritionally until
November 1916.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[first-world-war-1914]]` — the Somme was the British share of the coordinated 1916
Allied offensives, hastened to relieve the German assault on Verdun. The catastrophe was produced
proximately by **misplaced faith in the bombardment** (the wrong shell for the targets, amateur
gunnery, uncut wire) and by a **walking-pace wave plan** designed around the inexperience of the New
Armies — which forfeited tempo, surprise, and any means of command once the infantry crossed the
parapet.

## Historiography

The battle's historiography has shifted markedly away from the interwar "lions led by donkeys" narrative that blamed Haig's generalship almost exclusively. Primary sources include British and German unit war diaries, artillery logs, and veteran memoirs and literature (Sassoon, Graves, Blunden) that shaped the popular memory of futility; John Keegan's *The Face of Battle* (1976) reconstructed the mechanics of the first-day failure from the infantry's perspective and remains a standard methodological model for combat history. Since the 1970s, historians including Robin Prior, Trevor Wilson, and Gary Sheffield have argued for a more "learning curve" interpretation, in which the Somme's costly failures fed directly into the tactical innovations (creeping barrage, all-arms cooperation) that produced the more successful British operations of 1917–18. Casualty figures for the first day are well established; the deeper debate concerns whether the offensive was strategically necessary to relieve Verdun or reflected a failure of Allied coordination and imagination.
