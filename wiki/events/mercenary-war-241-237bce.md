# The Mercenary War / Truceless War (241–237 BCE)

## Narrative

The First Punic War ended with Carthage's treasury depleted and ~20,000 mercenary
soldiers awaiting discharge and back pay. The Carthaginian government sent them to
**Sicca Veneria** (modern El Kef in Tunisia) while it attempted — too slowly and too
stingily — to settle accounts. The assembly at Sicca grew increasingly mutinous.

Leadership fell to **Matho**, a Libyan, and **Spendius**, a runaway Roman slave from
Campania who feared re-enslavement if Carthage demobilized him. When negotiations
broke down over pay levels, the army marched on Tunis. Revolt spread rapidly to the
**subject Libyan population** — who had borne the heaviest burdens of the war —
and then to Numidia. Two loyal cities were besieged: Utica (by Spendius) and Hippo
Diarrhytus/Bizerta (by Matho).

The rebels showed remarkable organizational coherence: they issued **coinage** — a
bronze series (and some silver and gold) with Carthaginian types overlaid with the
ethnic ΑΙΒΥΩΝ ("of the Libyans"), signalling a political as well as military
organization. This is one of the most striking features of the revolt: a mercenary
army functioning as a proto-state.

### Hamilcar's Campaign

Carthage's initial response under **Hanno** failed. Command was transferred to
**Hamilcar Barca**, on political as much as military grounds — he was the returning
hero of the Sicilian war and represented the commercial/overseas faction against
Hanno's landed-aristocracy interest. With ~10,000 men, a large cavalry component, and
70 elephants, Hamilcar fought his way across the Bagradas bridge and inflicted initial
defeats using feigned retreat. When Matho retreated to higher ground to avoid
Carthaginian cavalry, a brutal guerrilla phase began.

The war's cruelty escalated: **Spendius tortured 700 Carthaginian prisoners** to
prevent any compromise. The Carthaginians responded in kind. Polybius, who calls this
*"a war of inexpiable hatred"* and says it *"far surpassed all the wars we know of in
cruelty and inhumanity"* (*Histories* I.65–88), attributes the atrocities to both
sides.

Sardinia's Punic garrison simultaneously mutinied, killed their Carthaginian commander,
crucified the reinforcements sent from Carthage, murdered all Carthaginians on the
island, and issued their own coinage (bronze, Isis / corn-ear types).

### Resolution and Roman Intervention

Forced into a reconciliation by the Carthaginian council's thirty-man committee,
Hamilcar and Hanno jointly commanded the final phase. In a decisive battle (probably
near Tunis) ~40,000 Carthaginians defeated ~30,000 rebels. The rest of Libya submitted.
Matho was paraded through Carthage and tortured to death. Utica and Hippo surrendered.

Rome's role was initially supportive of Carthage: it released Punic prisoners and
allowed supply shipments to Carthage but not the rebels. Rome declined the rebels'
invitation to occupy Sardinia — once.

When the Sardinian rebels were expelled by the natives, crossed to Italy, and appealed
again, Rome reversed course. With Carthage virtually without a fleet and exhausted,
Rome declared **war** when Carthage protested Rome's claim to Sardinia. Carthage
capitulated without a fight, ceding **Sardinia and Corsica** and paying 1,200 additional
talents. Polybius's verdict: "contrary to all justice."

## Significance

The Mercenary War established **Hamilcar Barca** as the dominant figure in Carthaginian
politics and military affairs. His vindication against Hanno's faction, the brutal
competence he demonstrated, and the unresolved sense of injustice over Sardinia all
drove his subsequent Spanish strategy — the construction of a Barcid empire in Iberia
that would fund and equip a new war against Rome.

Polybius treats the war as evidence that Carthage's reliance on mercenary forces was
a fundamental structural weakness compared to Rome's citizen army and loyal Italian
allies — a judgment that colors his entire assessment of the Punic Wars.

## "The Camp Comes to Carthage" (Miles)

Richard Miles ([[miles-carthage-must-be-destroyed-2010]]) reads the revolt not as a rabble
but as **"a decapitated version of Carthage's own Sicilian army"** turning on its employer.
Following Polybius, he stresses Carthaginian blunders — mustering all the mercenaries
together near the capital with no citizen counterforce, failing to keep wives and children
as hostages, then reneging on their generals' over-promises. The deliberately polyglot force
(mixed to prevent combination) now could not be negotiated with; **Spendius** (a Campanian
runaway slave) and **Matho** (a Libyan) seized leadership precisely because they feared
Carthaginian reprisal, and welded the mercenary grievance to a **Libyan tax revolt** bred by
the war's exactions (half of crop yields; doubled town taxes), drawing in ~70,000 Libyans.

The rebels **overstruck Carthaginian coin** with the Greek legend *LIBUWN* ("of the
Libyans"), using agricultural motifs to appeal to Libyans and Heracles/military motifs to the
mercenaries — evidence, for Miles, of a coherent breakaway polity. Polybius's term *polemos
aspondos* ("a war without possibility of truce") captures the atrocity spiral: the rebels
tortured the negotiator **Gisco** and their prisoners to death to foreclose reconciliation;
Hamilcar reciprocated by trampling captives with elephants. Miles also underscores the
decisive **external aid** — Hiero of Syracuse and, notably, **Rome** (banning trade with the
rebels, allowing recruitment, freeing 2,743 POWs without ransom) — even as Rome
simultaneously seized Sardinia, a seizure Polybius himself called "contrary to all justice."
The phrase "the camp had truly come to Carthage" frames the war as the moment the
mercenary system's contradictions came home.
