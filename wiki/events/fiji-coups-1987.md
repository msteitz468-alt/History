## Narrative

Fiji had been governed since independence in 1970 by the Alliance Party under Prime Minister Ratu Sir Kamisese Mara, whose electoral victories appeared "inevitable" according to [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]]. On 1 April 1987 the Alliance lost an election to a coalition of the new Fiji Labour Party — whose leader, Dr Timoci Bavadra, became Prime Minister — and the (Indian) National Federation Party (NFP). Bavadra's coalition "was dominated neither by chiefs nor by the Fijian 'establishment'", threatening the unity of turaga (chiefs) and vanua (people) that had always returned the Alliance to office.

Within a month, on 14 May, Colonel Sitiveni Rabuka and the Royal Fiji Military Forces overthrew Bavadra's government. A second coup in September installed an interim government — essentially the old Alliance Party, with Ratu Mara as interim Prime Minister — pending a new constitution.

The 1990 constitution "enshrines Fijian paramountcy in the political system, and contains provisions which prevented Fiji's return to the Commonwealth." The Great Council of Chiefs (Bose levu Vakaturaga) exercises a watchdog role, nominates the president (who must be of chiefly rank), and nominates 24 of 34 Senate members. In the Lower House, 37 of 70 seats are reserved for ethnic Fijians, 27 for Fijian Indians, five for General Voters, all elected from racially segregated rolls.

When elections were held in May 1992 the Bose levu Vakaturaga–sponsored party SVT won 30 Fijian seats. In an astonishing turn, Rabuka became Prime Minister — supported by Labour, the party he had overthrown — in exchange for assurances of a constitutional review. "A constitution intended to promote the unity of Fijians had divided them; Ratu Mara's nominee had lost; a commoner was Prime Minister, by the grace of the party which he overthrew in the first coup."

## Causal Analysis

**Direct causes:**
- caused_by: Fiji Labour Party victory threatening the chiefly hierarchy
- caused_by: Rhetoric of the Taukei Movement, which argued that "democracy is a foreign flower unsuited to the Fijian soil"

**Contributing factors:**
- contributed_to: Rapid social changes — "modern education was opening up opportunities for individual talent. Migration and urbanisation — more than one-third of Fijians live in urban areas — were inducing changes in values and perspectives"
- contributed_to: Ethnic demographic anxiety: Indo-Fijians were approximately 43 per cent of the population

**Deeper structural causes:**
- contributed_to: The "time-honoured and sacred institution of the [chiefly] system" as interpreted by nationalist Fijians was incompatible with one-person-one-vote outcomes

The source distinguishes two analytic poles: "Indigenous nationalists tend to applaud Rabuka's takeover as the only way to prevent 'foreign' (Indo-Fijian) domination and protect native sovereignty ... Liberal scholars counter that Fijian sovereignty was never at risk and that the new constitution is undemocratic and racist ... the coup leaders have used inflammatory and simplistic racial rhetoric to mask the real issues of class, rank, and customary authority."

## Consequence Analysis

**Immediate:**
- produced: 1990 Fijian constitution embedding ethnic paramountcy
- produced: Fiji's expulsion from the Commonwealth
- enabled: emigration of approximately 30,000 Indo-Fijians by mid-1990s

**Longer-term:**
- enabled: Constitutional review process and eventual adoption of new constitution
- produced: Deepening provincial fragmentation of Fijian politics
- produced: Land lease crisis when the Agricultural Landlord and Tenant Act expired in 1996

**Unintended:**
- produced: Fijian political disunity rather than unity the coup was meant to consolidate — "a constitution intended to promote the unity of Fijians had divided them"

## Historiography

The Fiji coups generated substantial scholarship. Key works cited in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] include: Ravuvu, *The Facade of Democracy*; Scarr, *Fiji: Politics of Illusion*; Sutherland, *Beyond the Politics of Race* (from which a submission to the Constitutional Inquiry and Advisory Committee is quoted); and Lal, *Power and Prejudice*.

The central historiographical divide is between an indigenous-rights reading (the coups as legitimate defence of Fijian sovereignty and land) and a liberal-democratic reading (the coups as ethnic elite manipulation). The source treats this as a live, unresolved debate as of 1997, noting that "neither is [Rabuka] a pawn in a scenario of Western 'impact' or a hapless victim of 'intellectual colonisation'. In public discourse Island political actors deploy racialist and nationalist ideas against established authorities. But underlying and feeding the rhetoric are more basic structural issues and intersecting interests — individual, local, factional, generational, and class."
