# The Spring and Autumn Period (771–479 BCE)

The first half of the **Eastern [[zhou-dynasty|Zhou]]** — the long unravelling of the Zhou order
after **771 BCE**, when royal authority collapsed into nominal supremacy and the regional states
became the real actors. It is named for the ***Springs and Autumns*** (*Chunqiu*), the chronicle of
the lords of **Lu** (first entry 722 BCE, last 481 BCE), and conventionally runs from the fall of
the western capital to the death of **[[confucius|Confucius]]** in 479 BCE. From
[[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]].

## Narrative

After the **[[zhou-dynasty|Quan Rong sacked Zong Zhou]] in 771 BCE**, the court fled east to
[[luoyang|Luo Yi/Cheng Zhou]]; the royal domain shrank to a fraction of its former size and the
kings could no longer raise armies. They kept the **title "king," the [[mandate-of-heaven|Mandate]],
and ceremonial monopolies**, and the territorial lords continued formally to acknowledge them — but
the grip was, in Major & Cook's words, "tenuous and slipping." The **regional cultures** of the
Chengdu Basin, the Middle Yangzi, Shandong, and the Huai — long overlaid by Zhou prestige — now
reasserted themselves, and states such as **Qin, Jin, Chu, Qi, Wu, and Yue** combined a Zhou
cultural veneer with local traditions to grow into great powers.

The era was one of **relentless inter-state competition**: the *Springs and Autumns* records the
**extinction of 52 states** and the **assassination of 36 rulers** over 241 years.

## The Ba Hegemons

To manage anarchy among nominally co-equal states, a new role emerged: the ***ba*** ("hegemon") —
a dominant lord who, in the name of "honouring the king and repelling the barbarians," enforced a
rough order. The system is traditionally credited to the Qi statesman **Guan Zhong** and his ruler
**Lord Huan of Qi** (r. 685–643 BCE), the universally acknowledged first hegemon; the second was
**Lord Wen of Jin** (the long-exiled prince **Chong'er**), confirmed by his victory over Chu at the
**Battle of Chengpu (632 BCE)**.

> **Source-critical caveat (Major & Cook):** the word *ba* appears **nowhere in the *Springs and
> Autumns* text itself** — only in the later *Zuo* commentary — and in **no known bronze inscription**
> of the period as a title. The hegemon "system," as later texts describe it, may be partly a
> retrospective construction.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` [[zhou-dynasty|the 771 BCE sack of the western Zhou capital]] and the structural decay of
a [[zhou-dynasty|"ritual state"]] that delegated power to hereditary regional lords. `enabled:` the
reassertion of regional cultures and the spread of **iron** (cast iron, adapted from bronze-casting
expertise) and the first **spade-shaped bronze coins** in Jin. The pivotal social change was the
transformation of the ***shi*** — the lowest aristocratic rank — from **chariot-warriors into
literate "men of service,"** the class from which Guan Zhong's merit-based administrators and
[[confucius|Confucius]] himself emerged.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **[[warring-states-period-475-bce|Warring States]]** that followed, as the surviving
great states abandoned even the fiction of hegemony for outright sovereignty and total war.
`produced:` the intellectual response to disorder — **[[confucianism|Confucius's]]** project to
restore the [[zhou-dynasty|early Zhou]] order — opening China's contribution to the
[[axial-age|Axial Age]]. Innovations like the appointed-official **county (*xian*)**, pioneered by
Guan Zhong and in Chu, foreshadowed the [[qin-dynasty|Qin]] administrative revolution.

## Historiography

The period is known above all from the terse ***Springs and Autumns*** annals of Lu and its three
classical commentaries (the narrative ***Zuozhuan*** and the exegetical *Gongyang* and *Guliang*) —
all later, [[confucianism|Confucian]]-shaped texts — supplemented by bronze inscriptions and,
increasingly, excavated manuscripts. [[major-cook-ancient-china-2017|Major & Cook (2017)]] apply
pointed **source criticism**: the **[[zhou-dynasty|ba hegemon]]** institution as later texts
describe it may be partly **retrospective**, since the title *ba* appears in **no** Spring and
Autumn bronze inscription and not in the *Springs and Autumns* itself; and the moralizing frame of
virtuous hegemons "honouring the king" is a Confucian construction read back onto an age of raw
inter-state competition. Dating is secure to the year (the annals are strictly chronological), but
the historicity of named **speeches and motives** in the *Zuozhuan* is debated.
