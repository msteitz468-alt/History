## Narrative

In the spring of 597 a company of nearly forty monks from Pope Gregory I's monastic foundation in Rome, led by their provost Augustine, landed on the Isle of Thanet in the territory of [[æthelberht-of-kent]], king of Kent. They had been sent by Gregory, who had been moved by the sight of Anglian slave boys in Rome and who was also responding practically to appeals from Christians in England, mediated through Frankish ecclesiastical channels.

The monks almost turned back during the journey north: "the Anglo-Saxons possessed a reputation for being a fierce and barbarous people." Gregory ordered them to continue (HE I, 23). They arrived apprehensive and were received courteously by Æthelberht, who established them at [[canterbury]].

Æthelberht's Frankish queen Bertha had already been practising her Christian faith at the church of St Martin in Canterbury, accompanied by the Frankish bishop Liudhard. Pope Gregory's understanding that the Merovingian kings wished their subjects converted (letter to Theuderic and Theudebert, 596) implies a Frankish political dimension to the opening of the mission.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by**: Gregory I's papal initiative, itself driven by the slave-market encounter with Anglian boys, intelligence about a potentially receptive Frankish-allied Kentish court, and claims of Frankish ecclesiastical and political influence over Anglo-Saxon territories.

**contributed_to**: The pre-existing Christian presence in Britain (Celtic church in the west; Frankish wife in the Kentish court; church of St Martin at Canterbury) created enabling conditions. Æthelberht's Frankish marriage gave Augustine's party diplomatic cover and probably confidence that the mission would be safe.

**preceded_by**: The Frankish marriage of Æthelberht to Bertha c. 580 and the accompanying presence of Bishop Liudhard at Canterbury.

## Consequence Analysis

The immediate consequence was the establishment of [[canterbury]] as the first archiepiscopal see for Anglo-Saxon England (displacing Gregory's original plan for London as the southern metropolitan). Augustine baptized Æthelberht between 597 and 601 (exact date uncertain — see Historiography). The creation of the see of London for Mellitus (HE II, 3) and the see of Rochester for Justus followed.

The larger-scale consequence was that the mission became the institutional spine of the Christianisation of England, enabling subsequent missions northward (the Paulinus mission to Northumbria) and the eventual conversion of all Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. The refusal of the British clergy at Augustine's Oak to accept Canterbury's authority was a serious setback to the pan-British ambitions of the mission.

## Actors

**Augustine**: Provost of Gregory's monastic foundation, first Archbishop of Canterbury. Bede styles him "archbishop of Britain" (HE II, 2).

**[[æthelberht-of-kent]]**: Received the missionaries; was baptized by 601; promulgated the earliest Anglo-Saxon lawcode; hosted the metropolitan see at Canterbury. Kirby argues his reign began no earlier than 589, so he had been king at most eight years when Augustine arrived — not the thirty-seven years implied by the conventional 560 accession date.

**Bertha**: Frankish queen of Æthelberht, already Christian. Her presence and the church of St Martin provided the physical and diplomatic foothold for the mission. Gregory's 601 letter criticised her for insufficient efforts to convert her husband.

**[[eadbald-of-kent]]**: Successor to Æthelberht; his pagan reaction (c. 616–c. 624) nearly destroyed the mission's gains, though [[canterbury]] itself was never abandoned.

## Historiography

The primary narrative source is Bede (HE I, 23–26; II, 2–5), drawing on oral tradition from Canterbury and surviving papal correspondence. Gregory's letters (some preserved only in Bede) provide contemporary documentation, allowing Kirby to test Bede's narrative. Key source-critical findings:

1. **Date of Æthelberht's baptism**: Bede implies 597 or shortly after. Gregory's letter to Eulogius (June 598) does not mention the king's baptism. The 601 letter to Bertha criticises her for insufficient effort to convert the king. Kirby judges baptism most likely sometime between 597 and 601, probability weighted toward the later end.

2. **Æthelberht's reign length**: The conventional 560–616 reign, if correct, would mean Æthelberht had been king for thirty-seven years when Augustine arrived. Kirby argues 56 years = age at death rather than regnal length, placing accession no earlier than 589 — reducing the king's establishment by the time of the mission to eight years at most.

3. **Gregory's original plan (London + York)**: Gregory intended London and York as twin metropolitans, each with twelve suffragans (HE I, 29). London never became a metropolitan. The emergence of Canterbury — not London — as the archiepiscopal centre was a contingent outcome, dependent on the Kentish political situation.

4. **The refusal of the British clergy**: The conference at Augustine's Oak, on the north-western border of West Saxon territory, ended in the refusal of British clergy to accept Canterbury's authority (HE II, 2), a major setback to pan-British ecclesiastical ambitions.
