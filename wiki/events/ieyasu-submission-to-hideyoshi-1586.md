## Narrative

From the end of 1584 to late 1586, Ieyasu and Hideyoshi maintained a wary standoff. Hideyoshi's power continued to expand — he defeated Chosokabe in Shikoku (1585), subdued Shimazu in Kyushu — but he avoided direct military confrontation with Ieyasu, recognizing the cost of the Komaki-Nagakute stalemate. Instead, he pursued diplomatic means: emissaries were repeatedly sent to Ieyasu in Mikawa asking him to come to the capital and submit. Ieyasu, hawk on wrist, dismissed them unceremoniously. "He doesn't know the country about here and I do," he told one envoy. "He seems to have forgotten Nagakude pretty quickly."

**The Marriage Alliance.** Hideyoshi then took a "distinctly novel step for one in his commanding position": he offered his half-sister Asahi-hime as Ieyasu's wife, effectively sending a member of his own family as a diplomatic gesture. Asahi-hime was already married; that husband was set aside. Ieyasu agreed but set conditions: his heir Naganaru (later Hidetada) must succeed him regardless of any son born of this union; Naganaru must not go as a hostage; and Naganaru must inherit all four provinces intact. Hideyoshi conceded all three terms.

**Hideyoshi's Mother as Hostage.** To bring Ieyasu to Osaka, Hideyoshi made an extraordinary concession: he sent his elderly mother O-mandokoro to Okazaki as a de facto hostage, allowing Ieyasu to visit Osaka without risking capture. Honda Shigetsugu warned Ieyasu that the old lady might be a substitute planted by Hideyoshi — suspicion only dispelled when Asahi-hime fell weeping into her mother's arms at the palanquin door.

**The Visit.** Ieyasu left detailed contingency plans with Ii Naomasa before departing: if Hideyoshi attempted to seize him, he would slip to Higashiyama and send word for two armies of 10,000 each to converge on Kyoto from different directions, cutting off Hideyoshi before he could reach Osaka. He then traveled to Osaka.

Hideyoshi visited Ieyasu incognito the night he arrived. He was frank: "My officers don't really respect me, for they were all my colleagues not long ago. So I want you to greet me before them all in the most deferential manner, for that will impress them as nothing else would. And I on my part will treat you with the greatest consideration." Ieyasu agreed, and at the formal ceremony deferred to Hideyoshi visibly before his assembled lords. Sadler quotes the proverb that crystallized the outcome: "Ieyasu won the Empire by yielding" ("Ieyasu makete tenka wo toru").

Both sides argued peace was better than further war. Ieyasu's speech to his own council was notable: "What would the loss of my one life matter compared with the avoidance of suffering and death to so many innocent ones?" The "simple Mikawa warriors" could not refuse such magnanimity — though Sadler, following Tokutomi, treats this as calculated diplomacy.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[komaki-nagakute-campaign-1584]]` — The stalemate established mutual deterrence; Hideyoshi could not simply conquer Ieyasu; Ieyasu could not ultimately prevail against Hideyoshi's superior resources.
- `contributed_to:` The defection of Ishikawa Kazumasa (one of Ieyasu's senior councilors) to Hideyoshi in 1585 demonstrated the limits of Ieyasu's position and the magnetic appeal of the Toyotomi court.
- `contributed_to:` Ieyasu's successful negotiations with the Hōjō family (1586 visit to Kisegawa) gave him security on his eastern flank before committing to the western relationship.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` A formal hierarchical relationship in which Ieyasu acknowledged Hideyoshi's supremacy while retaining his five provinces and de facto autonomy.
- `enabled: [[odawara-campaign-1590]]` — As Hideyoshi's subordinate ally, Ieyasu participated in the reduction of the Hōjō, which led directly to his own transfer to Kantō.
- `produced:` Ieyasu's court promotion to Gon-Chunagon (at Hideyoshi's suggestion), binding him further into the imperial honors system Hideyoshi was using to domesticate the daimyo.

## Actors

- [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] — submitted on terms he himself largely dictated
- [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] — secured nominal supremacy; needed the submission as legitimacy
- Asahi-hime — Hideyoshi's half-sister sent as Ieyasu's wife; a diplomatic instrument who died four years later having produced no children
- O-mandokoro — Hideyoshi's mother, sent to Okazaki as effective hostage to guarantee Ieyasu's safe visit

## Historiography

The Taikoki and Mikawa Fudoki provide the main narratives, each with pro-Toyotomi and pro-Tokugawa tilts respectively. Sadler, following Tokutomi Soho, interprets both sides as rational actors calculating expected costs and benefits. The characterization of Ieyasu's speech about sacrificing himself for the people as "magnanimous altruism" that the "simple Mikawa warriors" could not resist is clearly ironic. The episode is a classic case study in Sengoku-period diplomatic theater: the submission was real but the terms were negotiated, and both parties understood they were managing a relationship that would eventually have only one survivor.
