## Narrative

The Troubles were a thirty-year conflict (approximately 1968–1998/2007) centred on [[northern-ireland]] but with significant episodes in Britain and the Republic of Ireland. The conflict had three identifiable phases as analyzed in Paul Arthur's chapter (ch. 37 of Jackson, *Oxford Handbook*): analysis/frozen violence (late 1960s–1985), negotiation (1985–1998), and implementation (1998–2007).

**Origins.** Conor Cruise O'Brien, addressing students at Queen's University Belfast in October 1968, spoke of the 'condition of Derry' as one of 'frozen violence: any attempt to thaw it out will liberate violence which is at present static.' The IRA in Belfast numbered no more than 24 members in 1962 and had grown only to 120 by 1969. The Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) had been active since 1966 but was proscribed; the Ulster Defence Association (UDA) did not yet exist. The transition to protracted violence required not armament but a shift in attitudes — specifically, the crystallization of 'a discourse of grievance, a style of politics that had a long pedigree in Irish politics' (Aughey, cited in ch. 37).

The Civil Rights Movement, beginning with People's Democracy protests at Queen's University in October 1968, translated Catholic alienation from the Stormont regime into street action. Serious sectarian rioting in Belfast and Derry in August 1969 forced the British government to deploy the army, exposing Stormont's dependence on Westminster and triggering the Downing Street Declaration of 1969 — the first assertion of direct British responsibility for Northern Ireland's governance.

**Escalation: Internment and Bloody Sunday.** Internment without trial, introduced in August 1971, and Bloody Sunday on 30 January 1972 — when British paratroopers shot dead 13 unarmed civilians in Derry (a fourteenth dying later) — were catalysts of radicalization. The Saville Inquiry (2010) concluded that Bloody Sunday 'strengthened the Provisional IRA, increased nationalist resentment and hostility towards the army and exacerbated the violent conflict of the years that followed.' Prime Minister David Cameron acknowledged on 15 June 2010 that 'what happened on Bloody Sunday was both unjust and unjustifiable.'

Direct rule from Westminster was imposed in March 1972, with William Whitelaw as the first Secretary of State for Northern Ireland. During the period of direct rule (1972–2007, with a brief interlude in 1974), over 250,000 armed personnel served in Northern Ireland in 'Operation Banner' — the largest peacetime operation ever undertaken by the UK — of whom 651 were killed and 6,307 wounded.

**The IRA's 'Long War' strategy.** The Provisional IRA, which emerged from the 1969–70 IRA split, pursued what it called the 'long war': an armed campaign combining bombings, assassinations, and occasional mass-casualty attacks in Northern Ireland and Britain, combined from 1982 with a 'ballot box and Armalite' political strategy. The Enniskillen bomb (1987, 11 killed at a Remembrance Day ceremony) was a public relations catastrophe that accelerated republican re-examination of armed struggle. Gerry Adams was convicted in 1982 that 'without [armed struggle], the issue of Ireland would not even be an issue. So, in effect, the armed struggle becomes armed propaganda.'

**Peace Process.** See [[good-friday-agreement-1998]] for the diplomatic route to settlement. The ceasefires of August (IRA) and October (UDA/UVF) 1994, the Anglo-Irish Agreement 1985, the Downing Street Declaration 1993 (Major-Reynolds), and the internationalisation via US pressure were all preconditions for the 1998 Agreement.

**Statistics.** Over 3,600 people were killed during the Troubles. The nature of the conflict is documented most fully in McKittrick, Kelters, Feeney, and Thornton, *Lost Lives* (2000), which removes analysis 'from the statistical and abstract to the deeply personal' (ch. 37).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` partition of ireland 1921 — the structural settlement of 1921 created a political entity in which the Catholic minority had legitimate grievances about discrimination in employment, housing, and voting
- `caused_by:` Stormont one-party Unionist rule (1921–72) — the Unionist government's failure to reform produced the conditions for mass Catholic alienation
- `contributed_to:` internment (August 1971) and Bloody Sunday (January 1972) — radicalized a new generation of republicans and destroyed constitutional nationalist credibility with the SDLP's withdrawal from Stormont
- `contributed_to:` IRA military campaign — the bombing campaign created pressure for British concessions but also hardened Unionist resistance and produced loyalist counter-violence
- `preceded_by:` northern ireland civil rights movement 1967 (to be created)

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` [[good-friday-agreement-1998]] — after 30 years of violence, the political settlement redrew the constitutional architecture of Northern Ireland
- `produced:` policing reform — the Police Service of Northern Ireland (PSNI) replaced the RUC in 2001; Sinn Féin joined the Policing Board in 2007
- `produced:` power-sharing executive (2007) — the DUP and Sinn Féin entered government together under Ian Paisley and Martin McGuinness
- `produced:` transformation of Irish historiography — the Troubles dominated and distorted the writing of Irish history for a generation, as both revisionist and anti-revisionist factions mapped contemporary political commitments onto the past
- `enabled:` decline of the Orange Order — direct rule eroded the Order's nepotistic influence and a more assertive Catholic community challenged its control of public space

## Actors

Key actors include the Provisional IRA, the SDLP (particularly John Hume as leader from 1979), Sinn Féin (Gerry Adams), the Ulster Volunteer Force, the Ulster Defence Association, successive British Secretaries of State, and international actors including US Presidents (particularly Bill Clinton) and the European Community.

## Historiography

The Troubles generated an extraordinarily large scholarly literature. John Whyte's *Interpreting Northern Ireland* (1990) remains the most systematic survey of competing interpretations. By 1990, Northern Ireland was described as 'perhaps the most heavily researched area in the world' (ch. 37). The historiography was deeply affected by contemporary politics: works written before 1985 tend to emphasize security or structural-Marxist frameworks; those written after 1998 reflect 'quieter optimism' (Aughey, *Politics of Northern Ireland*, 2005). Paul Bew, Peter Gibbon, and Henry Patterson's *The State in Northern Ireland 1921–1972* (1979) and its 1996 sequel demonstrate how the same authors changed analytical frameworks across the period.

The Troubles complicated Irish historiography more broadly: revisionist historians who challenged nationalist myths of the revolution were accused of providing propaganda for Unionism, while traditionalists were accused of legitimating IRA violence. This debate now has 'somewhat subsided' in the post-1998 environment (ch. 31).
