# The Battle of Courtrai (11 July 1302)

At **Courtrai** (Kortrijk), an army of **Flemish urban and rural footsoldiers** annihilated
the **flower of French chivalry** — "an astonishing event in military history."
[[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]] (Wim Blockmans, Ch. 9b; William Chester Jordan, Ch. 11)
treats it as both a social and a military watershed. It is traditionally called the **Battle
of the Golden Spurs** for the hundreds of knightly spurs gathered from the French dead.

## Narrative

The battle was the climax of **[[philip-iv-capetian|Philip IV's]]** attempt to annex
**[[county-of-flanders|Flanders]]**. French occupation, resented by Flemish-speaking
artisans and peasants, provoked a strike of all "mechanical" workers in Ghent and the
**Bruges Matins** (18 May 1302), in which Flemish craftsmen slaughtered some 2,000 French
occupants and patricians. When the royal army came to punish the rebels, it met the Flemish
militia on **marshy ground** near Courtrai. The French committed their cavalry in a
"senseless charge"; up to 10,000 of their best-trained knights were broken, and **Robert of
Artois** and many of Philip's chief advisers — including the legist **Pierre Flotte** — were
"hacked to pieces."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Philip IV's annexation of Flanders** and the French occupation, which
  "awakened a fervent Flemish resistance, which might be labelled national."
- `contributed_to:` the deep **social antagonism** between the patriciate (allied with the
  king) and the craftsmen (allied with the count), whose conjuncture "made a revolutionary
  social and political transformation possible."
- `enabled:` the tactical conditions — the marshy terrain that neutralised cavalry — and "the
  emergence of the massive armies of artisans and peasants as occasional fighters."

## Consequence Analysis

Two lasting effects (Blockmans):

- It **secured the continuity of the county of Flanders** as a largely independent fief under
  the French crown — Philip recovered ground (Mons-en-Pévèle, 1304; the Treaty of
  Athis-sur-Orge, 1305) but never absorbed Flanders.
- It **radically ended the predominance of the patricians**, producing "a far-reaching
  breakthrough in the artisans' claims for autonomous organisation and political
  participation," giving Flanders "arguably the most open and broadly based political system
  of Europe."

Militarily, Courtrai (with the contemporary infantry victories of the Scots and Swiss) gave
"a heavy blow" to the assumption of noble military superiority: "New weapons, new social
relations and new values had definitively eroded the hegemony of the nobles." It belongs with
the early signs of the later-medieval **infantry revolution**.
