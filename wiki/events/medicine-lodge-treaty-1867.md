# Medicine Lodge Treaty (October 1867)

## Narrative

At **Medicine Lodge Creek** (Kansas) in October 1867 — a Kiowa medicine-dance ground ~75 miles southwest of present Wichita — U.S. commissioners negotiated a set of treaties with southern plains nations including [[actors/comanches|Comanches]], Kiowa, Southern Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Kiowa Apache. The agreements promised annuities, agencies, and reserved lands in Indian Territory in exchange for ending raids and ceding claims to vast hunting ranges. Free bands — notably Quahadi Comanches of the Llano — largely refused the bargain or were unrepresented. Continued raiding and the failure of Quaker-administered [[actors/grant-ulysses-s|Grant]] Peace Policy implementation set up Sherman's turn to hard war (from 1871) and eventually the [[events/red-river-war-1874|Red River War]].

### Gathering (Gwynne)

Last great free-Indian gathering of the southern plains. U.S. peace commission (including the commissioner of Indian affairs and [[actors/william-t-sherman|William T. Sherman]]) + Comanche, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa, Kiowa Apache; **~4,000 Indians** (100 Comanche lodges); 9 newspapers; 500 dress-uniform guard + howitzers; massive gift/food logistics.

### Speeches and terms

Penateka chief Tosawa (**Silver Brooch**) warned that long-promised goods had never come and threatened return to the prairie. **Ten Bears** (Yamparika) delivered the celebrated speech rejecting walls and blaming Texans/soldiers: "I was born under the prairie, where the wind blew free… I want to die there and not within walls. … If the Texans had kept out of my country, there might have been peace." Sherman's ultimatum: cannot stop white advance — "You can no more stop this than you can stop the sun or the moon… You must submit and do the best you can."

**Signed 21 October 1867** by marks of Yamparika, Nokoni, Penateka headmen (Ten Bears, Horse Back, Silver Brooch et al.). **~1/3 of Comanches unrepresented** — mostly Kotsotekas and **Quahadis** of the [[places/llano-estacado|Llano Estacado]]. Quahadis had never signed anything and never would — yet the U.S. held the **entire tribe** to the treaty.

**Terms (Comanche-Kiowa):** share a **2.9-million-acre** reservation in southwestern Oklahoma (good land; tiny vs ~**200-million-acre** peak Comanchería; excluded best Texas bison plains). Hunt south of the Arkansas allowed but meant the end of buffalo-following plains life as a free system.

**Quanah present at ~18** (from Navajo warpath via a Cheyenne village for beeves/sugar/coffee) as unreconstructed Quahadi element; recalled soldier chief's two propositions: fight on the Arkansas or move to the Wichita Mountains with help if the warpath stopped — chiefs chose move.

### Immediate betrayal / implementation failure

- Agent J. H. Leavenworth sited the Comanche-Kiowa agency at **Fort Cobb on the Wichita reservation** (wrong land).
- Thousands arrived winter 1867–68 but the treaty provided **no rations**; annuities delayed until Congress ratified summer 1868.
- Hungry Comanches raided Wichitas; troops banned from the agency; Leavenworth walked off the job spring 1868 — **May–October 1868: no federal authority** on the reservation.
- When goods came: shoddy oversized clothes, rancid pork, detested cornmeal; the 1867 peace commission itself had documented agent graft starving Indians.
- Office of Indian Affairs got Interior to deliver tons of quality arms (Spencer/Henry repeaters) while the army still used single-shots — in violation of laws against arming Indians.

Grant Peace Policy (~from 1869): Quaker agents replace corrupt Indian service; Comanches to reservation as farmers per Medicine Lodge. Lawrie Tatum found ~two-thirds of Comanches off-reservation 1869. Policy "rewarded aggression and punished good conduct": raids → treaties + gifts; good behavior → nothing. Reservation as sanctuary: troops barred → base for Texas raids; simply cross the Red River to keep stolen stock. Even pacifist Tatum concluded force was needed.

Gwynne on structure: "Though Medicine Lodge was ostensibly a bargaining session, in fact there was no bargaining at all. The whites were issuing a thinly disguised ultimatum." From a century and a half later it can seem cynical; eastern lawmakers at the time hoped for a final solution — "They were mistaken. Instead, Medicine Lodge provided the framework for the last great betrayal of the Indians."

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** U.S. need to secure the plains corridor after the Civil War for rails, cattle, and settlement.
- **contributed_to:** Reservation legal framework used later to define "hostiles" vs. agency Indians; paper claim that non-signatory Quahadis were bound.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** A treaty regime honored unevenly; moral and political cover for later military campaigns against non-signatory or non-compliant bands; 2.9M-acre legal reservation geography later centered administratively on [[places/fort-sill|Fort Sill]].
- **enabled:** Rhetoric of broken Indian promises *and* broken U.S. promises in subsequent historiography; Grant Peace Policy experiment and its collapse into the 1871 Sherman hard line and 1874 Red River War.
- **not produced:** Peace with free Quahadi/Kotsoteka bands of the Llano; end of raiding.

## Historiography

Documentary treaty texts vs. oral understanding of hunting rights; Ten Bears' speech often anthologized as plains eloquence. Gwynne treats Medicine Lodge as peace theater / thinly disguised ultimatum that could not bind the still-independent buffalo hunters of the llano, and as the legal frame for "the last great betrayal" via corrupt Indian Office after eastern hope of final solution. Do not present Medicine Lodge as successful pacification — it is the paper prelude to hard war. Later Jerome Commission / Dawes allotment fights (1880s–1900) still cited Medicine Lodge acreage baselines (e.g. 320 vs 160 acres) — long afterlife of the 1867 text.

### Fowler, CHNPA I.2 (append)

Fowler’s body narrative states that Southern Arapahos and some groups of Cheyennes and the Comanches and Kiowas agreed in **1865**, and again in **1867** when the government realized it could not adhere to the 1865 agreement, to accept **reservations, protection from unprovoked attack, and supplies for lost game**. The chapter body **does not use the place-name “Medicine Lodge”**; the bibliographic essay points to Douglas C. Jones, *The Treaty of Medicine Lodge*, as a detailed account of “one such treaty council.” Pair Gwynne’s council-scene detail with Fowler’s two-step (1865→1867) renegotiation frame. Southern Arapaho pursuit of a workable North Canadian IT reservation after 1867 terms proved unfeasible led to Grant’s **1869** Cheyenne–Arapaho executive-order reservation ([[actors/cheyenne]], [[places/indian-territory]]).
