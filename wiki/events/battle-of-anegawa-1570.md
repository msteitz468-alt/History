## Narrative

In the summer of 1570 [[oda-nobunaga]] and [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] had recently retreated from a campaign into Echizen after Asai Nagamasa — Nobunaga's brother-in-law — unexpectedly defected to the anti-Oda coalition organized by Shogun Yoshiaki. The retreat was managed by Ieyasu and Hideyoshi covering the rear. Nobunaga then advanced again and appeared before Asai's headquarters at Odani on the nineteenth day of the sixth month, moving to attack Yokoyama Castle on the left bank of the Anegawa River. Ieyasu arrived with approximately five thousand men to join the allied force.

The combined Asai-Asakura army — roughly eight thousand from Asai and ten thousand from Asakura — drew up on the far bank of the shallow Anegawa (about three feet deep) with the intention of attacking at dawn. Nobunaga moved first. In a last-minute command change Nobunaga decided to engage Asai personally, against whom he felt particular resentment, switching the original assignments. Ieyasu calmly accepted the change despite his commanders' objections about confusion in the orders: "To confront a great force is a fine thing," he observed (Sadler).

The battle opened at four in the morning with both armies crossing the river. Nobunaga commanded twenty-three thousand, but their quality was uneven; the Omi levies were placed under Hideyoshi. Nobunaga arranged his force thirteen ranks deep to withstand the initial shock. The Tokugawa-Mikawa force under Ieyasu engaged Asakura's larger contingent, with Sakai and Ishikawa commanding the van, and Sakakibara and Honda the second division.

The decisive moment came when Asai's men broke deeply into Nobunaga's ranks and "even menaced that commander himself" (Sadler). Ieyasu detached part of his force to strike Asai's flank while his men were still engaged with Asakura. Inaba Ittetsu, who had held troops in reserve, fell on Asai's right simultaneously, and elements of the Yokoyama besieging force rushed to support. The combined pressure broke both enemy formations. The toll was 3,170 heads, "a large proportion taken by the Tokugawa force" (Sadler). Ieyasu was "well splashed with blood" in the fighting; an unidentified Asakura warrior broke through his guard and was cut down by two of his retainers.

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause was the defensive need to contain the Asai-Asakura coalition that threatened Nobunaga's communications and base. The enabling condition was Ieyasu's arrival with the Mikawa force, whose discipline and fighting quality proved decisive. Sadler's *Mikawa Go-Fudoki* source gives a vivid picture of the battle as "a real feudal battle... bands of retainers fighting in groups, the lopping of heads by sword and bill, the confused mingling of the armies, the clouds of black smoke and dust."

## Consequence Analysis

The victory was attributed primarily to Ieyasu's tactical intervention at the critical moment — swinging forces from the Asakura front to support Nobunaga against Asai. Nobunaga's subsequent tribute described Ieyasu as "the sheet-anchor of our house, and the great builder of the portal of martial valour" and presented him with the Shogun Yoshiteru's sword and an arrowhead formerly belonging to Minamoto Tametomo. The battle did not end the Asai-Asakura threat, which continued until 1573, but it secured Nobunaga's position in Omi for the time being.

## Historiography

The primary source for this battle is A.L. Sadler's 1937 biography of Ieyasu, itself built on Japanese house-chronicle traditions such as the *Mikawa Go-Fudoki* — a document compiled to celebrate Tokugawa martial virtue and inherently favorable to Ieyasu's role. Numbers such as troop strengths and the 3,170-head count derive from this chronicle tradition and should be read as period convention rather than precise administrative record, a common caveat for Sengoku-era battle accounts. Anegawa receives comparatively little independent scholarly attention next to more celebrated engagements of the unification wars (Nagashino, Sekigahara), and modern narratives largely reproduce the same chronicle material rather than offering fresh archival discovery. No major revisionist reassessment of the battle is reflected in this wiki; its status as a secondary action supporting the ongoing Yokoyama siege is not seriously disputed.
