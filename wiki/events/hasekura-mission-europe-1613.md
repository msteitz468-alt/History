# Hasekura Mission to Europe (1613–1620)

The Hasekura mission was an embassy dispatched by [[date-masamune|Date Masamune]], lord of Sendai,
to the Pope in Rome and the king of Spain, led by Date's councilor Hasekura Rokuemon and the
Franciscan friar Father Luis Sotelo. It is the most ambitious Japanese diplomatic initiative in
the Western Hemisphere before the modern period, and its failure is a precise illustration of how
the early [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa bakufu]] neutralized tozama daimyo attempts at independent
foreign relations. Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Background

In 1613 Date Masamune contrived to send Hasekura Rokuemon to Rome, accompanied by Father Sotelo,
a Franciscan monk condemned to death for unauthorized preaching in Edo. Date obtained Sotelo's
pardon "for purposes of his own." Sotelo had won Date's goodwill by curing one of his ladies of
illness (refusing payment: "these things were done for the sake of his 'Way'"), and Date had given
him a temple in Sendai and listened to his preaching.

Date's motives were mixed: his Sendai fief lay directly opposite New Spain on the Pacific, and he
wanted direct trade; he may also have hoped for Spanish assistance against Ieyasu. Sotelo aimed to
be appointed Archbishop of Japan. Sebastian Viszcaino — a Spanish explorer in Japan on a search
for legendary islands of gold and silver — wanted a free passage home and was engaged as nominal
captain.

[[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] permitted and even assisted the mission, sending his own admiral Mukai
Shogen to Sendai to supervise construction of the ship: 108 feet long, 36 feet wide, with a
99-foot mast, built of cryptomeria wood. The voyage departed with 180 persons including Hasekura
and five senior Sendai officers, ten of Mukai Shogen's retainers, forty Europeans, and merchants.

---

## Voyage

**Pacific crossing.** Viszcaino quarreled violently with the Japanese, "threatened his life" was
reportedly feared, and he retired from command to become an ordinary passenger, departing at
Zakatula, the first Spanish American port. The mission continued to Acapulco (arriving January
1613 by Japanese calendar, likely 1614 by Western reckoning).

**Mexico.** At Mexico City, seventy-eight members of Hasekura's suite were baptized in the
cathedral of San Francisco. Hasekura declined baptism there, advised to save the ceremony for
Madrid where it would have more political impact. They crossed the isthmus to Vera Cruz and sailed
to Havana.

**Spain.** Traveling through Seville, Córdoba, Toledo, and Madrid. Philip III of Spain received
Hasekura and Sotelo. Hasekura was baptized by the Royal Chaplain acting for the Archbishop of
Toledo; the Duke of Lerma was his godfather, the Countess of Barachia his godmother. The mission
spent eight months in Spain, visiting Alcalá, Guadalajara, Zaragoza, Lérida, and Barcelona.

**Italy and Rome.** Via Savoy and Genoa to Rome. Pope Paul V received the embassy. Hasekura was
made a Roman citizen; the Latin document was still preserved in the Date family archives as of
Sadler's writing. The originals of Date's letters to Spain were likewise still held there.

**Date's letters to the Pope.** Date wrote that he "had great respect for Christianity, though he
is hindered in becoming a convert by an obstacle" (clearly Ieyasu's prohibition). He promised to
"make all my vassals Christians" if Franciscans were sent: "I will treat them kindly." He promised
annual ships to New Spain. These letters were simultaneously his opening bid for trade and a
covert suggestion that he might use Western backing against Ieyasu.

---

## Failure

The embassy was actively sabotaged on multiple fronts:

**The Jesuits** wrote to Rome that the mission came not from Japan's ruler but from "a subject,"
and was illegitimate.

**Sebastian Viszcaino** declared from personal grievance that the real object was purely trade,
that Japanese had "little love for Christianity," and — most damaging — that "Ieyasu and Hidetada
were Protestants." He also claimed Date had sent the mission without Ieyasu's knowledge (false).

**The Venetian ambassador in Rome**, Simon Contarini, reported that Sotelo had told the Pope that
Date was "next in power and dignity to the Emperor (Shogun), would try to supplant him, and then
would not only declare himself a Christian and the obedient servant of the Church of Rome, but
would afterwards compel all other princes of the country to do the same" — echoing Hideyoshi's
boast about China. Contarini also noted the Pope feared the king of Spain would make Japan
Spanish rather than papal territory.

**Bishop Cercyra** wrote the Jesuit general warning that if the mission succeeded and Franciscans
arrived in the Kantō, Ieyasu would "give vent to his indignation in causing the total ruin" of
Date, "for his estates are entirely dependent on the king's good-will."

The Pope received the mission but granted only one of three requests in modified form: he would
receive Date "under his protection." He deferred the appointment of a bishop and the sending of
monks to consultation with Spain. Sotelo was not made Archbishop of Japan.

---

## Return and Aftermath

When the mission returned to Japan, Christianity was more suppressed than ever. Hasekura reported
that European Christianity was "nothing but a vain show." As Sadler concludes: "none of the
promoters of the mission gained much, except it was the Bakufu that profited by the information
it brought back. Which was just what Ieyasu had intended."

The mission is connected to the broader Okubo Nagayasu conspiracy: Nagayasu had proposed calling
in foreign soldiers to overthrow Ieyasu; his patron Okubo Tadachika and Ieyasu's sixth son
Matsudaira Tadateru (who had married Date's daughter) both fell under suspicion and were punished.

## Historiography

The Hasekura mission is among the best-documented episodes in early Tokugawa foreign relations,
with sources from Japanese domain archives (Date family records), Spanish royal archives (originals
of Date's letters), and Venetian diplomatic dispatches (Contarini). VOC (Dutch) records provide
an adversarial European perspective. Sadler's account draws on all these strands and on Tokutomi's
Japanese biography of Ieyasu. The scholarly consensus is that Date's primary motive was commercial
(Pacific trade) rather than religious, and that Ieyasu co-opted the mission for intelligence while
allowing Date no independent benefit. The episode is sometimes described as the single most
ambitious Japanese attempt at Pacific diplomacy before the modern era.
