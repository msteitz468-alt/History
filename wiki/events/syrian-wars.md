## Narrative

The **Syrian Wars** were the recurring conflicts between the [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemies]]
and the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]] — the "scarlet thread" running through the history
of both dynasties (CAH VII.1 ch. 11, H. Heinen). Their root cause lay at
[[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]] (301): [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy I]] held the disputed
border province of **Coele-Syria** (Syria and Phoenicia), and the Seleucids never
abandoned their claim to it. But the wars ranged far beyond that frontier, reaching the
Aegean and the coasts of Asia Minor, "the sources of energy for the Hellenistic world,"
toward which both kingdoms were drawn "as though by a magnet."

CAH VII.1 covers the third-century cluster (the war-numbering is conventional):
- **War of Succession** (c. 280–279) — Ptolemy II exploits the chaos after
  [[battle-of-corupedium-281|Corupedium]] to seize coast and islands while
  [[antiochus-i-soter|Antiochus I]] secures his inheritance.
- **First Syrian War** (274–271) — Ptolemy II vs. Antiochus I; ends with no real change,
  a relative Ptolemaic success (celebrated in Theocritus' *Idyll 17* and the Grand
  Procession).
- **Second Syrian War** (260–253) — on Antiochus II's accession; Antiochus II gains in
  Ionia, Cilicia, Pamphylia, and the rise of [[rhodes|Rhodes]] begins; sealed by his
  marriage to Ptolemy II's daughter Berenice.
- **Third Syrian War / Laodicean War** (246–241) — the murder of Berenice triggers
  Ptolemy III's spectacular march to the Euphrates and Babylonia; he wins coastal
  Asia Minor and even holds Seleuceia-in-Pieria, but is recalled by an Egyptian revolt.
- **Fourth Syrian War** (221–217) — [[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]] vs. Ptolemy
  IV, decided at **[[battle-of-raphia-217|Raphia]]** (217), the terminal event of CAH
  VII.1.
- *(Later: Fifth (202–200, Seleucid victory at Panium gives them Coele-Syria) and Sixth
  (170–168, halted by Rome) — beyond this volume.)*

## Causal & Consequence Analysis

Heinen's analysis: the wars are the working-out of an **insoluble territorial claim**
(Coele-Syria) compounded by the fact that change of sovereign on either throne regularly
"provided the grounds for the military conflict." Coele-Syria mattered to the Ptolemies as
a **defensive glacis** for Egypt and a source of timber and trade revenue (see
[[ptolemaic-egypt]]); to the Seleucids it was an intolerable Ptolemaic foothold "at the
gates of the capital." There was a **de facto balance of power** but no agreed principle
of equilibrium — "a battle of each against the rest." The wars' wider consequences were
the reshaping of Asia Minor (the rise of [[pergamon|Pergamum]], [[rhodes|Rhodes]],
Bithynia, Pontus, Cappadocia in the gaps left by Seleucid distraction) and, eventually,
the invitation of **Rome** into Near Eastern affairs.

## Historiography

Heinen stresses how **poorly documented** the third-century East is: no connected ancient
narrative survives between Diodorus (breaks off 302) and [[polybius|Polybius]] (begins his
main account c. 220) — so the early wars are reconstructed from scattered notices,
inscriptions, papyri (the Gurob and Pithom documents), and coins. Polybius is the firm
source only from the Fourth War. See [[hellenistic-historiography]].
