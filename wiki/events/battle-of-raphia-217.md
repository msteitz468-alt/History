## Narrative

The battle of Raphia (**22 June 217 BCE**, near Gaza) was the decisive engagement of the
**[[syrian-wars|Fourth Syrian War]]** and the **terminal event of CAH VII.1's narrative**
— the year [[polybius|Polybius]] singled out (with the Peace of Naupactus) as the
beginning of *symploke*. It pitted **[[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus III]]** against
**Ptolemy IV Philopator** for the possession of Coele-Syria.

Antiochus had overrun much of Coele-Syria (219–218), helped by the defection of the
Ptolemaic commander **Theodotus**; meanwhile the Alexandrian ministers **Sosibius** and
Agathocles bought time with a truce and frantically rebuilt the army — including, for the
first time, the **enrolment of 20,000 native Egyptians into the phalanx**. At Raphia the
numerically superior Ptolemaic army (c. 75,000) broke the Seleucid line — the Ptolemaic
phalanx and the right wing under Echecrates routing Antiochus' left. Antiochus surrendered
Coele-Syria and withdrew to Antioch; **Ptolemy IV did not press his victory** to destroy
the Seleucid kingdom (a leniency Polybius criticised, but which Heinen judges realistic),
and a year's truce restored the *status quo ante*.

## Consequence Analysis

- **Coele-Syria recovered** by Egypt — the last phase of Ptolemaic rule there (lost for
  good at Panium in the Fifth War, 200).
- **The native Egyptian question.** [[polybius|Polybius]] famously claimed the arming of
  Egyptians for Raphia gave them a new pride that fuelled resistance to the regime — and
  Upper Egypt did revolt (the Thebaid breaking away under native rebel-kings from 207/6).
  Heinen cautions that the link is "a very one-sided Greek view": the revolt had many
  causes (economic, social, religious-separatist), and the arming of Egyptians is better
  seen as one episode in a long Ptolemaic policy (from Ptolemy I) of binding the natives to
  the dynasty.
- **The Raphia decree** — a trilingual priestly decree (Memphis) presenting Ptolemy IV in
  full Pharaonic guise, smiting the foreigner like Horus avenging Osiris, restoring
  Egyptian idols — the same Graeco-Egyptian double-face the dynasty had always worn (see
  [[ptolemaic-egypt]]), not (Heinen argues) a sudden "Egyptianizing" breakthrough.

## Historiography

Raphia is the first major event of the period for which [[polybius|Polybius]] (Book V) is
the firm source — supplemented uniquely by the demotic/Greek **Raphia decree**, which
gives the Egyptian priestly perspective alongside the Greek narrative. The Seleucid order
of battle that Polybius records for the campaign is the single best description of a
[[hellenistic-monarchy|Hellenistic army]]. See [[syrian-wars]], [[antiochus-iii-the-great]].
