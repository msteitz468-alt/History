## Narrative

On August 29, 1871, Emperor Meiji summoned to the palace the leaders of the four domains most actively involved in both the Restoration and the new government — Chōshū, Satsuma, Hizen, and Tosa — to inform them of a momentous change. The minister of the right, Sanjō Sanetomi, read an imperial rescript declaring it necessary, "in order to protect at home the countless millions of people and to achieve abroad equality with all nations, to extend and unify laws throughout the country and to do away with an institution that by now was significant only in name." That institution was feudalism — the division of Japan into semi-autonomous domains (han) each ruled by a hereditary daimyo.

The proclamation of *haihan chiken* ("abolition of the domains, establishment of prefectures") dismantled the formal political structure inherited from the Tokugawa shogunate in a single day. Later that afternoon the emperor summoned fifty-six domain governors (*chiji*) resident in Tokyo and informed them by proclamation of the change; they prostrated themselves in token of submission. The following day the same message was delivered to representatives of daimyo living in the provinces. On September 1 the minister of foreign affairs, Iwakura Tomomi, reported to the ministers of the various foreign countries that the domains had been abolished and replaced with prefectures.

The transition was so swift that it astonished contemporaries. Acting British minister F.O. Adams, told of the change on September 5, expressed his surprise at the smoothness of what was, in effect, a revolutionary centralization of power. Keene notes that the daimyo, stripped of their feudal authority, were retained in honorific posts as governors of the new prefectures — a calculated face-saving arrangement that dampened resistance. Former domain armies were dissolved and replaced by a nascent national military force.

The immediate consequences were sweeping. Former samurai, who had derived their stipends and status from their domain lords, were effectively cut loose from the feudal order. The government assumed responsibility for paying their stipends, creating a massive fiscal burden. In compensation, samurai were formally given the freedom to pursue any occupation — a change that foreshadowed the deeper transformations of the conscription law of 1873. The 302 domains became seventy-two prefectures, later consolidated further into roughly forty-seven, under officials appointed by and accountable to the central government in Tokyo.

## Causal Analysis

The deeper causes lay in the Meiji leaders' acute awareness that Japan, to resist Western imperialism and negotiate the revision of the unequal treaties, had to function as a unified state. Domain autonomy made national taxation, a national army, and uniform law impossible. The return of domain registers (*hanseki hōkan*) in 1869, in which the major domains voluntarily surrendered their records to the throne, had been a preparatory step; but it had not ended real domain power.

The Iwakura mission's impending departure (December 1871) made the timing urgent: the government wanted to have consolidated central authority before its senior leadership left the country for nearly two years.

Contributed_to: the political will of the Satsuma and Chōshū oligarchs who recognized that reforming Japan required dismantling the very domains that had brought them to power.

## Consequence Analysis

Haihan chiken was the structural prerequisite for all subsequent Meiji reforms. Without a single national government controlling taxation and military recruitment, the conscription law of 1873, the land-tax reform, and the national education system would have been impossible to implement uniformly.

The dissolution also generated the dislocations that fueled samurai discontent: the Saga Rebellion of 1874, in which former Councillor Etō Shimpei led a brief revolt in his home prefecture, was in part an expression of the samurai class's anger at having lost its privileged position. The Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 would represent the final, larger spasm of the same grievance.

The smoothness of the transition became a point of pride in Meiji government narratives. Iwakura Tomomi, in a memorial to the throne on December 31, 1874, included haihan chiken among the "felicitous developments" of the reign, attributing it to the emperor's "ceaseless pondering of the needs of the country."

## Historiography

Keene presents haihan chiken as proceeding "with unbelievable smoothness," emphasizing the absence of resistance from the daimyo. He attributes this partly to the fact that many domain lords were already deeply indebted and welcomed relief from fiscal responsibility. The standard interpretation — confirmed across Cambridge History of Japan volumes — is that haihan chiken was the decisive constitutional act of the Meiji era, more consequential than the Restoration itself in practical terms, because it created the administrative machinery necessary for all that followed.

The question of why the daimyo submitted so readily remains a point of scholarly interest. The combination of face-saving (appointment as governors), financial relief (assumption of stipend obligations by the state), and the implicit threat of the new conscript-backed government's military capacity are the standard explanations.
