# The Hittite Sack of Babylon

The **destruction of Babylon by the [[hittites|Hittite]] king Mursili I** (~1595
BCE, middle chronology), which ended the **[[babylon-first-dynasty|First Dynasty of
Babylon]]** and opened the way for the **[[kassites|Kassites]]**. A raid from far
beyond the horizon, it closes the Old Babylonian period. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Ch. V §VII, Gadd; with the Hittite
side from Chs. VI/XV, Gurney — to be deepened in Section 4).

## Narrative

Babylon under its last king **Samsuditana** had declined to a small realm "spinning
itself out only because there was no neighbour with force enough to cut even so thin
a thread." The thread was cut from Anatolia. **[[hittites|Mursili I]]**, grandson of
Hattusili I, having already destroyed [[iamkhad|Aleppo]] in Syria, marched the whole
length of the Euphrates and fell on Babylon. The Babylonian record is a single
laconic chronicle note — *"in the time of Samsuditana the Hittite came to the land
of Akkad"* — phrased with such reserve that its meaning was once doubted. The
Hittite side confirms a true sack: "prisoners and possessions" were carried back to
Hattusha, including the statues of **Marduk and his consort Sarpanitum**, the gods
of Babylon. Mursili did not stay to rule; he withdrew to Anatolia (where he was soon
assassinated), leaving Babylonia in a vacuum that the [[kassites|Kassites]] filled.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** [[hittites|Hittite]] military reach under Mursili I — the same
  thrust that had just ended the "Great Kingship" of [[iamkhad|Aleppo]].
- **contributed_to (enabling):** the long **structural decay** of the
  [[babylon-first-dynasty|First Dynasty of Babylon]] (loss of the Sealand, Nippur,
  and the Euphrates under Samsuiluna and after), which left Babylon defenceless.
- **concurrent_with:** the broad reshuffling of the Near East ~1600 BCE —
  [[hurrians|Hurrian]]/[[mitanni|Mitannian]] consolidation in the north, the
  expulsion of the Hyksos and the rise of the Egyptian New Kingdom.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** the **end of the First Dynasty of Babylon** and the **[[kassites|
  Kassite]] dynasty**, which would rule Babylonia for ~four centuries and make it a
  Late Bronze "great power." The Kassite king **Agum II** later **ransomed Marduk
  and Sarpanitum** home from "the land of Khani."
- **enabled:** the transition from the Old Babylonian to the
  [[late-bronze-age|Late Bronze]] world.
- **chronological keystone:** the date of this sack is the single most important —
  and most **[[mesopotamian-chronology|disputed]]** — anchor for the absolute
  chronology of the entire second millennium (high ~1651, middle ~1595, low ~1531).

## Historiography

The event survives in **two terse, asymmetric sources** — a Babylonian chronicle
sentence and the Hittite tradition (the *res gestae* of Hattusili I and the deeds of
Mursili I) — supplemented by the inference from **Agum II's** statue-recovery
inscription. The reserve of the Babylonian note and the gap before the Kassite
takeover leave the *mechanism* (raid vs. brief occupation) and above all the *date*
open: it is the textbook case of how a single poorly-dated event can hold an entire
chronological system hostage. See [[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]] and
[[mesopotamian-chronology]].
