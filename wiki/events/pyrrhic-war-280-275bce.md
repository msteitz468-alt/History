# The Pyrrhic War (280–275 BCE)

## Narrative

### Background: The Road to War (282–280 BCE)

Rome's southern Italian expansion triggered the war. In **282 BCE** Rome responded
to an appeal from **Thurii** (a Greek city on the instep of Italy under Lucanian
pressure) by installing a garrison and sending a naval squadron into the Tarentine
Gulf. The Tarentines, who regarded themselves as the protectors of the Italiote
Greeks and who held a formal treaty right to exclude Roman warships from their
gulf, attacked the Roman ships, sank several, and expelled the Thurian garrison.
When a Roman embassy arrived to demand satisfaction, it was insulted and dismissed.
War became inevitable.

Tarentum could not fight Rome alone. The city appealed to **[[pyrrhus-of-epirus|
Pyrrhus of Epirus]]**, king of the Molossians and the greatest military commander of
the Hellenistic world, a kinsman of Alexander and ambitious for a western empire.
Pyrrhus accepted. He crossed to Italy in spring **280 BCE** with an army of some
**20,000 infantry, 3,000 cavalry, 2,000 archers, 500 slingers, and 20 war elephants**
— the first elephants ever seen in Italy.

### Battle of Heraclea (280 BCE)

The Roman consul **P. Valerius Laevinus** advanced against Pyrrhus before his
allies could assemble. At the River Siris near Heraclea, the armies engaged. The
battle was hard fought: Roman tactical persistence repeatedly reformed their
shattered lines. But the elephants — which the Romans had never encountered — broke
the Roman cavalry, and Pyrrhus's disciplined Macedonian phalanx ultimately prevailed.
Roman losses were roughly 7,000; Pyrrhus's were substantial too.

The victory was decisive but costly. After inspecting the Roman dead, Pyrrhus
reportedly said: *"With such soldiers as these I could conquer the world"* — and of
his own losses, in what gave us the phrase "Pyrrhic victory": *"If we win one more
such battle against the Romans, we shall be completely ruined."* (Plutarch,
*Pyrrhus* 21.)

Pyrrhus advanced toward Rome. Several Samnite, Lucanian, and Bruttian communities
submitted. But **Appius Claudius Caecus** — old and blind — had himself carried to the
Senate and delivered the first preserved Latin prose speech against accepting peace,
arguing Rome should negotiate only from victory. The Senate, moved by his words and
by the report of the Roman ambassador **C. Fabricius Luscinus** (who had been unimpressed
by Pyrrhus's attempt to bribe him), refused peace. **Cineas**, Pyrrhus's ambassador,
famously described the Senate to Pyrrhus as *"an assembly of kings"* — recognizing in
the Roman senatorial class an aristocracy as formidable as any in the Hellenistic world.

### Battle of Ausculum (279 BCE)

In 279 Pyrrhus again met the Romans, this time at **Ausculum** in Apulia, on terrain
that partly neutralized the phalanx. Over two days of fighting the Romans actually
forced Pyrrhus's line on the first day; on the second day, on more open ground, Pyrrhus
prevailed again. But his cavalry general Leonnatos was killed, and Pyrrhus himself
was wounded. His losses were again severe. His response to congratulations: *"One more
such victory and I am undone."* The proverb was born.

### Romano-Punic Treaty (279/8 BCE)

After Ausculum, **Carthage** — alarmed that Pyrrhus might cross to Sicily to relieve the
Sicilian Greeks from Punic pressure — sent its admiral **Mago** with an imposing fleet
of 120 ships to the mouth of the Tiber. The visit produced Polybius's **third
Romano-Punic treaty** (see [[romano-punic-treaties]]): both parties affirmed all
previous agreements and added mutual-aid clauses permitting either to assist the other
against Pyrrhus in their respective territories (Italy/Sicily), with Carthage providing
transport ships. The clause was permissive, not mandatory. The great Punic fleet was
designed to impress as much as to offer — and to keep Pyrrhus occupied in Italy rather
than Sicily. See [[philinus-treaty-controversy]] for the disputed question of whether this
treaty also included a secret "spheres of influence" clause.

### Pyrrhus in Sicily (278–275 BCE)

Leaving a garrison at Tarentum, Pyrrhus crossed to Sicily in 278, invited by the
Syracusans and other Greek cities who were hard-pressed by Carthage. With extraordinary
energy he drove the Carthaginians out of most of the island within two years, reducing
their hold to **Lilybaeum** (which resisted siege). But his increasingly ruthless rule
alienated the Sicilians. His execution of **Thoenon** (a Syracusan popular leader) and
his demand for Sicilian troops for a renewed Italian campaign provoked revolt. When
news came that Tarentum was in danger, he abandoned Sicily. His parting words, looking
back at the island: *"What a wrestling-ground we are now leaving for Carthaginians and
Romans to fight in."* (Plutarch, *Pyrrhus* 23.8.)

### Battle of Beneventum (275 BCE)

Back in Italy, Pyrrhus attempted a night attack on the Roman camp near Beneventum under
**M'. Curius Dentatus**. His forces became disordered in the woods; the dawn attack
failed; Curius then sallied out with his full force. When Pyrrhus unleashed his elephants,
the Romans drove them back with firebrands, and the panicking animals trampled their own
infantry. Pyrrhus retreated to Tarentum and shortly after sailed back to Epirus with most
of his army, leaving a garrison. His Italian ambitions were over.

### End of the War (275–272 BCE)

Without Pyrrhus, the remaining Italian holdouts could not resist. Samnium was finally
pacified; Locri, Rhegium, Bruttii, and Lucanians submitted. **Tarentum** surrendered to
the Romans in **272 BCE**, becoming a nominally allied city (retaining some autonomy
while accepting a Roman garrison). In the same year, Pyrrhus — now campaigning in
the Peloponnese — was killed at **Argos** when a roof-tile thrown by an old woman knocked
him from his horse. The era ended.

## Causal Analysis

The war's trigger was **structural**: Rome's expansion toward southern Italy inevitably
threatened the position of Tarentum, which had treaty rights excluding Roman ships from
its gulf and which regarded itself as protector of the Italiote Greeks. The specific
**proximate cause** was the military confrontation of 282 BCE (Roman ships in the gulf,
Tarentine attack, Roman embassy insulted).

Pyrrhus's intervention was enabled by his own availability — recently frustrated in
Macedonian ambitions, he needed a grand project — and by the attractiveness of the
invitation (Sicily + Italy = a western empire to match the Hellenistic kingdoms of
the east). The war was not inevitable from the Roman side; Rome showed no strategic
interest in Sicily before it was thrust upon her by the logic of the war's conclusion.

## Consequence Analysis

The war's consequences were out of proportion to its duration:

**Military.** Rome proved capable of absorbing two battlefield defeats from the finest
Hellenistic army and fighting on. The Romans' tactical resilience, the manipular
flexibility of the legion against the rigid phalanx, and Roman discipline in defeat
were all tested and validated. Rome had no answer to elephants initially; that problem
would recur in the First Punic War and against Hannibal.

**Strategic.** Rome became the undisputed master of peninsular Italy south of the
Po Valley. All the major Greek cities of Magna Graecia entered the Roman alliance.
This gave Rome access to maritime cities and naval allies that would prove essential
for building the fleet against Carthage.

**Diplomatic.** Rome entered the consciousness of the Hellenistic world. Pyrrhus's
description of the Roman Senate as an "assembly of kings," Ptolemy III's recognition
of Rome (alliance of amicitia c. 273 BCE), and the Philinus treaty dispute all attest
to Rome's new Mediterranean visibility.

**Catalytic.** The war directly produced the conditions for the First Punic War:
the Roman-Mamertine alliance in 264 arose from patterns set by the protection of
Thurii in 282; the garrison at Rhegium created obligations; the Mamertine appeal to
Rome had immediate parallels to the Tarentine appeal to Pyrrhus. Pyrrhus's own
prediction on leaving Sicily proved accurate within a decade.

## Historiography

The primary source for the Pyrrhic War is **Plutarch's Life of Pyrrhus** (written
c. 100 CE), which drew on Hieronymus of Cardia, Timaeus, and others; it is
substantially reliable for military events but shaped by biographical conventions.
**Dionysius of Halicarnassus** (*Roman Antiquities*, Books XIX–XX, fragmentary) provides
additional detail from a pro-Roman perspective. The **Ennius fragments** (for the
Roman side) and the Sicilian historian **Timaeus** (hostile to Pyrrhus) are also
relevant.

Scullard (CAH VII.2) and Franke (Ch. 10) treat the war as a turning point in Roman
history rather than merely an episode in Pyrrhus's biography. The debate over the
[[romano-punic-treaties|Romano-Punic treaty of 279/8]] — specifically whether Mago's
visit produced only the formal Polybian terms or also a secret "spheres of influence"
clause — remains open. See [[philinus-treaty-controversy]].

The collection's coverage is good: CAH VII.2 Ch. 10 is the principal treatment;
[[pyrrhus-of-epirus]] also carries material from CAH VII.1 (Will, Ch. 4) on Pyrrhus's
earlier career.
