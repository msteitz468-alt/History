# Hideyoshi's Invasions of Korea (the Imjin War, 1592–1598)

The two great seaborne invasions of Korea launched by [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi
Hideyoshi]] — to the Koreans "the second coming of the *waekan* plague and the biggest *wakō*
raid of all" — the largest overseas military undertaking in pre-modern East Asian history and
the catastrophic foreign coda to [[azuchi-momoyama-period|Japan's unification]]. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v4-early-modern|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 4]] (Asao Naohiro,
ch. 2; Jurgis Elisonas, ch. 6 — "The Inseparable Trinity").

## Narrative

Hideyoshi mobilized an expeditionary army of ~158,000 men in nine divisions; the wider
mobilization "encompassed the entire country," with levies assessed at five men per 100 *koku*
under the new **[[kokudaka-system|kokudaka]]** system. The troops were not mainly samurai —
muster rolls show that no more than half of a contingent were fighting men, the rest farmers,
fishermen, and laborers impressed into transport and construction; there is "ample evidence
of absconding and other forms of passive resistance" by peasants dragged to a war of
aggression.

- **First invasion (1592–93).** The vanguard (heavily officered by **Christian daimyo** of
  western Japan — Konishi Yukinaga, Sō Yoshitoshi, Ōmura, Arima — giving the campaign "the
  air of a bizarre crusade") landed at **Pusan** on 23 May 1592; the city fell the next day.
  Hardened by the wars of unification and organized around companies of musketeers, the
  Japanese drove up the peninsula with "startling speed," taking Seoul and P'yŏngyang and
  reaching the **Tumen River** and a probe into Manchuria. They tried to govern the conquered
  provinces "according to Japanese rules" — cadastral surveys, sword hunts, taxation rolls —
  "as though subjecting yet another Japanese province." But the conquest could not be held:
  **Korean "righteous armies"** (*uibyŏng*, gentry-led guerrillas) rose in every province,
  Admiral **Yi Sun-sin's** navy cut the sea-supply routes, and in the seventh month of 1592 a
  **Ming army** crossed the Yalu at Korea's request. Defeated at P'yŏngyang, the Japanese fell
  back on Seoul; everywhere "Hideyoshi's armies bogged down."
- **The four-year peace negotiations (1593–96).** A truce held while Konishi Yukinaga and the
  Ming envoy Shen Weijing conducted talks — which both deceived their masters to keep alive.
  Hideyoshi's seven demands (a Ming princess for the Japanese emperor, the cession of four
  Korean provinces, hostages, resumed licensed trade) were incompatible with the Chinese
  view of a tributary world. The Ming embassy of 1596 simply **invested Hideyoshi as "King of
  Japan"**; when he grasped that he had been managed into a Chinese diplomatic victory, he flew
  into a rage and ordered a second invasion.
- **Second invasion (1597–98).** Some 140,000 troops returned, now aiming only to seize
  Korea's southern provinces and hostages. Met by joint Chinese–Korean armies, guerrillas,
  and Yi Sun-sin's navy, and unable to supply themselves from a countryside "devastated by
  successive years of warfare," the Japanese were pushed back to the **Pusan coast**. When
  **Hideyoshi died suddenly in the eighth month of 1598**, the [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Council of
  Five Elders]] kept his death secret and arranged the **total withdrawal** of the army. The
  seven-year war ended "without the Toyotomi regime or any of the daimyo gaining any rewards
  or a single inch of territory."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] — the war was his personal project. The volume rejects
  pure "megalomania": "not so much megalomania as ignorance moved the entire enterprise" —
  Hideyoshi invaded with "the most abysmal want of knowledge" of continental conditions, even
  believing Korea was subject to Tsushima.
- `contributed_to:` the dynamic of unification turned outward — a vision of conquering China
  was "the common conceit of the two great unifiers" (first proclaimed by [[oda-nobunaga|Oda
  Nobunaga]] in 1582), an extension of the domestic warfare by which Japan was being subdued.
- `caused_by:` Korea's principled refusal to grant passage or join an assault on its Ming
  suzerain — "comfortable with the international order of East Asia headed by China," Chosŏn
  chose "resistance over dishonor."

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **devastation of Korea**, especially the southern agricultural heartland,
  which "would not recover for several generations"; the abduction of Korean potters and
  artisans; lasting Korean–Japanese enmity.
- `contributed_to:` the **fiscal exhaustion of [[ming-dynasty|Ming China]]** — the huge
  mobilization "exacerbated already-existing financial difficulties and hastened its
  collapse" (the Ming fell in 1644).
- `contributed_to:` the **decline of the Toyotomi regime** — the failure ended the outward
  impetus that had sustained unification and "surely hastened" the fall already in evidence,
  clearing the path for [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] at [[edo-period|Sekigahara (1600)]].
- `produced:` (per Asao) a **new Japanese conception of the state and the world** — confronted
  with a real foreign presence, Hideyoshi cast Japan as the *shinkoku* ("land of the gods,"
  the phrase opening his 1587 anti-Christian edict), distinct from both the Buddhist
  triad of Japan–China–India and the newly arrived Christian world.

## Actors

- [[toyotomi-hideyoshi]] — author of the invasions.
- Chosŏn Korea — Admiral **Yi Sun-sin**, whose navy and "turtle ships" severed Japanese
  supply lines; the gentry-led *uibyŏng* guerrillas.
- [[ming-dynasty|Ming China]] — Korea's suzerain, whose intervention turned the war.

## Historiography

The Cambridge History (Elisonas) frames the war within the "inseparable trinity" of **war,
trade, and piracy** (Goethe) that defined Japan's East Asian relations — the *wakō* raiding
and the Sinocentric tally trade of which the invasion was the violent culmination. Mainstream
postwar Japanese scholarship sees the war's roots partly in Hideyoshi's frustrated demand for
licensed trade with the Ming; the older "megalomaniac" image is qualified by emphasis on
ignorance and the internal logic of unification. The war remains a charged site of memory in
Korea (the *Imjin waeran*) and Japan.
