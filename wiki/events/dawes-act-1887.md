# Dawes Severalty Act

General Allotment Act (1887) authorizing presidential division of reservations into individual holdings and sale of unclaimed “surplus” lands—principal statutory vehicle of [[allotment-policy]] until 1934.

## Narrative

In **1887** President Grover Cleveland signed the General Allotment Act into law. Called the **Dawes Act** for its Senate sponsor, Massachusetts Senator **Henry L. Dawes**, it was—in Hoxie’s framing—the fullest expression of the American government’s commitment to replacing communal with capitalist values. The statute authorized the president to divide any reservation into individual holdings and to negotiate sale of unclaimed “surplus” lands. Between passage in 1887 and repeal in **1934**, this “reform statute” was called upon to dismember most of the reservations in the United States and became the principal vehicle for facilitating non-Indian access to Indian lands.

part_of: [[reservation-period-1880-1960]]; produced: [[allotment-policy]]; followed_by (repeal context): [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]]

Exemptions and parallel statutes: **Indian Territory** was broken up under the **Curtis Act**, not Dawes; **New York State** and arid **Southwest** communities were exempted from Dawes. Northwest interior reserves in Washington/Oregon were brought under allotment with ~3 million acres transferred over ~30 years (Fisher). Canada had **no** general allotment law (contrasts_with Canadian Indian Act individual-title provisions and smaller reserve averages).

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by / part_of: [[us-civilization-program]] — church–government common front teaching private property and Victorian morality as sole alternatives to “traditional” lifeways.
- contributed_to: closed settlement frontier—impossible to remove Natives “beyond” settlement; land hunger therefore combined with civilization campaign (Hoxie).
- preceded_by: 1880s imposed land-sale agreements already transferring 70+ million acres; Wisconsin Ojibwa 80-acre surveys and surplus sales as local prototypes.
- Reformist ideology: potlatch ban (Canada 1884) and Dawes (US 1887) as twin “improvement” statutes that were also assaults on Native autonomy (Fisher end-state comparison).

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: continental [[allotment-policy]] regime.
- **Land loss:** 1930s federal accounting estimated **over 90,000,000 acres (36.4 million hectares)** of Indian-owned land lost under Dawes.
- Second wave: forced fee patents peak **1915–1920** (>20,000 Indians—more than twice all patents since 1887), exposing land to tax sale and speculation.
- *Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock* (1903) enabled disposals without Indian consent by affirming Congressional power to abrogate treaties (related: [[treaty-sovereignty-native-north-america]]).
- Economic subordination: leasing of fertile Crow, Yakima, Papago lands; subsistence-only farming by 1920 for remaining Indian farmers.
- Meriam Report (1928) traced 1920s bleakness to allotment origins → reform climate for IRA.
- contrasts_with / repealed by: [[indian-reorganization-act-1934]] (abolished allotment; incomplete land restoration).

## Historiography

Hoxie (CHNPA I.2 ch. 12) presents Dawes as the statutory core of the reservation-period land assault and quotes the 1930s 90-million-acre estimate as government officials’ final accounting—not a modern re-audit. He carefully distinguishes Curtis Act (Indian Territory) and Dawes exemptions (New York; arid Southwest). Fisher’s Northwest chapter treats Dawes and Canada’s potlatch law as comparative 1880s reformist instruments with equivalent dispossession results. Later Meriam and New Deal literature made allotment the named origin of reservation poverty—policy history written as indictment that justified IRA repeal. Do not collapse “reform intention” into “land-transfer outcome”; Hoxie holds both in frame.
