# The Ardennes Offensive (December 1944 – January 1945)

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]]: Hitler's last western gamble, launched 16 December 1944 under the cover of weather that grounded Allied air power — 200,000 men, 600 tanks, and 1,900 guns against 80,000 defenders, advancing 65 miles toward the Meuse before the fuel ran out. American armor held by Christmas Eve; 5,000 Allied aircraft joined once the skies cleared; Montgomery (over-cautious, in Evans's account) failed to pinch off the salient while Patton counter-attacked from the south. The supporting air strike of 1 January 1945 destroyed as many German planes as Allied (~280 each), and the Alsace subsidiary attack failed. On 17 December 1944 SS panzer troops murdered American POWs at **Malmedy**. Each side lost ~700 armored vehicles and ~70,000–80,000 men — losses the Americans replaced and the Germans could not. Hitler pulled back the main force on 3 January and returned to Berlin by train on the 15th, telling his adjutant: "I know the war is lost . . . We'll not capitulate. Never. We can go down. But we'll take a world with us."

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Hitler briefed his senior Western-front generals at Ziegenberg on the evening of 12 December 1944 only after having them stripped of side arms and briefcases and driven in circles to disorient them en route — they learned the offensive would launch in four days only on arrival. Rundstedt, as Commander in Chief West, called the plan a shock: "Hitler had not troubled to consult me... it was obvious to me that the available forces were far too small"; with Model he proposed a scaled-down alternative limited to pinching off the Aachen salient, which Hitler rejected. For the offensive Hitler assembled some 28 divisions (nine panzer) plus six more for a planned follow-on Alsace attack, collecting nearly 1,500 new or rebuilt tanks and assault guns in November and another 1,000 in December; Göring promised 3,000 fighter planes. When Guderian (responsible for the Eastern Front) protested the diversion of reserves, Hitler shouted him down: "There's no need for you to try to teach me. I've been commanding the German Army in the field for five years... I'm more in the picture than you are!" and dismissed Soviet build-up figures as "the greatest bluff since Genghis Khan!" General Manteuffel's description of Hitler at the 12 December briefing: "a stooped figure with a pale and puffy face, hunched in his chair, his hands trembling, his left arm subject to a violent twitching... When he walked he dragged one leg behind him."

The offensive opened the night of 15–16 December on a 70-mile front between Monschau and Echternach; German meteorologists had correctly predicted the fog and cloud cover that grounded Allied air power for five days. A German armored spearhead reached Stavelot on the night of 17 December, within a mile of a 3-million-gallon American fuel dump at Spa that was never captured — a contributing factor in the fuel shortage that stalled the advance. McAuliffe's one-word "NUTS!" answered a German surrender demand at Bastogne on 22 December. Manteuffel: "On the evening of the twenty-fourth it was clear that the high-water mark of our operation had been reached." The turning point was the failed German 2nd Panzer reconnaissance battalion at Dinant, three miles from the Meuse, running out of fuel and reinforcements as US 2nd Armored struck from the north. At the 28 December war conference, over Rundstedt and Manteuffel's advice to withdraw, Hitler ordered the offensive resumed and a new Alsace offensive launched, in an extended monologue that included his refusal-of-capitulation passage: "I have never learned to know the word 'capitulation'... nothing will make the slightest change in my decision to fight on till at last the scales tip to our side."

By 16 January 1945, when German forces were back to their starting line, final losses stood at roughly 120,000 German killed, wounded, or missing, 600 tanks and assault guns, 1,600 planes, and 6,000 vehicles, against American losses of 8,000 killed, 48,000 wounded, 21,000 captured or missing, and 733 tanks and tank destroyers. Shirer: "the Americans could make good their losses; the Germans could not... This was the last major offensive of the German Army in World War II." [SHIRER-THESIS] He further argues the failure "not only made defeat inevitable in the West, it doomed the German armies in the East" by exhausting the reserves that might otherwise have met the coming Soviet Vistula offensive.

On the Malmédy massacre and its 1946 Dachau trial: Nuremberg evidence initially cited 129 American POWs killed by Col. Jochen Peiper's 1st SS Panzer Division combat group near Malmédy on 17 December 1944; the subsequent trial reduced the verified figure to "at least seventy-one." The 1946 Dachau trial condemned 43 SS officers to death (including Peiper), 23 to life imprisonment, and 8 to lesser terms (Sepp Dietrich 25 years, Kraemer 10, Priess 18); Senator McCarthy alleged coerced confessions, General Clay commuted 31 death sentences in 1948 (later reduced from 12 to 6), and McCloy commuted the remaining death sentences to life in January 1951 — "at time of writing all have been released" (Shirer, 1960). Operation Greif, Skorzeny's brigade of roughly 2,000 English-speaking Germans in US uniforms and captured vehicles, was "Hitler's brain child"; captured plans discovered 16 December caused widespread US paranoia (soldiers as far back as Paris were quizzed on World Series winners and state capitals on rumors Skorzeny's men were targeting Eisenhower). Several captured infiltrators were summarily shot; others were court-martialed and executed. Skorzeny himself was acquitted by an American tribunal at Dachau in 1947.

## Historiography

Evans's account follows the post-archival consensus: the offensive was strategically hopeless from conception (his generals said so at the 3 November 1944 briefing), and its chief effect was to strip the eastern front of reserves before the January Soviet offensive; the "what if Antwerp fell" counterfactual finds little scholarly support.
