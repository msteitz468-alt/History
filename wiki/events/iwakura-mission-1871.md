## Narrative

On December 23, 1871, approximately fifty senior officials, specialists, and students (including young women) departed Japan under the leadership of Iwakura Tomomi, the minister of foreign affairs, bound for the United States and then Europe. The mission visited twelve countries over twenty-one months, returning to Tokyo on September 13, 1873.

The official justification for the mission was the approaching window for treaty revision. The treaties of commerce signed with Western powers in 1858 contained a provision permitting their terms to come up for reconsideration; July 1, 1872, was the earliest date at which renegotiation could begin. The unequal treaties had imposed extraterritoriality (placing resident foreigners outside Japanese legal jurisdiction) and fixed import tariffs that deprived Japan of revenue and commercial autonomy. Both provisions were widely regarded as humiliations incompatible with national sovereignty.

The idea for the mission had been proposed in February 1871 by [[ito-hirobumi]], then in Washington, who wrote to senior officials proposing that "a mission of outstanding officials be dispatched in the near future to Europe and America to examine in each country the situation with respect to friendly relations, trade, and customs duties." He hoped the mission would convince Western nations that Japan had matured sufficiently to deserve equal treatment. In May 1871 Councillor Ōkuma Shigenobu and Yoshida Kiyonari examined the proposal and recommended it to the cabinet. The American missionary educator Guido Verbeck also claimed credit as an organizer, producing documentary evidence that a proposal he had submitted years earlier was adopted almost verbatim.

### The American Interlude and Treaty Revision Fiasco

The mission arrived in San Francisco in January 1872 and proceeded to Washington. There, an improvised attempt at treaty revision proved embarrassing: the credentials Iwakura and his colleagues carried did not include authorization to negotiate treaty revision. Ōkubo Toshimichi and Itō Hirobumi had to make the long journey back to Tokyo to obtain proper credentials — a round trip that consumed months. When they returned, Iwakura concluded that unilaterally concluding a revised treaty with the United States would be disadvantageous under the most-favored-nation clauses: any concession to one country would have to be extended to all others without necessarily receiving equivalent concessions in return. He broke off the treaty-revision negotiations and reverted to the mission's original purpose: courtesy calls and observation. The mission spent six and a half months in America before proceeding to Europe.

### European Observations

The mission then proceeded to Britain, France, Germany, and other European countries. The members were observing intensely: Kume Kunitake, the mission's official chronicler, produced a five-volume account (*Beiōkairan jikki* — "A True Account of the Tour of America and Europe") documenting what they saw in government, law, industry, education, and social life. This account has been described by Keene as "extraordinary." The mission members were witnessing the Western nations "at a time of prosperity and optimism" — the aftermath of the Franco-Prussian War had established a new European order under unified Germany, and the industrial economies of Britain and France were at their peak.

The observation of the Franco-Prussian War had already shifted Japanese military thinking before the mission's European leg: Japanese observers who went to France during the 1870–71 war had been "deeply impressed by the strength and battle tactics of the Prussian military." Until then Japan had modeled its army on France; after France's defeat, Germany became the model. The mission reinforced this shift.

### Domestic Policy in the Mission's Absence

Because so many of the senior oligarchs were abroad, the government left behind a "caretaker" administration under Sanjō Sanetomi. The caretakers were supposed to avoid major new initiatives. In practice, however, 1872–73 saw the implementation of the compulsory education ordinance, the promulgation of the conscription law, the adoption of the solar calendar, and an escalating crisis over Korea — all while the mission's senior leadership was away.

The Korean crisis came to a head in autumn 1873. Saigō Takamori proposed to send himself as an envoy to Korea, expecting to be killed, which would provide a *casus belli*. The Court Council approved the plan in his absence. Iwakura returned on September 13, 1873, and within weeks had organized opposition to Saigō's plan, eventually persuading the emperor to support his position. On October 24 the emperor endorsed Iwakura's memorandum opposing the Korean expedition. Saigō and four other councillors resigned. Iwakura's return from the mission was thus the pivotal moment in a major policy crisis.

### Assessment

Contemporaries and later historians divided sharply over whether the mission succeeded or failed. Those who focused on treaty revision saw failure: no treaties were revised, and the frustrating wait in Washington wasted months. The philosopher Miyake Setsurei said that once the mission left the United States, "its activities degenerated into an aimless wandering." Others, notably W.E. Griffis, described it as one of the most remarkable events of the era.

Keene's assessment is strongly positive: "the Iwakura mission was a stunning success, and the fruits of the long voyages would be shared by the emperor and the entire Japanese people." The mission gave its members a quality of firsthand knowledge of the West that no other means could have supplied. Whether in advanced machinery, political institutions, etiquette at a European reception, or the organization of universities and armies, the mission provided experiential grounding for the reform program that followed.

## Historiography

The dominant controversy concerns whether treaty revision was the mission's "prime objective." Keene notes that "a question that has divided specialists is whether treaty revision was the mission's prime objective." Those who say yes emphasize the cost, duration, and the disappointment on return. Those who say no point to the original memoranda, which listed observation and relationship-building as primary. Keene comes down clearly: treaty revision was "ardently desired" but was not the original objective, and the mission's failure to achieve it does not constitute overall failure.

The role of Guido Verbeck as the mission's "originator and organizer" — claimed by W.E. Griffis in 1900 — is a secondary historiographical controversy. Iwakura confirmed to Verbeck that his earlier proposal had been adopted; but the claim inflates Verbeck's role in a process driven by Japanese political analysis.
