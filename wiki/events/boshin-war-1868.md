# Boshin War (1868–1869)

The civil war between the new imperial government and Tokugawa loyalists that followed the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]] proclamation of January 4, 1868. Its name derives from the Japanese year-cycle name *Boshin* (戊辰). The war opened at Toba-Fushimi south of Kyōto and ended with the surrender of the Ezo Republic at Hakodate in June 1869. It was the decisive military confirmation of the Restoration.

## Narrative

### Background: The Restoration Proclamation and Its Rejection

The formal proclamation of the Restoration (January 4, 1868) abolished the shogunate and ordered Tokugawa Yoshinobu to surrender his rank and lands. The response inside Nijō Castle was fury: "soldiers loyal to the shogunate surrounded them and screamed insults, calling them lackeys of 'Satsuma bandits' and traitors" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]). Yoshinobu himself moved to Ōsaka Castle, declaring on January 17 that he would not be bound by the proclamation.

The outbreak of fighting was precipitated by deliberate Satsuma provocation. Saigō Takamori directed rōnin to commit arson and looting around Edo; the crucial incident came January 18 when the outworks of Edo Castle burned and Satsuma rōnin attacked the Shōnai domain office. Shogunate troops retaliated by surrounding and burning the Satsuma residence in Edo. Word reached Ōsaka Castle just as Yoshinobu had drafted a letter accepting the court's demands — the news swept him away, and he ordered his forces to march on Kyōto.

### Toba-Fushimi (January 27–30, 1868)

On January 27, 1868, Aizu and Kuwana domain forces advancing on Kyōto via the Toba and Fushimi roads were met by Satsuma imperial troops. "The Satsuma troops' reply was a burst of cannon and rifle fire" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]). Despite outnumbering the imperial forces roughly three to one and including French-trained units, the shogunate army collapsed — partly from a bolting horse that panicked the advance column, but above all from the psychological effect of the imperial brocade pennant (*nishiki no mihata*), which bestowed legitimacy on the Satsuma forces and made shogunate soldiers hesitate to fire on the emperor's authorized defenders.

Yoshinobu slipped out of Ōsaka Castle and sailed for Edo on the warship *Kaiyō maru*, abandoning his army. He later explained: "once the brocade pennant appeared, he lost all desire to fight." The imperial forces entered Ōsaka Castle on February 2. The Toba-Fushimi defeat gave the imperial government control of western and southern Japan.

### The March on Edo (February–May 1868)

The court dispatched Prince Arisugawa Taruhito as supreme commander (March 1, 1868) with a brocade pennant and detailed codes of conduct for the army prohibiting harm to civilians, shrines, and foreigners — designed to demonstrate to Western powers that the imperial army was not a band of ruffians.

Yoshinobu in Edo Castle vacillated between resistance and submission. He dismissed the hardline Oguri Tadamasa, entered seclusion at the Kan'ei-ji in Ueno, and sent intercessions to Matsudaira Yoshinaga and Yamauchi Yōdō. His surrender was finally negotiated between Saigō Takamori and Tokugawa councillor Katsu Kaishū, with British minister Sir Harry Parkes urging leniency — advising that "severity toward Keiki [Yoshinobu] or his supporters, especially in the way of personal punishment, would injure the reputation of the new government in the opinion of European Powers." On May 4, 1868, Edo Castle was delivered to the imperial army without a fight.

### Resistance in Edo: the Shōgitai (June 1868)

After the castle's surrender, the Shōgitai — a Tokugawa loyalist force based at the Kan'ei-ji in Ueno, which used Prince Rinnōjinomiya (Yoshihisa) as a figurehead — refused to disband. On June 6, 1868, imperial forces attacked at dawn. The outnumbered Shōgitai was defeated; Satsuma troops took the temple's Black Gate by early afternoon. Rinnōjinomiya escaped in disguise, eventually boarding Enomoto Takeaki's fleet and sailing north.

### The Northern Alliance (May–November 1868)

A league of northeastern domains (the *Ōuetsu Reppan Dōmei*) formed in May 1868, signing an oath of allegiance on June 22. Rinnōjinomiya became its symbolic leader in August, establishing what amounted to a rival court at Shiroishi Castle. One source records a claim that the nengō was changed in the north to "Taisei" and Rinnōjinomiya enthroned as the "emperor Tōbu" — though the historicity of this is uncertain ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]). Imperial forces gradually reduced the league, and by October 22, 1868, Rinnōjinomiya wrote his apology and the alliance collapsed.

### The Ezo Republic and Hakodate (1868–1869)

Five months after Edo Castle's surrender, Enomoto Takeaki escaped with eight warships and sailed for Ezo (Hokkaidō), establishing himself at the Five Point Fortress near Hakodate and overwhelming local defenders. On January 14, 1869, Enomoto sent a message through the British and French ministers asking permission to develop the northern region; Iwakura Tomomi refused, declaring he "would not be able to escape the name of traitor." Enomoto then declared an "Ezo republic," which received conditional recognition from British and French naval squadrons at Hakodate. The republic fell on June 27, 1869, when Enomoto surrendered to imperial forces under Kuroda Kiyotaka — the last and longest of the Boshin War's battles.

## Causal Analysis

The war's proximate cause was the political-military collision set in motion by the Restoration proclamation, but its structural causes lay in the prior decade of Bakumatsu crisis. The Satsuma-Chōshū alliance, forged in 1866-1867, provided the military core of the imperial forces. The alignment of foreign powers — Britain backing the court, France the shogunate — played an indirect role: the British minister Parkes's pragmatic acceptance of the new government, and his advice against harsh treatment of Yoshinobu, helped achieve the bloodless surrender of Edo.

The brocade pennant was a decisive psychological weapon. It transformed what might have been a straightforward military confrontation between shogunate and rebel domains into a contest in which the shogunate forces were cast as enemies of the emperor himself — a category the military culture of Japan made nearly impossible to inhabit with full conviction. Yoshinobu's own statement captures this: he did not want to fight "the court," only Satsuma.

## Consequence Analysis

The war resolved in eighteen months what might have required years of guerrilla conflict. Its relative brevity owed much to: the speed of the Toba-Fushimi victory; Yoshinobu's personal decision not to fight; the negotiated surrender of Edo; and the pragmatism of the new government in offering Yoshinobu's life. The war confirmed that the [[meiji-restoration-1868]] was not simply a palace coup but a genuine transfer of military-political power. Its conclusion enabled the new government to move quickly to foundational reforms — the abolition of domains (1871), the conscript army, and ultimately the [[meiji-constitution-1889]].

## Actors

- **[[emperor-meiji]]**: Symbolic supreme commander; reviewed troops, observed the fleet at Ōsaka, received foreign ministers. His public role was unprecedented for a modern emperor.
- **[[tokugawa-yoshinobu]]**: The last shogun; yielded power voluntarily, then was drawn into hostilities; entered seclusion after Toba-Fushimi.
- **[[tokugawa-shogunate]]**: Lost. Its 265-year reign ended militarily with the fall of Edo Castle.
- **Saigō Takamori**: Real military commander of the imperial forces; deliberately provoked the war and negotiated Edo's bloodless surrender.
- **Ōkubo Toshimichi**: Political architect of the Satsuma strategy; later a dominant figure in the Meiji government.
- **Katsu Kaishū**: Tokugawa councillor who negotiated the surrender of Edo Castle with Saigō.
- **[[iwakura-tomomi]]**: Court noble who organized the Restoration coalition; refused Enomoto's overtures.
- **Enomoto Takeaki**: Commanded the Tokugawa naval fleet; established the Ezo Republic; eventually pardoned and became a Meiji official.

## Historiography

The war is well documented through contemporary diaries (Ōkubo, Kido), foreign diplomatic memoirs (Satow's *A Diplomat in Japan*), and official records. Keene's *Emperor of Japan* (2002) draws heavily on Satow and on the *Meiji Tennō ki* (the official record). The war's brevity and relatively low casualties (especially Edo's bloodless fall) have led some historians to stress its "revolution from above" character — a top-level political deal rather than mass mobilization. Others emphasize the genuine military danger, especially the Hakodate campaign. The Ezo Republic's brief recognition by foreign squadrons raises unresolved questions about how close Enomoto came to establishing a viable rival state.

The role of Satsuma provocation in starting the war — Saigō's deliberate arson campaign around Edo — is now widely accepted by Japanese historians but was suppressed in Meiji-era accounts that preferred a narrative of loyalist resistance to shogunate aggression.
