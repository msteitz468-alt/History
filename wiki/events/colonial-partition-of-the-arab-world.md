# The Colonial Partition of the Arab World (1830–1923)

The carving-up of the Arab lands by the European empires — from the French conquest of Algiers
(1830) to the post–First World War **mandates** that drew the borders of the modern Middle East.
The episode that, in Eugene Rogan's [[rogan-the-arabs|*The Arabs*]], left the Arabs "a community
of nations rather than a national community" and seeded a century of grievance.

## The partition of North Africa (1830–1912)

The first wave fell on **North Africa**, as European interests overrode the old commitment to
Ottoman territorial integrity: **France** took **Algeria** (from 1830 — 132 years of rule),
**Tunisia** (1881), and (with Spain) a protectorate over **Morocco** (1912); **Britain**
occupied **Egypt** (1882); **Italy** seized **Libya** (1911). By 1914 the whole of North Africa
had passed under direct European rule. Rogan notes the role of **precedent** (Algeria set the
pattern) and of Great-Power horse-trading (Tunisia "offered" to France at the Congress of Berlin,
1878, to balance Britain's acquisition of Cyprus).

## The First World War and the three contradictory promises

The decisive partition came in the **First World War**, when Britain made **three incompatible
commitments** for the disposition of the Ottoman Arab lands:

1. **The Husayn–McMahon Correspondence (1915–16).** In return for an **Arab Revolt** against the
   Ottomans, the British High Commissioner Sir Henry McMahon promised **Sharif Husayn of Mecca**
   British recognition of an **independent Arab kingdom** across most of the Arab east (with
   vague exclusions for French interests in coastal Syria and British claims in Iraq).
2. **The Sykes–Picot Agreement (1916).** Secretly, Britain and France (with Russian assent)
   agreed to **partition the same lands between themselves** — a British zone (Baghdad, Basra,
   and a corridor to the Mediterranean), a French zone (Cilicia and coastal Syria), and an
   international zone in Palestine.
3. **The Balfour Declaration (1917).** Britain pledged support for **"a national home for the
   Jewish people" in Palestine** — a third claim on territory already promised twice over.

## The Arab Revolt and the betrayal

The **Arab Revolt** (from June 1916), led by Husayn's son **Amir Faysal** (with T. E.
Lawrence), took Mecca, harried the Hijaz Railway, seized Aqaba (1917), and rode into **Damascus
(October 1918)** alongside Allenby's army as Ottoman rule in the Arab lands collapsed "402 years
after Selim the Grim." But the revolt "divided local loyalties" (many tribes stayed loyal to the
Ottomans), and the wartime promises to the Arabs were **not honoured**. At the **San Remo
conference (1920)** and under **League of Nations mandates**, the lands were divided as
Sykes–Picot had foreshadowed: **France** took mandates over **Syria and Lebanon** (expelling
Faysal from Damascus, 1920); **Britain** took **Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` European imperial expansion and the erosion of Ottoman authority over the Arab
  provinces.
- `caused_by:` the **First World War** and Britain's contradictory wartime diplomacy — promising
  the same lands to the Arabs, the French, and the Zionists.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **mandate states** and the **modern Middle East borders** — Syria, Lebanon,
  Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan — drawn by imperial convenience, not Arab consent.
- `produced:` deep and lasting **grievance**: the **Iraqi Revolt (1920)** against broken
  promises (crushed by Britain), and the conviction that the colonially-drawn states were
  illegitimate — frustrating [[arab-nationalism|pan-Arab unity]].
- `produced:` the conditions for the **[[arab-israeli-conflict|Arab–Israeli conflict]]** (the
  Balfour pledge in Palestine).
- `enabled:` the [[cold-war|Cold War-era]] and later foreign domination of the region — the next
  links in Rogan's chain of Arabs living "by other peoples' rules."

## Significance

The partition is the formative trauma of the modern Arab world: it ended four centuries of
[[ottoman-empire|Ottoman]] rule only to substitute European empire, fixed the borders and
states the Arabs still inhabit, and bred the nationalism — and the sense of betrayal — that
shaped the century that followed.
