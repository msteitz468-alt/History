# The Opening of Japan (Perry and the Unequal Treaties, 1853–1858)

The forced ending of Japan's two-century [[sakoku|seclusion]] by Western naval and
diplomatic pressure — from **Commodore Matthew Perry's** "black ships" (1853) to the
**Ansei commercial ("unequal") treaties** of 1858 — the external shock that destroyed the
[[shogunate-bakufu|Tokugawa bakufu's]] legitimacy and set in train the
[[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]]. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 5]]
(W. G. Beasley, ch. 4; Marius B. Jansen, Introduction & chs 1, 5).

## Narrative

- **The Perry expedition (1853–54).** Beasley stresses that — unlike China — Japan was
  **opened not by Britain but by the United States**: the Japan trade interested Britain far
  less than Canton (Stamford Raffles's 1813–14 overtures had been judged unprofitable and
  abandoned), leaving the field to an America driven by Pacific whaling, the China route, and
  the new steamship. Commodore Perry arrived at Uraga in July 1853 with a squadron of steam
  warships, delivered President Fillmore's letter, and withdrew, promising to return for an
  answer. The shaken senior councillor **Abe Masahiro** broke precedent by **circulating
  Perry's demands among the daimyo and the court** — an admission of weakness that began the
  unravelling of bakufu authority. Perry returned in 1854 and secured the **Convention of
  Kanagawa**: the opening of Shimoda and Hakodate to provisioning, consular residence, and
  most-favored-nation treatment — a wedge, not yet commerce.
- **The Harris commercial treaty (1858).** The American consul **Townsend Harris**, resident
  at Shimoda from 1856, pressed for a full **China-type commercial treaty**, lecturing the
  bakufu that the steamship had made the world "like one family," that Britain would impose
  worse terms by force, and that Japan should "enter the family of nations without violence
  or dishonor" by treating with "a single individual, unattended" rather than waiting for
  "fifty men-of-war." Senior councillor **Hotta Masayoshi** agreed in principle but —
  fatefully — sought **imperial sanction** at Kyoto and was refused, exposing a fatal split
  between bakufu and court. The regent **Ii Naosuke** signed the **Harris Treaty** without
  imperial approval in 1858; parallel treaties with the Netherlands, Russia, Britain, and
  France followed (the **Ansei treaties**).
- **The treaty-port system (from 1859).** The ports — above all **Yokohama** — opened to
  foreign residence and trade in 1859.

## The "unequal" terms

The Ansei treaties imposed the classic semi-colonial package: **extraterritoriality**
(foreigners tried under their own consular law), a **fixed low tariff** removed from Japanese
control (tariff autonomy lost), and the **most-favored-nation clause**. Beasley notes the
bakufu negotiators saw the earlier Dutch (Nagasaki) arrangement as a milder modification of
old practice; Harris's treaty went much further, imposing genuine free trade on terms Japan
could not revise — making **treaty revision** the central Japanese diplomatic goal for the
next forty years (achieved only in 1894; tariff autonomy in 1911).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Western (US) commercial-strategic expansion across the Pacific and the
  demonstrative power of the industrial West in the **Opium War and the humbling of China** —
  which convinced an informed Japanese elite that resistance risked China's fate.
- `caused_by:` the obsolescence of [[sakoku|seclusion]]: the bakufu's 1825 "drive off
  foreign ships" edict and its 1844 dismissal of the Dutch king's warning showed (Jansen) a
  system "coming to the end of its resources," unable to respond "with imagination or
  effectiveness."

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **collapse of bakufu legitimacy** — Abe's appeal to the daimyo and court,
  and Ii Naosuke's signing **without imperial sanction**, shattered the fiction of shogunal
  authority and gave the rallying cry ***sonnō jōi*** ("revere the emperor, expel the
  barbarian") to the loyalists of the western *tozama* domains.
- `produced:` economic dislocation — the gold/silver exchange discrepancy **drained Japan of
  precious metal**, and the trade unleashed a "wild inflation" that ruined fixed-income
  [[samurai]] and squeezed artisans; bakufu attempts to monopolize the trade's profits
  angered powerful vassals.
- `contributed_to:` the [[meiji-restoration-1868|Meiji Restoration]] — Mason & Caiger call
  the opening "the most proximate cause"; the failure of the pre-industrial seclusion order
  before the industrial West forced the regime change that remade Japan.

## Historiography

Beasley's chapter (drawing on his own *Great Britain and the Opening of Japan*) stresses the
**American**, not British, agency in Japan's opening and the relative restraint of the terms
compared with China's. Jansen sets the episode within the long *bakumatsu* decline — the
Western shock made the Tokugawa endgame "more extreme than earlier" *bakumatsu*, but the
dynasty was already "spiraling downward." The older nationalist memory of Perry's "black
ships" as the sudden trauma of modern Japan is qualified by emphasis on internal causes
([[tokugawa-economy|commercialization]], fiscal crisis, intellectual ferment).
