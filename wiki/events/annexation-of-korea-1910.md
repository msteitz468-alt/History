## Narrative

The annexation of Korea in August 1910 formalized Japan's control over the Korean peninsula,
ending the Korean Empire (Daehan Jeguk) and incorporating Korea as a Japanese colonial
possession governed by a governor-general in Seoul. The formal treaty — the Japan–Korea
Annexation Treaty — was signed on August 22 and proclaimed on August 29, 1910.

The event falls within the last two years of Emperor Meiji's reign. Keene's account of the
period leading into 1910–1911 situates the annexation as the completion of a process set in
motion by Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) and the subsequent Protectorate
treaty of 1905, which had already stripped Korea of its foreign policy independence. By 1910
Japan had progressively dismantled the structures of Korean sovereignty: the 1907 Hague mission
by Korean Emperor Gojong to contest the protectorate led to his forced abdication; the Korean
army was disbanded; and Japanese advisers assumed control of internal administration.

Keene records that by 1911 the former Korean crown prince, Yi Eun, was being educated in Japan
under Japanese court supervision. Emperor Meiji on July 14, 1912 — just weeks before his own
death — sent a palace officer to Prince Yi Eun with a message "praising his diligence in his
studies and urging him to keep up the good work while on summer vacation," a detail that captures
the studied paternalism of Japanese colonial policy toward the Korean royal house. At the Meiji
funeral cortege on September 13, 1912, Yi Kang — the elder son of the former Korean emperor —
marched in the official procession alongside Japanese princes of the blood.

Keene's treatment of the period also notes earlier Japanese violence in Korea: he records that
"Queen Min of Korea had been killed by Japanese ruffians," a formulation that tacitly acknowledges
state-adjacent Japanese culpability in the 1895 assassination of the Korean queen.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[russo-japanese-war-1904]]` — Japan's decisive victory over Russia removed
  the only great-power counterweight to Japanese hegemony in Korea and produced the
  Portsmouth Treaty (1905), which recognized Japan's "paramount interests" in Korea.
- `caused_by: [[korea-protectorate-1905]]` — The Eulsa Treaty (1905) established the
  protectorate and set up the Residency-General; annexation in 1910 was its logical completion.
- `caused_by: [[japanese-imperialism-meiji]]` — The broader process of Meiji-era imperial
  expansion, grounded in the Meiji state's strategic logic of creating a buffer zone and
  controlling Korea to prevent it from being used as a base by hostile powers.
- `contributed_to: [[sino-japanese-war-1894]]` — Korea had been the original battleground of
  Sino-Japanese rivalry; the 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki formally ended Qing suzerainty over
  Korea, clearing the path for Japanese domination.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[japanese-colonial-korea]]` — Thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule
  (1910–1945), marked by cultural suppression, forced labor mobilization, and eventual
  dissolution of Korean independence.
- `produced: [[korean-independence-movement]]` — The annexation galvanized Korean nationalist
  resistance, including the March 1st Movement (1919) and the establishment of the Korean
  Provisional Government in Shanghai.
- `enabled: [[japanese-empire-expansion-1930s]]` — Korea became an industrial and agricultural
  base for subsequent Japanese expansion into Manchuria and China.
- `concurrent_with: [[high-treason-incident-1910]]` — In the same year, the Japanese state
  suppressed domestic anarchism, illustrating the dual face of Meiji-era power at its apex.

## Actors

- **[[emperor-meiji]]**: In whose name the annexation was effected; the Meiji state's imperial
  expansion culminated in this act in the final years of his reign.
- **[[katsura-taro]]**: Prime minister during the annexation; signed the treaty.
- **[[korean-empire]]**: The state dissolved by the treaty.
- **[[yamagata-aritomo]]**: Genrō who had long advocated Japanese hegemony over Korea as a
  strategic necessity.

## Historiography

The annexation is among the most contested events in modern East Asian historiography. Japanese
nationalist accounts have at times framed the annexation as modernization and protection; Korean
historiography universally treats it as an illegal act of colonial aggression. The treaty's
legitimacy has been questioned on grounds of coercion — the Korean prime minister Yi Wan-yong
signed under duress. Keene's biography does not treat the annexation in detail, mentioning it
primarily as geopolitical context; the entry above draws primarily on contextual framing within
the chapters covering 1910–1912.

The collection's Korea holdings are weak; this entry reflects what Keene's account provides.
Dedicated sources on the Korean Empire, the protectorate period, and the independence movement
are absent and represent a gap to fill.
