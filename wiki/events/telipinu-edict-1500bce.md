# The Edict of Telipinu

The **constitutional decree** issued by the [[hittites|Hittite]] king **Telipinu**
(~1500 BCE) to end the cycle of royal murder that had wracked the Old Kingdom — the
ancient Near East's clearest attempt to **bind the monarchy by law**, and the single
most important written source for early Hittite history. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v2p1-1973|CAH Vol. II, Part 1]] (Chs. VI & XV, Gurney).

## Narrative

Telipinu came to the throne by **deposing (not killing)** his brother-in-law the
usurper Huzziya, whom he merely banished — a pointed break with precedent. His
edict opens with a long **historical preamble** contrasting the orderly, victorious
reigns of the early kings (**Labarna, [[hattusili-i|Hattusili I]],
[[mursili-i|Mursili I]]**), when "the royal family was united," with the **anarchy**
that followed Mursili's assassination, when usurpers butchered their predecessors'
families and the kingdom's provinces fell away. Drawing the moral from this history,
Telipinu then legislates.

## What the edict established

- **A fixed law of succession.** "Let a prince of the first rank become king; if
  there is no prince of the first rank, one of the second rank; if there is no
  prince at all, let them take a husband for the first-rank daughter, and let him
  become king." This **abolished the king's free choice of heir** and the recurrent
  resort to adoption, substituting a clear hereditary order.
- **The pankuš as a court.** The **pankuš** — the "whole body" of fighting men — is
  charged to judge wrongdoers and, crucially, to **sit in judgement on the king
  himself** if he sheds the blood of brother or sister: "you are his pankuš."
  Telipinu illustrates the principle with the case of two earlier royal murders, in
  which he insists the **instigator**, however high his rank, must suffer in person
  rather than escape behind a hired "man of straw."

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** the **half-century of regicide** (Hantili–Zidanta–Ammuna–Huzziya)
  unleashed by the assassination of **[[mursili-i|Mursili I]]**, which had no
  stable rule of succession to contain it.
- **contributed_to (enabling):** the **loss of provinces** (Cilicia/Adaniya,
  Arzawa) and the external pressure of the [[hurrians|Hurrians]] and the Kaska,
  which made internal order an urgent survival question.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** a **fixed succession law** and a recognized judicial role for the
  pankuš — a brief stabilization of the late Old Kingdom (Telipinu also secured his
  flank by treaty with Kizzuwadna's king Išputahšu).
- **enabled:** the survival of the Hittite state into the Middle Kingdom and,
  ultimately, the Empire — though the pankuš is **never heard of again** after this
  reign, suggesting the order owed more to Telipinu's personality than to the reform.

## Historiography

The edict is a **double-edged source**: it is the indispensable framework for
Old-Kingdom chronology and the genealogy of the early kings, **yet it is a
partisan, retrospective document** written some five generations after Labarna,
possibly constructing history out of the state archives rather than recording a
living tradition. Scholars debate whether the **pankuš** was a real constitutional
check (an Indo-European "tribal assembly," compared to Germanic institutions) or
merely the passive audience of royal pronouncements, and how far Telipinu's
succession law **codified existing custom** versus **innovating**. The dynastic
disturbances it records have also been read as a struggle between **matrilineal
(Hattian)** and **patrilineal (Indo-European)** succession. See
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
