# Sack of Alexandria (1365)

In October 1365 **[[peter-i-of-cyprus|Peter I of Cyprus]]** led a predominantly Cypriot
fleet — reinforced by Hospitallers and a limited western contingent — in a crusading
assault that **sacked Mamluk Alexandria**. Edbury calls it "the most notable blow struck
by a Christian army against the Mamluk sultanate at any point in its history," and with
hindsight the final major Christian assault on the Mamluks.

## Narrative

Destination was announced only after sailing from Rhodes on 4 October 1365 (secrecy against
Muslim preparation and European merchants). The fleet arrived off Alexandria on 9 October;
on 10 October forces disembarked, routed landing opposition, entered by the custom-house
gate, and unleashed massacre and plunder. Two of three landward gates were destroyed
(an-Nuwairī: without which Christians might have held the city). Main Mamluk forces were
in Cairo. Peter, with Mézières and Peter Thomas, wanted to hold Alexandria; westerners and
(per Mézières) Hospitallers and Peter's brothers insisted on withdrawal. On **16 October**,
as Mamluk troops entered from Cairo, the Christians left for Cyprus.

### Force composition (Edbury's argument)

| Source | Fleet / force notes |
|---|---|
| Mézières | ~100 ships total; ~60 from Cyprus under prince of Antioch; ~10,000 armed men under Peter |
| Makhairas | 165 ships at Rhodes, **108 from Cyprus**; 16 Venetian galleys for Peter/westerners; 4 Hospitaller |
| Amadi | 92 Cypriot ships (33 galleys) of 165 |

Despite western recruiting efforts, **the force was mainly Cypriot vassals and men-at-arms
plus foreigners already in the East**.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Peter I's crusade initiative after Hugh IV; papal proclamation under French
  then (after John II's death) Cypriot leadership
- `enabled:` Cypriot naval capacity; Rhodes rendezvous; Hospitaller contingent
- Edbury's interpretation: Holy Land language was western propaganda; real aim was commercial
  — hold Alexandria or extract privileges for Cypriot middlemen against Venice and Genoa

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Mamluk–Cypriot war to 1370; no retaliatory invasion (Mamluk aversion to naval war)
- `contributed_to:` western merchant hostility; fiscal overstretch; noble discontent →
  Peter's murder (1369)
- `preceded_by:` [[fall-of-acre-1291]] (Cyprus as sole outpost framing later crusading)
- With hindsight, 1360s Egypt–Syria campaigns diverted attention from the Aegean as Ottomans
  consolidated in Europe

## Historiography

Fleet and manpower figures differ sharply among Mézières, Makhairas, Amadi, and Arab notices.
Edbury privileges the composition argument over absolute numbers. Setton's *Papacy and the
Levant* supplies the wider diplomatic frame.
