# The Treaty of Paris (1783)

The settlement that ended the **[[american-revolution|Revolutionary War]]** and recognized the
**independence of the United States**. After [[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]] the British "gave up
all hope of restoring royal authority"; Lord North's government fell, and the new ministry opened
negotiations.

## Narrative

**Benjamin Franklin**, the chief American negotiator, "was … pushing at a half-open door": the Earl of
**Shelburne** offered **territorial concessions the Americans had never realistically expected** —
hoping to **drive a wedge between the United States and France** and rebuild a transatlantic relationship.
Britain's improved position after Yorktown (Rodney's victory at the **Saintes**, the relief of
**Gibraltar**) firmed its hand on other fronts: France made Caribbean gains but failed in Asia; Spain
recovered Florida and Minorca but not Gibraltar; for the Dutch the war "had been an unmitigated disaster."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **defeat at Yorktown** and the collapse of British political will.
- `contributed_to:` Shelburne's strategy of **generosity to split the Franco-American alliance**.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` formal **American independence** with expansive boundaries to the Mississippi.
- `enabled:` the postwar **fiscal-military crisis** — Britain retained frontier forts and the
  Confederation could not enforce the treaty — that fed the movement toward the
  **Constitution** (see [[fiscal-military-state]]).

## Historiography

The negotiating correspondence of Franklin, Adams, Jay, and Shelburne is extensively preserved and studied, making the treaty's diplomatic history unusually well documented; historians (Conway among them) debate how much of Shelburne's generosity toward American boundaries reflected genuine strategic calculation to split the United States from France versus domestic British political weakness that left him needing a quick peace. The treaty's near-total exclusion of Native American interests, despite Britain nominally ceding all territory to the Mississippi, is increasingly treated in more recent, ethnohistorically informed scholarship as a foundational injustice rather than a footnote, since none of the affected nations were party to the settlement — a theme this wiki also treats via the [[treaty-of-new-york-1790|Treaty of New York (1790)]]. The treaty's generous boundaries are also debated as a cause of the young Republic's subsequent fiscal-military weakness, since Congress could promise little to enforce them.
