# English East India Company Mission to Japan (1613–1623)

The arrival of Captain John Saris aboard the *Clove* at Hirado in 1613 inaugurated a brief
English trading presence in Japan. Despite a generous commercial licence from [[tokugawa-ieyasu|
Ieyasu]], structural disadvantages — smaller capital than the Dutch, inability to compete in the
Moluccas spice trade, and the post-Ieyasu hostility of [[tokugawa-shogunate|Hidetada's]] bakufu
toward Europeans — led the English to abandon their Japan factory voluntarily in 1623.
Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Background

When English merchants in London heard that Ieyasu had sent a letter inviting Dutch merchants to
Japan, they sought the same opportunity. [[adams-william|William Adams]] had sent letters home
describing conditions in Japan, and the English East India Company — founded in 1600, capitalized
at only £70,000 compared to the Dutch VOC's £600,000 — ordered John Saris to Japan.

---

## The Mission

In 1613 the *Clove* arrived at Hirado, where the Dutch factory had already been established
(through the persistent hospitality of lord Matsuura Shigenobu, who gave both nations warm
welcomes). Adams went to Hirado to greet Saris, who was "rather offended by his refusal to stay
in his house, for he preferred to put up with the Bugyo Zenzaburo." Saris suspected Adams of
favoring Dutch and Japanese interests.

Adams escorted Saris to Sumpu, where Saris was presented to Ieyasu, and then to Edo for an
audience with Hidetada. Adams also took him to his own estate at Uraga.

**The royal letter.** King James I's letter (summarized phonetically in Japanese records as from
"King Zemeshi") presented Saris as "Captain General Juan Saris" sent to bear greetings and
establish trade. Ieyasu's reply was warm: "though separated by myriads of miles of sea and sky
we may in this way well be said to become like near neighbors."

**The seven-article licence.** Ieyasu issued a trading patent allowing English ships to trade in
all commodities at any Japanese port, land goods freely, take shelter in any harbor in distress,
build houses in Edo and reside there, take home property of English who died in Japan, sell
without coercion, and manage their own internal discipline. This was exceptionally generous — more
so than the terms eventually conceded to the Dutch under sakoku.

**Saris chooses Hirado.** Despite Ieyasu's invitation to establish headquarters at Edo, Saris
chose Hirado — partly to escape what he saw as Adams's undue influence. Sadler and Murdoch both
consider this a strategic error; at Edo there would have been no Dutch competition and the Company
might have been better protected after Ieyasu's death. At Hirado, Saris found Matsuura (called
"Old King Foyne" by the English) chronically in debt and always borrowing, while the Dutch
outcompeted the English at every turn.

---

## Decline and Withdrawal

After Ieyasu's death in 1616, "things were not as they were in the old man's days." Hidetada
regarded European religion and trade together as threats to Japan and the Tokugawa house, and
restricted trade to Hirado. The English could not afford their share of the 1619 Anglo-Dutch
Treaty of Defense (an agreement to raid Portuguese and Spanish ships that brought £100,000 in
plunder from expeditions against Manila). In 1622 the English and Dutch fell out over this.

In **1623** the Amboyna massacre occurred: the Dutch tortured and executed an English captain,
nine Englishmen, nine Japanese, and one Portuguese on charges of plotting to seize the Dutch
garrison. This forced the English to abandon the Moluccas entirely and concentrate on India.

The English left Hirado the same year, voluntarily, leaving buildings behind and the port several
thousand pounds out of pocket. As Sadler notes, they "left voluntarily, and were not subjected to
any of the humiliating conditions which attended the conserving of their trading privileges by the
Dutch in Deshima. That was something." They also left a permanent trace: **the potato**, still
known in Japan as *Jagatara-imo* (Jacatra potato, from the Dutch name for Batavia/Jakarta), or
the Satsuma sweet variety.

**Adams's assessment.** Richard Cocks's diary provides the primary running account of the English
decade at Hirado. Adams never used his shogunal access to enrich himself through the Company
despite Saris's suspicions; his continued trusted status with Ieyasu was the Company's principal
diplomatic asset. Adams died in 1620, predating the English departure.

## Historiography

The English decade in Japan is documented primarily through Richard Cocks's Hirado diary, a major
source for Tokugawa foreign-relations history. Sadler also draws on James Murdoch's characterization
of Saris. The episode is often read as a case study in the costs of insufficient capitalization
and poor on-the-ground judgment; more recent scholarship has also emphasized structural constraints —
the VOC's spice-trade monopoly made Dutch competition in the Moluccas inescapable.
