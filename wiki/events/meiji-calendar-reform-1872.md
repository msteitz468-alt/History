## Narrative

On December 10, 1872 (by the old lunar calendar), Emperor Meiji performed a ceremony of changing the calendar preparatory to adopting the solar (*Gregorian*) calendar in place of the traditional lunar calendar. He announced that the third day of the twelfth month of Meiji 5 would become January 1, 1873 — thus skipping several weeks in the transition. The emperor reported the change to the spirits of his ancestors and handed to Prime Minister Sanjō Sanetomi a rescript explaining the rationale.

The official justification was accuracy. The lunar calendar required the insertion of an intercalary month every two or three years to keep pace with the solar year. The solar calendar, requiring only one extra day every four years, "would not err by even a single day for 7,000 years." The emperor stressed the superior precision of the new system.

However, Keene notes that the emperor "did not mention in this rescript what may have been the chief reason" for the change: government salary payments. Since the ninth month of 1872, Japanese governmental offices had been paying salaries monthly. Under the lunar calendar, a year with an intercalary month would require thirteen salary payments — "obviously undesirable to the government."

The transition aligned Japan temporally with the major Western nations: "in most respects, Japan was now living within the same temporal frame as the advanced nations of the West." Some Japanese "felt that they had lost a valuable segment of their lives when the day following the third of the twelfth month became January 1," but the new calendar was nonetheless adopted in official life. The lunar calendar persisted for years in religious ceremonies and private custom.

The calendar change coincided symbolically with a cluster of other reforms: the prohibition of selling human beings (November 1872), the abolition of Buddhist ceremonies in the palace, and the promulgation of the conscription law (January 1873). Together these measures mark a deliberate effort to remake Japanese public life in alignment with international norms.

## Historiography

The fiscal motivation behind the calendar change — concealed in the official rescript — is characteristic of the Meiji government's presentation of practical administrative reforms in ideological terms (modernization, accuracy, alignment with international standards). Keene's observation about the salary motive is not contested but does not appear in official accounts of the reform.
