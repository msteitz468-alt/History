# Pruth River Campaign

## Narrative
The **Pruth River Campaign** of July 1711 was a military engagement in Moldavia that resulted in the encirclement and near-destruction of the Russian army under Peter the Great by the Ottoman forces (L2892).

Instigated by France and the exiled Charles XII of Sweden, Turkey declared war on Russia in 1710 (L2892). Peter I acted rashly, marching into Moldavia with an inadequate force, relying heavily on the promised but unfulfilled support of the Christian subjects of the Sultan (including Moldavia, Wallachia, Serbia, and Montenegro) (L2892). 

In July 1711, the Russian army found itself cut off from supplies and completely surrounded by a vastly superior Turkish force near the Pruth River (L2892). Facing certain capture, Peter I signed a peace treaty to extricate his army (L2892). Under the terms of the settlement, Russia surrendered Azov, destroyed its newly built southern fleet, promised not to intervene in Polish affairs, and guaranteed Charles XII safe passage back to Sweden (L2892).

## Causal Analysis
The campaign was caused by:
* **Swedish and French Lobbying:** Charles XII, residing in Turkey after Poltava, worked with French diplomats to convince the Sultan that Russia's rise threatened Ottoman interests (L2892).
* **Peter I's Miscalculation:** The Tsar underestimated the Ottoman military capacity and acted on the mistaken belief that Balkan Christians would rise in rebellion to join his army (L2892).

## Consequence Analysis
The Pruth River Campaign produced:
* **Southern Setback:** The treaty reversed all of Russia's gains from the 1700 Treaty of Constantinople, dismantling Peter I's Azov fleet and delaying Russian access to the Black Sea for sixty years (L2848, L2892, L3412).
* **Northern Focus:** By paying the heavy price of abandoning his southern ambitions, Peter I extricated his army and preserved his ability to bring the Great Northern War to a victorious conclusion on the Baltic (L2892).
* **Historiographical Debate:** Historians continue to debate why the Ottoman Grand Vizier accepted a relatively mild peace treaty instead of destroying the surrounded Russian army, with explanations ranging from Ottoman weariness and military losses to Russian bribery and diplomatic skill (L2892).
