# Fall of Tula (Tollán)

## Narrative

The **fall of Tula** (Tollán) ends the first major post-Teotihuacan macroregional state in
central Mexico and opens the Early Aztec period of city-state competition from which the
[[aztec-empire|Aztec]] Triple Alliance later emerged. Archaeological [[tula-hidalgo|Tula]]
in Hidalgo — the Tollan of many sixteenth-century sources, long confused with
[[teotihuacan|Teotihuacan]] until Jiménez Moreno and Acosta — had flourished as a denser
city of roughly **12–13 km²** and perhaps ~**60,000** people in the Tollan ceramic phase
(traditionally ~950–1150/1200 CE; Cowgill 2000 prefers ~**800–1000** on recent Basin dates
and Tohil Plumbate). Its civic quality was below Teotihuacan’s, and the state’s integrated
territory was never very extensive, but its role in later Nahua charter myths was enormous.

Thomas H. Charlton (CHNPA II.1 Ch. 11) frames Central Mexico between the fall of Tula
(c. A.D. 1150) and the Spanish conquest of Tenochtitlan (1521) as complex, dynamic cultural
evolution at a state level. The major defining characteristics of the period include
**breakup of the Tula macroregional state** that had incorporated much of Central Mexico and
**devolution of Toltec political and economic structure into small separate city-state
units**, followed by the third and final prehistoric macroregional system — the Aztecs.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` no single documented conquest narrative is archaeologically secure.

`contributed_to:` **climatic and frontier pressure.** Charlton (following Armillas)
includes continued southward movement of northern frontier agriculturalists facing climatic
deterioration, begun before Tula’s fall and possibly associated with it.

`contributed_to:` **limited state wealth and environmental harshness.** Cowgill notes the
Tula region’s harsh environment (possible soil degradation) and that the state was never very
wealthy or extensive; other important centers coexisted in central Mexico.

`preceded_by:` **northwest migration into highlands** as Teotihuacan declined
(Coyotlatelco/Prado–Corral ceramic affinities; possible Nahua entry) — structural background
to the Toltec polity, not the proximate sack (Cowgill 2000).

`concurrent_with:` **migrations of peoples once inside Toltec systems**, possibly starting
in Tula’s last decades and continuing to c. 1250 — both causes and results of upheaval
(Charlton).

**Archaeological destruction evidence.** Decline is poorly understood. Residential
excavations show trash dumping in abandoned rooms, shrine offerings torn out, and
dismembered adults and children consistent with **massacre** (Mandeville & Healan cautious;
Cowgill finds the massacre case strong). This is local violent evidence, not a full
political narrative of who destroyed the city.

**Dating as process, not a day.** Documentary data suggest fall by A.D. 1170 (Brundage;
Davies; Diehl). Archaeology neither confirms nor rejects. Cobean & Mastache: no conclusive
evidence when/how the Early Postclassic city collapsed; use A.D. **1150–1200**. Diehl argues
collapse by A.D. **1100**. Charlton accepts historically derived **A.D. 1150** while
recognizing the fall probably occurred within the last fifty years of the twelfth century,
and treats 1150/1200 as end of Tula and start of Early Aztec. **“The fall of Tula, although
useful as a starting point, was a process more than an event”** (Charlton).

Cowgill’s preferred **earlier** Tollan-phase peak (~800–1000) does not by itself fix the end
date; Stage-2 integration keeps the conventional c. 1150 slug while recording Cowgill’s
chronology as an attributed tension on floruit, not a silent overwrite of Charlton’s end
date.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` **power vacuum and city-state devolution.** After c. 1150, power vacuum in
Central Mexico with population movements and new small political centers/city-states (some
developments may predate Tula’s end). From ~1350, incorporation into larger confederacies
and tributary empires (Tepaneca, Acolhua); from ~1428, Triple Alliance expansion under
Tenochtitlan, Tlacopan, and Texcoco.

`produced:` **migration traditions as charter history.** Carrasco: “It is the downfall of
Tollan that starts the migration movements which were the basic historical traditions of the
people in all political units of central Mexico at the time of the Spanish conquest.”
Narratives have factual bases but by the early sixteenth century also maintained ethnic
identities and elite genealogies — not precise objective histories (Charlton).

`produced:` **demographic and ceramic reordering.** Period 1 (1150/1200–1250) = first half
of Bray’s “period of wandering”: major demographic shift possibly depopulating/underpopulating
northern CMSR; multi-ethnic communities in the southern two-thirds; foundations for renewed
regionalism. Archaeology does not clearly reflect a century of turmoil: ceramic lag, possible
unrecognized continuities, or real northern depopulation; alternative reading centers
population gravity on [[cholula]] and Aztec I/II geography (Charlton).

`analogous_to:` **epi-Teotihuacan fragmentation.** Events after Tula’s fall mirror the Early
Toltec / Epi-Teotihuacan period (A.D. 650/750–900/950): after Teotihuacan’s end, population
movements and small independent units; those units were probably structurally and
functionally equivalent to city-states Spaniards met in 1519 (Bray 1972; Charlton). Epi-
Teotihuacan ended with Tula’s rise; post-Tula fragmentation ended with Aztec expansion.

`enabled:` secondary-state continuum Teotihuacan → Tula → Tenochtitlan (Charlton): once
Teotihuacan existed, the state was a given; post-Teotihuacan processes (warfare, tributary
state formation strongest outside but adjacent to the Basin) underwrote successive rise of
Tula then Tenochtitlan — not identical to processes that first produced Teotihuacan.

## Actors

- **Primary:** [[toltec]] state centered at [[tula-hidalgo|Tula]] — macroregional system
  whose breakup defines the event.
- **Secondary / structural:** northern frontier agriculturalists under climatic stress;
  peoples relocating from within former Toltec systems; later city-state dynasts who claimed
  Tollan legitimacy (Mexica and others).
- Immediate military destroyers remain archaeologically anonymous; Aztec later excavations
  at Tula were meaningful reclamation of charter objects, not the fall itself (Cowgill 2000).

## Historiography

**Sources.** Cowgill, “From the Rise of Teotihuacan to the Decline of Tula,” CHNPA II.1
Ch. 6 (city scale, chronology of florescence, massacre evidence, Aztec digging, Chichén
links, polysemy of Tollan/Toltec). Charlton, “The Aztec period of Central Mexico,” CHNPA II.1
Ch. 11 (end date debate, process-not-event framing, migrations, city-state devolution,
parallels to epi-Teotihuacan, path to Triple Alliance).

**Dating debate (open).**

| Scholar / tradition | End date claim |
|---|---|
| Charlton (working date) | c. **1150** (within last 50 years of 12th c.) |
| Cobean & Mastache | **1150–1200**; no conclusive how/when |
| Diehl | collapse by **1100** |
| Documentary (Brundage, Davies, Diehl) | by **1170** |
| Cowgill on florescence | Tollan phase more likely **~800–1000** than traditional 950–1150/1200 |

**Toltec Horizon / empire scale.** Diehl argues a merchant-spread Toltec Horizon; Calnek —
physical evidence for distant imperial control not obvious; Weaver — limited integrated
territory, extended influence; Charlton notes Aztec empire appears equally “ephemeral”
archaeologically (Umberger & Klein 1993).

**Method.** Native histories both enable and hinder reconstruction; correlation with ceramic
complexes is difficult and uneven. Charlton presents historical reconstructions and
archaeology as different valid portrayals; lack of congruence is as important as coherence.
