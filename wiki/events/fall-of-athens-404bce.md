# The Fall of Athens (the Ionian War), 413–404 BCE

## Narrative

The **Ionian (or Decelean) War** was the final phase of the
[[peloponnesian-war|Peloponnesian War]] (413–404 BCE), in which a Persian-funded Sparta broke
Athenian sea-power and forced the city's surrender. [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 11,
A. Andrewes) follows Thucydides (Book VIII, breaking off in 411) and thereafter Xenophon and
Diodorus. After the **[[sicilian-expedition|Sicilian disaster]]** (413) and the Spartan
fortification of **Decelea** in Attica (which strangled the countryside, costing Athens more
than 20,000 deserting slaves), the empire began to **revolt** — Chios, Miletus, Lesbos — and
**Persia re-entered Greek affairs**.

## Persia, the satraps, and the fleet

The war turned on **Persian money**. The satraps **Tissaphernes** (Sardis) and
**Pharnabazus** (Dascyleum), ordered by **Darius II** to recover the tribute of the Asian
Greeks, financed a Peloponnesian fleet through a series of **treaties** with Sparta — Sparta
conceding the Asian Greeks to the King. **[[alcibiades]]**, having fled Sparta for the court
of Tissaphernes, intrigued to keep the two sides balanced and to engineer his own return to
Athens.

## The oligarchic revolution and the recovery

Alcibiades' promise of Persian gold helped trigger the **oligarchic revolution of 411**: the
**Four Hundred** seized power at Athens (Antiphon, Phrynichus, Theramenes), but the fleet at
**Samos** — led by Thrasybulus and Thrasyllus — **remained democratic** and recalled
Alcibiades. The narrow Four Hundred fell within months, replaced by the broader **Five
Thousand** and then the restored democracy. The Athenian fleet, with Alcibiades, won a string
of victories in the Hellespont — **Cynossema**, and the brilliant **Cyzicus** (410), where
Mindarus was killed — reopening the Black Sea **grain route**; a Spartan peace offer was
rejected (Cleophon). **Alcibiades returned in triumph to Athens in 407**.

## Lysander, Arginusae, and Aegospotami

The balance shifted again when the King's son **Cyrus the Younger** arrived to back Sparta
without reserve and found a ruthless partner in the admiral **[[lysander]]**. Lysander's
victory at **Notium** (406) discredited Alcibiades, who withdrew into exile for good. The
Athenians won a costly last victory at **Arginusae** (406) — but then, in a fit of democratic
**hysteria**, condemned and executed six of their victorious generals for failing to rescue
the shipwrecked (Socrates alone resisting the illegal vote). In **405**, Lysander caught the
Athenian fleet beached and unmanned at **Aegospotami** in the Hellespont and **destroyed it
at a stroke**, executing the prisoners.

## The surrender

With the fleet gone, the grain route cut, and the Peloponnesian army under **Pausanias** and
**Agis** before the walls, **Athens was besieged and starved**. After protracted negotiation
(Theramenes the envoy), it surrendered in **404 BCE**. Against the demand of **Corinth and
Thebes** that the city be destroyed, Sparta refused "to enslave a city which had rendered such
service to Greece in her hour of danger." The terms: the **Long Walls and Piraeus
fortifications dismantled**, the fleet surrendered (all but twelve ships), exiles recalled,
and Athens bound "to have the same friends and enemies as the Spartans." Lysander sailed into
the Piraeus and the **walls came down to the music of flute-girls** — a day, Xenophon wrote,
men "thought the beginning of freedom for the Greeks."

## Consequence Analysis

Decelea, the revolt of the allies, **Persian gold**, and Athenian **internal dissension**
(Thucydides' verdict). produced: the **end of the Athenian empire** and of the radical
democracy (the **Thirty Tyrants** imposed by Sparta); a brief, resented **Spartan
hegemony**; and the exhaustion of the [[city-state|polis]] system. A transition page in the
wiki's framework — the close of the Athenian fifth century.

## Historiography

Thucydides' history breaks off in 411, so the last seven years rest on **Xenophon's
Hellenica** and **Diodorus** (from Ephorus and the *Hellenica Oxyrhynchia*), which often
disagree. Thucydides' epilogue (ii.65) insists Athens fell not to enemy strength but to its
own **internal quarrels** and the distrust of Alcibiades — a judgement Andrewes finds
powerful but "surely incomplete," neglecting the missed opportunities for peace. See
[[classical-greek-historiography]].
