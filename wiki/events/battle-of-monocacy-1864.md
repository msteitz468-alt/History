# Battle of Monocacy (9 July 1864)

Delaying action on the Monocacy River near Frederick, Maryland, in which Union forces under Lew Wallace fought [[actors/early-jubal-a|Jubal Early]]'s raiding column. Ellsworth (*Midnight on the Potomac*, 2025) stresses the battle's strategic payoff: a tactical loss that bought hours and days for [[places/washington-dc|Washington]]'s reinforcement.

## Narrative

As Early marched on the capital, Wallace improvised a blocking force at the Monocacy crossings. Outnumbered Union troops were pushed aside but imposed delay. Early reached the northern approaches of Washington only after veteran VI Corps elements and other reinforcements had stiffened the works; he declined a full assault and withdrew.

## Causal Analysis

- **part_of:** [[events/early-raid-on-washington-1864]]

## Historiography

Often called "the battle that saved Washington" in public history; military historians debate how close Early truly came to a decisive coup de main versus a demonstration raid. Ellsworth's popular narrative leans toward high contingency.


## Ellsworth Detail (*Midnight on the Potomac*, 2025)

Wallace (Eighth Corps/Middle Department) moved to Monocacy Junction night of 5 July after telegraphs west of Frederick died. On **9 July** his largely "hundred-day" Ohio troops plus veterans fought behind rail fences; the 61st Georgia fell from 150 to 52 men; 114th New Jersey lost 12 of 15 officers. Union flank turned; Wallace withdrew toward Baltimore—"The road to Washington now lay open," but he had "bought some time, some precious time." Capital fortifications (~53 forts, 700+ guns) were undermanned and neglected; by 8 July Rebels were <40 miles away.
