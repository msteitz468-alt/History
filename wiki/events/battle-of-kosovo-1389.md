# The Battle of Kosovo (1389)

At **Kosovo Polje** ("the Field of Blackbirds") the **[[ottoman-empire|Ottoman]]** army of **Murad
I** met a Serbian-led Christian coalition under **Prince Lazar** — the iconic battle of the Ottoman
conquest of the Balkans. [[jones-ncmh-v6-2000|NCMH VI]] (I. Metin Kunt, Ch. 22; and the Balkans
chapters) sets it at the heart of the Ottoman advance under Murad I and Bayezid I.

## Narrative

By 1389 the Ottomans, having crossed into Europe (1354) and established their capital at
**Edirne**, were subjugating the fragmented Christian Balkans. The **Serbian empire** built by
Stefan Dušan had dissolved after his death (1355) into rival principalities; Prince **Lazar**
assembled a coalition to halt the Ottoman advance. The two armies clashed at Kosovo on 15 June 1389.
Both rulers died — **Murad I** assassinated (by legend, by the Serbian knight Miloš Obilić) and
**Lazar** captured and executed — and the battle was tactically indecisive or a costly draw; but its
strategic result was Ottoman ascendancy. Murad's son **Bayezid I** secured the succession on the
field and pressed on with the conquest.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **Ottoman conquest of the Balkans** under Murad I.
- `contributed_to:` the **fragmentation of Serbia** after the collapse of Dušan's empire, which
  left the Balkan Christians divided against the rising Ottoman power.

## Consequence Analysis

Kosovo confirmed **Ottoman dominance** in the Balkans: Serbia became an Ottoman vassal (and
contributed troops to Bayezid's later campaigns), and the road lay open to the subjugation of the
rest of the peninsula and the encirclement of **[[byzantine-empire|Constantinople]]**. Beyond its
military result, the battle became the **central myth of Serbian national identity** — the
"Kosovo legend" of heroic sacrifice and martyrdom for the faith, transmitted in epic poetry and
still potent into the modern era. It stands with **Nicopolis (1396)**, the crushed western crusade,
as a marker of the Ottoman ascendancy that only Timur's blow at Ankara (1402) briefly interrupted.
