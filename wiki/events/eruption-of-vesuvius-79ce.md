# The Eruption of Vesuvius, 79 CE

The catastrophic eruption of **Mount Vesuvius** in **79 CE**, which buried the Roman towns of
**Pompeii**, **Herculaneum** and **Stabiae** on the Bay of Naples under metres of ash and pyroclastic
flow, killing thousands — and, by sealing the towns, preserving them as the most complete surviving
record of Roman daily life.

## Narrative

In the reign of **Titus**, Vesuvius erupted (traditionally 24 August, though some now argue October on
the evidence of autumn fruit and a coin). A towering eruption column — likened by the eyewitness to an
**umbrella pine** — rained pumice on Pompeii, collapsing roofs; successive **pyroclastic surges** then
killed those who remained and engulfed Herculaneum. The admiral and encyclopedist **Pliny the Elder**,
commanding the fleet at Misenum, sailed across both to observe the phenomenon and to rescue people, and
**died** on the shore at Stabiae, overcome by fumes.

## The Source

The event is known in detail from two letters of his nephew **[[pliny-the-younger|Pliny the Younger]]**
to **[[tacitus|Tacitus]]** (*Letters* 6.16 and 6.20), now ingested as part of the
**[[pliny-the-younger-letters|Letters]]** — the **only surviving eyewitness account** of the
disaster, describing his uncle's death and his own flight from Misenum. Volcanologists honour Pliny's
precise description by naming such explosive eruptions **"Plinian."**

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: the geological eruption of Vesuvius. produced: the obliteration of three towns and the death
of Pliny the Elder and thousands of others; and — the great historical paradox — the **preservation**
of Pompeii and Herculaneum (buildings, frescoes, graffiti, food, bodies) as an unparalleled
archaeological window onto Roman life. enabled: the **rediscovery** and excavation of the sites from
the eighteenth century, which transformed European knowledge of antiquity and fed Neoclassicism.

## Historiography

The eruption is unusually well documented for an ancient event, yet the evidence
is lopsided. The **only surviving eyewitness narrative** is the pair of letters
[[pliny-the-younger|Pliny the Younger]] wrote to Tacitus a generation later
(*Letters* 6.16, 6.20) — retrospective, literary, and focused on his uncle's
death; no contemporaneous account survives. The long-standing **24 August** date
rests on manuscripts of Pliny that vary, and later finds (autumn fruit, a coin,
a charcoal graffito) have led many scholars to favour an **October** eruption,
though the question is unresolved. Volcanology reconstructs the event's phases
(Plinian column, then pyroclastic surges) from the deposits themselves, making
the disaster a type-site — hence "Plinian" eruptions. For historians of Roman
daily life the deeper significance is the paradox of preservation: the towns are
known in extraordinary material detail precisely because they were destroyed,
which makes them exceptionally rich but also **untypical** — a single moment in
two Campanian towns, not a cross-section of the empire. The
[[ancient-graffiti|graffiti corpus]] (*CIL* IV) shares this survival bias.
