# Year of the Five Emperors

## Narrative

The year 193 CE saw five men hold or claim the Roman imperial title within twelve months, following the assassination of Commodus on 31 December 192. The Praetorian Guard first installed the elderly senator Pertinax, whose attempts at fiscal discipline and discipline of the guard itself led to his murder within three months. The Praetorians then notoriously auctioned the throne to the wealthy senator Didius Julianus, an act that scandalized the provincial legions and shattered the legitimacy of the Rome-based succession process. Three provincial army commanders — Septimius Severus in Pannonia, Pescennius Niger in Syria, and Clodius Albinus in Britain — were simultaneously acclaimed emperor by their own troops. Severus moved fastest, marching on Rome, disbanding the Praetorian Guard, and having Julianus executed by the Senate in June 193, before turning east to defeat and kill Niger (194) and finally, after a temporary alliance, Albinus at the Battle of Lugdunum (197).

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** the assassination of Commodus, which ended the Antonine dynasty's succession and left no agreed mechanism for choosing a successor; the Praetorian Guard's overreach in selling the throne, which discredited the capital-based succession process in the eyes of the frontier armies.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced:** the Severan dynasty (193–235), which restructured the army's pay and privileges and increasingly relied on provincial and military elites; a durable precedent — repeated throughout the later Crisis of the Third Century — that legitimate rule flowed from army acclamation rather than senatorial or hereditary sanction.

## Historiography

The principal narrative sources are Cassius Dio (a contemporary senator and eyewitness to much of the crisis) and the later, less reliable Historia Augusta; Dio's account, despite his own senatorial biases against the Severan military reforms, remains the backbone of modern reconstructions. Historians debate how much the year's chaos reflected structural weaknesses in Antonine succession practice versus a genuinely contingent breakdown triggered by Commodus's erratic rule.
