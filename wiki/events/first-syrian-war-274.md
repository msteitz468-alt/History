# First Syrian War (274–271 BCE)

The first of a series of recurring wars — collectively the Syrian Wars — fought between the Ptolemaic and Seleucid dynasties over control of the borderlands between their kingdoms, chiefly Coele-Syria (the Levantine corridor) and coastal Anatolia, following the division of Alexander's empire among his successors.

## Narrative

Sources are fragmentary (chiefly incidental references in later writers, since no connected contemporary narrative survives), but the war is understood to have pitted [[actors/antiochus-i-soter|Antiochus I Soter]] of the Seleucid Empire against [[actors/ptolemy-ii-philadelphus|Ptolemy II Philadelphus]] of Egypt. Ptolemaic naval and land forces appear to have held the advantage, consolidating and extending Ptolemaic holdings along the Phoenician coast and into parts of Anatolia and the Aegean, exploiting the Seleucid Empire's simultaneous preoccupation with defending its eastern and northern frontiers.

## Causal Analysis

The war was `contributed_to` by unresolved territorial claims left over from the Wars of the Diadochi (the succession struggles among Alexander's generals), which had never definitively settled control of Coele-Syria between the two dynasties, and by Ptolemy II's active naval expansion, which the Seleucids saw as encroachment.

## Consequence Analysis

The war `produced` confirmed Ptolemaic dominance in the region for a generation and `enabled` the recurring cycle of Syrian Wars — a second, third, and [[events/fourth-syrian-war-219|fourth]] war followed across the third century BCE as the underlying territorial dispute remained unresolved.

## Historiography

The First Syrian War is among the least well-documented of the Hellenistic successor conflicts; no surviving connected ancient narrative treats it directly, and modern reconstructions rely on scattered references, inscriptions, and papyri. It is generally treated by modern historians as part of the broader, better-attested [[events/syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]] sequence rather than analyzed in isolation.
