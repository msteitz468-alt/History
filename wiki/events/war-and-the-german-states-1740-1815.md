## Narrative

The period 1740–1815 constitutes a single coherent era of near-continuous warfare that fundamentally remade the political landscape of Central Europe. It encompasses four distinct but interconnected phases: the Wars of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748), the Seven Years' War (1756–1763), the French Revolutionary Wars (1792–1802), and the Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815). The Oxford Handbook treats this era under the rubric "War made the state, and the state made war" — a formulation drawing on Charles Tilly — as the foundational process of German state-building and incipient nationalism.

### Phase 1: The Silesian Wars (1740–1763)

The conflict began when [[frederick-the-great]] invaded the Habsburg province of Silesia in December 1740, exploiting Austria's political vulnerability after the death of Emperor Charles VI and the contested succession of Maria Theresia. The invasion was embedded in a wider context of Anglo-French colonial rivalry: "The War of the Austrian Succession concerned far more than just Silesia and was fought simultaneously on different continents," making it a conglomerate of dynastic and colonial conflicts.

The Seven Years' War (1756–1763), triggered when Prussia preemptively attacked Saxony in August 1756 to forestall Habsburg encirclement, escalated into what the Handbook calls "the first world war" — battles fought simultaneously in Europe, North America, India, and the Caribbean. The colonial dimensions were decisive: "From a British perspective, the war in North America was, in fact, won in Silesia" — British subsidies to Prussia diverted French military resources, helping Britain win its colonial struggle with France. The Seven Years' War thus established global interconnection as a structural feature of European politics.

Prussia's survival was contingent rather than inevitable. As the Handbook stresses, Prussia "was saved not by its inner strength, but by the death of Empress Elizabeth of Russia and the withdrawal of Russian troops from the Seven Years War." This near-extinction shaped subsequent Prussian political culture profoundly, hardening the militarized state structure that would later drive nationalist mythology.

### Phase 2: The French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (1792–1815)

The French Revolutionary armies repeatedly defeated Prussia (most decisively at Jena-Auerstedt, 1806) and Austria, transforming the map of Central Europe and dissolving the Holy Roman Empire in 1806. Napoleon's reorganization created the Confederation of the Rhine from German medium states, imposed French legal codes (particularly in the Rhineland), and abolished hundreds of small principalities. The Handbook distinguishes between the reactions of different German regions to French dominion:

- **Rhineland and former Confederation states**: French rule was resented primarily for economic hardship (Continental System), conscription burdens, and high taxes — but not on national grounds. "French rule was hated, however, not for national reasons (for that, city-state republicanism remained too prominent), but because it had caused hardship and misery for the people."

- **Prussia**: Sweeping Prussian reform (Stein, Hardenberg, Scharnhorst) followed the 1806 defeat, aiming to create a French-style conscript national army. The reformers introduced the "Krümper system" of rotating reserve training, enabling Prussia to mobilize 270,000 soldiers against Napoleon by 1813.

- **Catholic South and West**: Many ordinary Germans "hoped for the return of the Habsburg monarchy or even the restoration of the Old Reich," not Prussian liberation.

### The 'Wars of Liberation' (1813–1815) and the Problem of Nationalism

The standard nationalist account of 1813 as Germany's heroic national uprising is substantially revised by the Handbook. The "nationalist enthusiasm was predominantly a media event created by the educated elite." Around 500 writers — mostly Prussians — produced nationalist literature, but regional loyalties remained dominant: "German soldiers persistently perceived themselves as Saxons, Bavarians, and Württembergers." The wars produced "a transnational pantheon of heroes, including not only Tsar Alexander and Prince Bernadotte, but also the King of Great Britain and the Generals Blücher, York, and Wellington."

The historiographical contest over terminology — "wars of freedom" (Freiheitskriege) versus "wars of liberation" (Befreiungskriege) — itself reflects competing interpretations. The term "Wars of Liberation" can "only be traced from 1816 onwards" and was itself a retrospective nationalist construction; only from the vantage of post-1871 nationalism could these be read as German national wars.

## Causal Analysis

The 75-year arc of warfare, 1740–1815, was driven by:

1. **Dynastic competition**: The Austro-Prussian rivalry for Central European supremacy, energized by the Hohenzollern-Habsburg dualism dating from 1740.
2. **Global colonial rivalry**: The British-French competition for Atlantic and Asian empire consistently drew continental powers into interconnected conflicts.
3. **Structural features of the Holy Roman Empire**: The Empire's "multiple levels of legal relationships and loyalties, which oscillated between the Reich and individual states," made centralized war-making complex and created competing foci of patriotic identification.
4. **French Revolutionary dynamics**: The French republic and empire's military mobilization — levée en masse, conscription, nationalism — forced German states to respond with modernizing reforms or face extinction.

## Consequence Analysis

The wars reshaped Germany in five decisive ways:

1. **Dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire** (1806) and creation of the [[german-confederation]] (1815), replacing a thousand-year framework of overlapping sovereignty with a looser inter-state system.
2. **Territorial consolidation**: Hundreds of small principalities, free cities, and ecclesiastical territories were absorbed into larger states. The "assemblage of modern territorial states" that emerged from the French era became the building blocks of the Kaiserreich.
3. **Legal modernization**: French law codes, particularly in the Rhineland (where they survived until the twentieth century), introduced principles of legal equality, rationalized administration, and undermined feudal privilege.
4. **Incipient nationalism**: Prussian war propaganda during the Seven Years' War and the Wars of Liberation planted structural elements — war heroes, patriotic sacrifice, identification of the fatherland with the state — that later nationalist movements mobilized on a mass scale. See [[german-regionalism]] and [[war-and-the-german-states-1740-1815]].
5. **Gender and citizenship**: The Prussian reform movement introduced universal male conscription in 1814, institutionalizing the linkage of military service to citizenship and masculinizing the public sphere. See [[gender-in-modern-germany]].

## Historiography

German-language historiography has traditionally dated the beginning of the modern age to the Napoleonic era — "In the beginning was Napoleon," as Thomas Nipperdey's famous formulation has it. The Oxford Handbook's Chapter 5 (Ute Planert) deliberately challenges this periodization by pushing the starting point back to 1740, emphasizing the Seven Years' War as the crucible of both Prussian state nationalism and the first interconnected global conflict.

The contest over the meaning of the 1813–1815 "wars of liberation" has been a central battlefield in German historiography. Nineteenth-century nationalist historians (Treitschke, Droysen) interpreted them as a German national awakening; post-1945 revision has emphasized their dynastic, regional, and heterogeneous character. Ute Planert's *Der Mythos vom Befreiungskrieg* (2007) represents the most systematic recent revision, documenting resistance to French rule grounded in pragmatic hardship rather than national ideology, particularly in the Catholic South.
