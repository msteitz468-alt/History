# Ieyasu's Relations with New Spain (1609–1614)

Between 1609 and 1614 [[tokugawa-ieyasu|Ieyasu]] conducted an extended but ultimately fruitless
diplomatic exchange with New Spain (Mexico), seeking to open direct Pacific trade while resisting
Spanish missionary and strategic demands. The episode illuminates Ieyasu's consistent commercial
pragmatism and his equally consistent refusal to accept any foreign political interference under
cover of religion. Source: [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]].

---

## Don Rodrigo's Shipwreck (1609)

In 1609 Don Rodrigo Vivero y Velasco, returning to New Spain after a period as Governor of Manila,
was shipwrecked on the coast of Kazusa province when the *San Francisco* was blown off course.
Honda Tadatomo received him at Otaki castle and sent him to Edo, where he was presented to Shogun
Hidetada and then taken to Sumpu for audience with Ieyasu. [[adams-william|William Adams]] coached
Don Rodrigo in the procedures of the shogunal courts.

Don Rodrigo left a description of Ieyasu's court: the population of Edo was approximately 150,000
(Sumpu 100,000); the castle was entered through successive gates each guarded by two hundred
matchlock men; the palace corridors held "a thousand men-at-arms." The apartments were decorated
with gold ceilings. Ieyasu received him in a robe of white damask and spoke through an interpreter.

Ieyasu provided a ship to return Don Rodrigo to New Spain, and sent a letter with him requesting
that the Viceroy of New Spain send mining experts to Japan — Ieyasu's consistent desire throughout
his dealings with both Spanish and Portuguese. Don Rodrigo for his part urged:

1. That the Spaniards be allowed to build ships freely in Japan
2. That they be allowed to survey Japanese coasts
3. That Spanish ships sell goods where they pleased without search
4. That the Dutch be forbidden from Japan and their ships burned

## Ieyasu's 1612 Letter to New Spain

When Sotomayor, the envoy from New Spain, arrived in 1611 repeating essentially the same demands,
Ieyasu granted items 1 and 2, said nothing about 3, and flatly refused 4: "he could not interfere
in the quarrels of foreign nations or expel any of them from Japan."

His July 1612 letter to the Viceroy of New Spain is Sadler's most direct statement of Ieyasu's
foreign-policy philosophy:

> "Your honored country and mine are thus strengthening the bonds of friendship, and if ships come
> every year and exchange the valuable commodities of our two territories even the best government
> cannot equal the benefit that will ensue to the land and its people. But this land is a Divine
> country... But the religion that your honored country uses has tendencies of quite a different
> kind. There seems to be no affinity with anything in this land... so that as regards propaganda on
> reflection it seems better to cease from it and employ it no more. But the mutually profitable
> trade that will follow the arrival of merchant vessels is in the highest degree desirable."

The historian Tokutomi commented that Ieyasu's commercial views "would have won the approval of
Cobden and Bright and the Manchester School." Against Christianity "he set his face as firmly as
Hideyoshi had done, and for the same reason."

## Sebastian Viszcaino's Coastal Survey

Viszcaino had been sent to Japan to thank Ieyasu for his treatment of Don Rodrigo and to search
for legendary islands of gold and silver. He brought portraits of the King and Queen of Spain,
clothing, wine, and a clock (still preserved at Kunozan), but not the mining experts Ieyasu had
requested. He obtained permission to survey Japanese coasts — [[adams-william|Adams]] told Ieyasu
that coastal surveys were considered acts of espionage and acts of hostility in Europe. Ieyasu
replied that he did not fear foreign invasion, having sufficient troops to deal with any who came.
Viszcaino surveyed the coast from Akita to Nagasaki. During this voyage he encountered
[[date-masamune|Date Masamune]] and was recruited as nominal captain for the [[hasekura-mission-europe-1613|
Hasekura mission]] to Europe, a role he filled badly and abandoned at the first Spanish American port.

## The Madre de Dios Incident (1609–1611)

A parallel strand of Ieyasu's New Spain / Portuguese relations was the destruction of the
Portuguese galleon *Madre de Dios* at Nagasaki. In early 1609 Arima Harunobu had sent a ship to
Champa to procure incense wood for Ieyasu. Stopping at Macao, a fight broke out between Japanese
crew and Portuguese servants; five Japanese were killed and property stolen. Arima brought the
grievance to Ieyasu, who ordered him to attack the next Portuguese vessel at Nagasaki.

That vessel was the *Madre de Dios*, commanded by the very Pessoa who had been Governor of Macao
during the incident. Arima requested sole right of revenge. Ieyasu agreed. After failed attempts
to lure Pessoa ashore or assassinate him, Arima launched a night attack with six warships and two
specially built tower-ships. Portuguese fire-pots set the *Madre de Dios*'s own sails alight;
Pessoa fired the magazine and the ship exploded, sinking two hours after the battle began, taking
200 persons and a cargo of one million crowns (including 200,000 ryo of silver and 200,000 pounds
of silk thread) to the bottom. Arima recovered much of the cargo. The loss fell hardest on the
Jesuits awaiting their annual supply ship.

Portugal sent envoys from Goa in 1611. Ieyasu received them politely but sent a formal reply
(drafted by Hayashi Doshun, signed by Honda Masazumi) arguing that the attack was legally
justified: the captain had refused to answer government summons and had fired on officials.
The reply included a shogunal licence permitting future Portuguese trade: "According to the rules
of commerce they may trade as in former days." This was printed beneath the "August Red Seal."

## Outcome

No lasting commercial relationship with New Spain materialized. Spanish demands for coastal surveys
and missionary access were incompatible with Tokugawa policy. The [[hasekura-mission-europe-1613|
Hasekura mission]], partly inspired by the New Spain opening, failed to produce a trade agreement.
By 1614 Ieyasu was moving toward general suppression of Christianity and had ordered Jesuits out
of Japan entirely. The [[nanban-trade]] with Portugal continued in attenuated form until the
expulsion of 1639.

## Historiography

Don Rodrigo's account of his experiences in Japan survives and is used by Sadler to reconstruct
Ieyasu's court atmosphere. The 1612 letter to the Viceroy of New Spain is a primary document
of Tokugawa foreign policy, one of the clearest surviving statements of the religion-commerce
separation. The *Madre de Dios* incident is documented in Portuguese, Dutch (adversarial), and
Japanese sources.
