# The Reform of the Areopagus (Ephialtes), 462/1 BCE

## Narrative

In **462/1 BCE** the Athenian statesman **[[ephialtes]]**, exploiting the absence of
[[cimon|Cimon]] and 4,000 hoplites in the Peloponnese, carried through the Assembly a measure
that stripped the **[[areopagus|Council of the Areopagus]]** — the ancient body of ex-archons
— of the political powers it had accumulated as "guardian of the laws." [[lewis-cah-v5-1992|
CAH Vol. V]] (Ch. 4, Rhodes) titles the chapter "The Athenian Revolution": this is the moment
the wiki treats as the hinge between the Cleisthenic [[isonomia]] and the radical
**[[athenian-democracy]]** of the Periclean age.

## What was reformed

The Areopagus had held a cluster of **judicial-political powers** (*epitheta*, "accretions"):
the vetting (*dokimasia*) and audit (*euthynai*) of magistrates and the trial of *eisangeliai*
(impeachments for offences against the state). Ephialtes **transferred these to the Council of
Five Hundred, the Assembly, and the popular lawcourts (*dikasteria*)**, leaving the Areopagus
only its ancient jurisdiction over **homicide, wounding, arson, and certain religious
offences**. The *Athenaion Politeia* (favourable) calls the lost powers "accretions";
Plutarch (hostile) says the reform pitched the city "into undiluted democracy" — language
Rhodes reads as the surviving propaganda of both sides. A linked, symbolic act was the moving
of the law-tablets (*axones*) of Draco and Solon to the Agora, marking the transfer of the
laws to the people. The follow-on measures — [[pericles|Pericles']] **jury pay** and the
**citizenship law of 451/0** — completed the democracy.

## Causal Analysis

Rhodes resists a purely doctrinaire reading. Structural causes: the **decline of the
archonship** (sortition from 487/6; the rise of the elected generals) made a powerful Areopagus
of ex-archons "increasingly hard to justify," while the Cleisthenic machinery had given the
dēmos a taste for participation and growing confidence. Immediate triggers: the Areopagus had
been giving verdicts favourable to [[cimon|Cimon]] and his circle (acquitting him,
condemning [[themistocles]]), prompting their opponents to question its right to such
influence; and the humiliating **dismissal of Cimon's expedition** by Sparta discredited the
conservative line just as Ephialtes gained control. The contemporary debate is visible in
**Aeschylus** — the *Supplices* (463) sympathetic to popular sovereignty, the *Eumenides*
(458) refocusing the Areopagus as a homicide court and warning against civil strife.

## Consequence Analysis

- **Constitutional**: the popular organs (Assembly, Council, *dikasteria*) became the effective
  sovereign and judicial power — the **classical democracy**.
- **Political violence**: [[cimon]] was **ostracized** when he tried to reverse the reform, and
  **Ephialtes was murdered** (461) — the *Eumenides*' fear of *stasis* nearly realized.
- **Strategic**: Athens broke the alliance with [[sparta]], allied with Argos and Thessaly, and
  entered the **[[first-peloponnesian-war|First Peloponnesian War]]** — the constitutional and
  the foreign-policy revolutions were one event.

## Historiography

There are no contemporary narrative sources; the reform is reconstructed from the *Athenaion
Politeia*, Plutarch, and Aeschylus, all partisan or late. Disputed points: exactly **which**
powers the Areopagus lost (and whether some, e.g. *ephesis* against its capital verdicts, had
already gone c. 500); whether the reform was primarily **constitutional** or merely incidental
to a **foreign-policy** realignment (Rhodes insists the constitutional change was genuine and
central); and the authorship of the follow-on measures. See [[classical-greek-historiography]].
