# The Battle of Gaugamela, 331 BCE

## Narrative

At **Gaugamela**, on the plain near Arbela (modern Erbil) on **1 October 331 BCE**,
**[[alexander-the-great|Alexander]]** decisively defeated the main army of **[[achaemenid-empire|
Darius III]]** and broke the field power of the **Achaemenid Persian empire** for good.
[[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Chs. 16, 18, Bosworth & Hornblower) treats it as the
**culminating moment** of the volume's central event — "the biggest single change" of the
period, "the overthrow of the Achaemenid empire." On ground the Persians had levelled for
their scythed chariots and vast cavalry, Alexander's outnumbered combined-arms army — the
**sarissa phalanx** holding the centre and the **Companion cavalry** delivering the decisive
charge toward Darius — won by manoeuvre and timing; Darius fled the field, as he had at
Issus.

## "A game of chess"

Hornblower's Epilogue frames the campaign up to Gaugamela as resembling "**a game of chess,
with the opposing king as the prize** whose few and majestic movements dictate the lines of
penetration and pursuit." Once Darius was beaten and soon afterward murdered by his own
satrap Bessus (330), Alexander became "**the new Great King of Persia**," and the later
phases — the guerrilla war in Bactria, the Indian campaign — were "simply imposed on" him or
driven by his army's growing mutinousness. Gaugamela is thus the hinge: before it,
Alexander pursues a kingdom; after it, he governs one.

## Significance

Gaugamela ended the **two-century Achaemenid empire** (550–330) — the first power to unite
the Near East and the Aegean — and opened the **Hellenistic age**. Babylon, Susa and the
ceremonial capital **Persepolis** (later burned) fell in its wake; the wealth of Asia passed
to Macedon. It is the consummation of the long Greek argument with Persia that runs through
this volume — from [[cyrus-the-younger|Cyrus the Younger]] and the
[[march-of-the-ten-thousand-401bce|Ten Thousand]] through the panhellenism of Isocrates to
the [[league-of-corinth|League of Corinth's]] war of revenge — and the structural beginning
of a connected Greco-Near Eastern world reaching from Egypt to Bactria.
