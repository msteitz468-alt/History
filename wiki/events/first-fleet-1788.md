# First Fleet and Founding of New South Wales (1788)

On **18 January 1788** the ships of the First Fleet — six transports (*Alexander*,
*Friendship*, *Scarborough*, *Charlotte*, *Prince of Wales*, *Lady Penrhyn*), three
storeships, and two naval escorts — dropped anchor in **Botany Bay**. The Fleet
carried approximately **1,487 people**: Governor Arthur Phillip, 550 officers and marines,
**736 settler-convicts** (548 men, 188 women, and around 25 children of convicts), and
civilian personnel (CHA vol. 1, ch. 4).

## Narrative

The Fleet had departed Portsmouth in May 1787, sailed to the Cape of Good Hope, then
used the roaring forties to reach New Holland — "a place that seemed absurdly remote to
eighteenth-century newspaper readers" (CHA vol. 1, ch. 3). The ships arrived in Botany
Bay on the country of the **Gweagal and Kamaygal** peoples. An El Niño summer had
reduced fresh water; the bay offered inadequate shelter for the ships. Phillip reconnoitred
to the north and found **Port Jackson**, specifically **Sydney Cove** — "a snug cove in
Port Jackson to the north" with a reliable fresh-water stream — and no armed warriors on
the shore when the boats approached. He moved ships and people there; formal possession
was claimed on **26 January 1788** (later commemorated as Australia Day / Invasion Day).

Within days Phillip also despatched Lieutenant (later Governor) **Philip Gidley King**
with 22 convicts and seamen to **Norfolk Island**, 1,600 km to the east-north-east, to
secure it as a British possession and assess its flax and timber for naval stores.

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause of the First Fleet's dispatch was the **1786 Botany Bay Decision**:
Prime Minister Pitt's administration, facing hulks dangerously overcrowded after the
American market for convict labour closed with the Revolution, selected New Holland as
the destination for excess convicts (preceded_by: loss of American colonies 1776–83;
caused_by: British penal management crisis). Strategic and commercial rationales —
Pacific trade routes, naval stores, China tea trade — were attached to the decision
in public debate but their primacy is contested. See [[botany-bay-decision]].

The Botany Bay scheme was described by contemporaries as "not a land flowing with milk
and honey"; it was to be governed by military rather than civil law and the colony would
"transform felons into farmers" under Governor Phillip's Instructions.

## Consequence Analysis

1. **Foundation of New South Wales.** Sydney Cove became the nucleus of Australia's
   first permanent European settlement and the hub of the colonial economy.
2. **Assignment system embryo.** From 1792, Governor Grose began assigning convicts to
   officers as private labour (each new landowner received ten assigned convicts on full
   rations), creating the mechanism that would dominate convict life across both colonies
   for decades.
3. **Norfolk Island twin settlement.** Within two years, Norfolk Island housed 41 per cent
   of the colony's population; its later abandonment (1804–14) forced settlers into
   [[van-diemens-land|Van Diemen's Land]].
4. **Displacement of Aboriginal peoples.** Settlers from governor to convict carried
   assumptions rooted in John Locke's labour theory of property and cultural superiority;
   Aboriginal sovereignty was neither foreseen nor legally acknowledged, constituting the
   practical application of [[terra-nullius]]. Frontier violence began within years.
5. **Precedent for an emancipist society.** The founding 736 convicts and their
   descendants became the backbone of colonial agriculture and commerce, producing a
   society distinct from the planned agrarian penal experiment.

## Actors

**Arthur Phillip** — selected partly because he had transported Portuguese convicts to
Brazil; a "cautious and diligent man" who implemented his orders to the letter, initiated
small-farm policy, and established diplomacy with the Eora. He departed in late 1792
unwell and "perhaps demoralised" by false reports of failure reaching England.

**Gweagal and Kamaygal peoples** — the Aboriginal custodians of Botany Bay; their
warriors met the fleet in other bays. The Eora more broadly engaged, avoided, and
eventually partially accommodated the settlers at Sydney Cove in a long and violent
frontier process.

## Historiography

The First Fleet is among the most studied events in Australian history. Grace Karskens
(*The Colony*, 2009) and Alan Frost (*Botany Bay: The Real Story*, 2011) provide
contrasting emphases — Frost stressing imperial design, Karskens stressing convict
agency and pre-industrial popular culture. John Hirst (*Freedom on the Fatal Shore*, 2008)
argues the colony materialised as "a new society, not a prison." All are drawn on in
CHA vol. 1, ch. 4. The question of Aboriginal sovereign resistance as a structural
factor in colonisation is treated by Karskens (*The Colony*, chs 11–13) and by the
historiography of [[terra-nullius]].
