# The Mfecane

The **Mfecane** (Zulu, "the crushing"; Sotho *difaqane*) was the period of **upheaval, warfare, migration
and state-formation** that convulsed southern Africa c. 1815–1840 — out of which the **Zulu**, **Sotho**,
**Swazi**, and **Ndebele** states emerged. Its causes are the subject of one of the most charged
**controversies** in African history, and **[[reader-africa-biography|Reader]]** sides firmly with the
revisionists.

## The traditional account

The long-standing "Shaka-centric" narrative held the Mfecane to be an **internally generated African
cataclysm**: the rise of the militarized **Zulu kingdom under Shaka** (from c. 1816) set off a chain
reaction of conquest and flight that depopulated the interior, drove the Ndebele north, and forced
refugees into the defensive mountain kingdoms of Moshoeshoe's Sotho and the Swazi. In this telling the
violence was **black-on-black**, set in motion before whites arrived.

## The revisionist (Cobbing) thesis — Reader's view

Reader adopts **Julian Cobbing's** argument that the Shaka-centred story is **largely a myth** — one that
conveniently served twentieth-century apartheid ideology by depicting the interior as **emptied by African
savagery** (legitimating white settlement) and by casting whites as **peacemakers**. On this account the
true drivers were **two external "plunder systems"**:

- **Slave-raiding out of Delagoa Bay** (Lourenço Marques), where Portuguese, French and Brazilian demand —
  surging after the Haitian revolution and Brazil's plantation boom — drove armed raids that tore ~20,000+
  people from the hinterland in the 1820s, generating the refugee waves and forcing groups (including the
  proto-Zulu) into defensive amalgamation.
- **Cape-frontier labour-raiding**, where settlers desperate for workers conducted slave-raids dressed up
  as battles against marauding "hordes." Reader dissects the **Dithakong "battle" (1823)** and the
  **Mbolompo massacre (1828)** as labour-raids by missionaries, agents and commandos who then "rescued"
  captives into unpaid "apprenticeship" — the myth of a 100,000-strong "Mantatee horde," and the invocation
  of "Chaka's people," manufactured to justify the violence. Southern Africa, in this reading, was caught
  in "the **transcontinental cross-fire** of interrelated European plunder systems," not convulsed by Shaka.

## Historiography

This is a genuine, **partially-resolved** controversy. Cobbing's 1988 critique forced a wholesale
re-examination; most historians now accept that the **Shaka-centred "Mfecane" was overstated and
ideologically loaded**, and that slaving and settler pressure were major factors — but many also hold that
Cobbing **overcorrected**, underrating genuine internal dynamics (ecological stress, the real expansion of
the Zulu state, indigenous political innovation). The wiki records both, giving weight to the revisionist
reframing that Reader endorses while not erasing Zulu agency. The episode is a prime case of how
**historiography serves politics** — the "empty land" myth underwrote both settler land claims and
apartheid.

The **standard view this revisionism reacted against** is well represented by the **UNESCO
[[gha-vol6-19th-century|General History of Africa, Vol. VI]]** (ch. 5, Ngcongco, 1989), which — in the
**Omer-Cooper "Zulu aftermath" tradition** — treats the Mfecane as a genuine **African revolution in
state-formation**: an internally-generated process of military and political innovation (the Zulu
*amabutho* regimental system) that, for all its violence, **built new and larger states** (Sotho, Swazi,
Ndebele, Gaza, Kololo) across southern and central Africa. Reader (1997), following Cobbing, contests this
internal-revolution framing; GHA VI exemplifies the position it argues against — which is exactly why the
debate remains live.
