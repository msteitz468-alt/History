# The Second Punic War (218–201 BCE)

The **Hannibalic War** — the central war of the western Mediterranean and, in
[[polybius|Polybius']] design, the hinge on which the histories of West and East
were interwoven (*symploke*) into a single field. [[hannibal]] carried the war
into Italy and held the peninsula for sixteen years; Rome survived three
catastrophic defeats, ground the invader down by attrition, conquered Punic
Spain, and finally destroyed Carthaginian power in Africa at
[[battle-of-zama-202bce|Zama]]. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 3
(Briscoe), with the Spanish prelude in Ch. 2 (Scullard).

## Narrative

### Outbreak

The war's origins are analysed on [[causes-of-second-punic-war]]. In brief:
[[hamilcar-barca]] and his son-in-law Hasdrubal had rebuilt a Carthaginian
empire in Spain (237–221) after the losses of the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|
First Punic War]] and Rome's seizure of Sardinia (238). The **Ebro treaty**
(226) set the river as the northern limit of Punic arms; Rome's separate
*fides*-tie with **Saguntum** (south of the Ebro) was the flashpoint.
[[hannibal]], commander from 221, besieged and took **Saguntum**
([[siege-of-saguntum-219bce|219]]); Rome declared war in 218.

### The war in Italy (218–203)

Hannibal marched from New Carthage over the Alps into the Po valley
(autumn 218), where the recently subjugated Gauls welcomed him. He won a
cavalry action at the **Ticinus**, then a major victory at the **Trebbia**
(Dec. 218), destroyed a consular army by ambush at **Lake Trasimene** (217,
the consul Flaminius killed), and annihilated a huge Roman army — Polybius'
80,000 — at **[[battle-of-cannae-216bce|Cannae]]** (216). Cannae triggered the
defection of much of southern Italy (Capua, Tarentum, the Bruttii) and of
Syracuse.

Yet no Latin colony and no Roman citizen body defected; Etruria and central
Italy held. Under the attrition strategy associated with
[[fabius-maximus|Fabius Maximus]] ([[fabian-strategy]]), Rome avoided pitched
battle on Hannibal's terms, kept vastly superior numbers of legions in the
field, and recovered the defecting cities one by one — **Syracuse** (Marcellus,
212), **Capua** (211), **Tarentum** (Fabius, 209). Hannibal's march on Rome
(211) to relieve Capua was a feint that changed nothing. His brother
**Hasdrubal**, crossing from Spain to reinforce him, was intercepted and killed
at the **Metaurus** (207) — the decisive turn in Italy. Hannibal, confined to
Bruttium, was recalled to Africa in 203.

### Spain (218–206)

The Senate's original plan was to fight in Spain. **Gnaeus** and **Publius
Cornelius Scipio** kept the Carthaginian armies occupied for seven years and
prevented reinforcement of Hannibal, but both were killed in 211. The young
**[[scipio-africanus|P. Cornelius Scipio]]** (son and nephew of the dead
commanders), elected to an extraordinary command, stormed **New Carthage**
(209), won at **Baecula** (208) and decisively at **Ilipa** (206), driving
Carthage out of Spain entirely.

### Sicily, Sardinia, and the sea

[[hiero-ii-of-syracuse|Hiero II]] of Syracuse stayed loyal until his death
(215); his successor's defection brought **Marcellus'** siege and the sack of
Syracuse (212). Sardinia saw a suppressed revolt (215). Rome held continuous
naval superiority throughout — never decisive in battle, but it denied Hannibal
seaborne reinforcement and secured grain imports from Sicily, Sardinia, and
Egypt.

### Africa and Zama (204–201)

Scipio crossed to Africa (204), burned the camps of Hasdrubal Gisgo and
**Syphax**, won at the **Great Plains**, and detached the Numidian
**[[masinissa]]** to Rome's side. Recalled Hannibal met Scipio at
**[[battle-of-zama-202bce|Zama]]** (202) and was defeated. The peace (201)
stripped Carthage of Spain, its fleet (all but ten ships) and elephants,
forbade war without Roman permission, recognised Masinissa's expanded
kingdom, and imposed a 10,000-talent indemnity over fifty years.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: [[causes-of-second-punic-war]] — the Barcid Spanish base, the
unresolved grievances of the [[first-punic-war-264-241bce|First War]] and
Sardinia, the Saguntum/Ebro collision.
contributed_to: Rome's reserves of citizen, Latin, and allied **manpower**
(a peak of ~240,000 under arms in 212) and the loyalty of the Latin and central
Italian allies — the structural factors Briscoe and Polybius both stress.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: Roman supremacy in the western Mediterranean; the **Spanish provinces**
([[roman-provincial-system]]); the disarmament of [[carthage]] and its reduction
to a client hemmed in by [[masinissa|Numidia]] (the seed of the
[[first-punic-war-264-241bce|Third Punic War]]); the monetary reform (the
denarius, c. 212).
enabled: the **eastward turn** of Roman power — Briscoe argues the declaration of
war on [[philip-v|Macedon]] in 200 was driven less by aggressive imperialism than
by the memory of Hannibal in Italy and fear of another invader; the war also
hardened the Senate's refusal to reward the allies, storing up the Italian
question for the Gracchan age.
contributed_to: the **transformation of Italian agriculture** — devastation,
the long absence of small farmers, and the confiscation of Capuan and rebel land
as *ager publicus*.

## Historiography

The war is exceptionally well documented: **[[polybius]]** (books III–V intact to
216, fragments thereafter), an eye-witness's friend of the Scipios, and
**[[livy]]** (books XXI–XXX), who drew heavily on Polybius for eastern matter and
on the Sullan annalists (Valerius Antias, Claudius Quadrigarius) for Roman
detail. Where Polybius is a control, Livy is reliable; where not, his casualty
figures and dramatic set-pieces need caution. Persistent debates: the **route of
Hannibal's Alpine crossing** (Mt Genèvre / Mt Cenis / Col de Clapier); the
**causation** of the war (see [[causes-of-second-punic-war]]); the reliability of
the **factional/*gentes* analysis** of war-time Roman politics (the
"Scipionic" vs. "Fabian–Fulvian" groupings, developed by Scullard and accepted
in part by Briscoe, but resting on contested prosopographical inference);
**Hannibal's war aims** (Briscoe: not the destruction of Rome but a settlement
recovering Sicily and Sardinia and checking Roman expansion); and whether Rome's
post-war foreign policy was driven by acquisitive imperialism (Harris) or
defensive fear (Briscoe). The Barcid coinage of Spain is a key independent
witness for a period the Greek sources illuminate only obliquely.
