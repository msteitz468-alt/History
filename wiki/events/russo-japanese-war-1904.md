# The Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905)

The war in which [[japan|Japan]] defeated the **Russian Empire** — the **first victory of a
modern Asian state over a European great power**, and the event that confirmed Japan's
arrival among the world's powers. From Mason & Caiger's
[[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]] (ch. 15).

## Narrative

Russia's expansion across northern Asia (the trans-Siberian railway) had by 1900 made it
the dominant power in **Manchuria**, and the tsar's government also pressed into **Korea**,
which Japan regarded as vital to its security. With negotiations failed and backed by the
**Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)**, Japan went to war in 1904. It was far larger and more
serious than the Sino-Japanese War: ~200,000 Japanese and ~300,000 Russian casualties in
the **Manchurian** land fighting. Japan captured the fortress of **Port Arthur** (Jan. 1905,
after a seven-month siege) and won the great land battle of **Mukden**; **Admiral Tōgō**
annihilated the Russian Baltic fleet at **Tsushima (May 1905)**. US President **Theodore
Roosevelt** mediated the **Treaty of Portsmouth**, by which Russia ceded southern Sakhalin
and Port Arthur and conceded Japan's paramountcy in Korea.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Russo-Japanese rivalry over Korea and Manchuria** as both empires expanded
  into the power vacuum of a weakening China.
- `caused_by:` the **[[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Triple Intervention of 1895]]** — by
  forcing Japan to disgorge the Liaodong peninsula, which Russia then occupied (Port Arthur),
  the three powers turned Japanese expansionist energy directly against Russia (the revenge
  slogan *gashin shōtan*). The [[cambridge-history-japan-v5-nineteenth-century|Cambridge
  History Vol. 5]] (Iriye) treats this as the direct sequel to the Sino-Japanese war.
- `contributed_to:` the **Anglo-Japanese Alliance** (which kept other powers neutral) and
  the breakdown of negotiations.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Japan's recognition as a **great power** — and, resoundingly, the demonstration
  that an **Asian nation could defeat a European empire**, electrifying anticolonial
  nationalists across Asia.
- `produced:` Japanese control of **Korea** (a protectorate under Itō Hirobumi, **annexed
  outright in 1910**) and **southern Manchuria** — deepening [[japanese-imperialism|Japanese
  imperialism]].
- `contributed_to:` the **Russian Revolution of 1905** (defeat discrediting the tsarist
  regime).
- Domestically, the harsh-seeming Portsmouth terms sparked riots in Tokyo (the public
  wanting more) — a sign of the bellicose popular nationalism Meiji leaders had to manage.

## Significance

Mason and Caiger present the war as the capstone of Meiji diplomacy: with **treaty revision
achieved (1894)** and now two great wars won, Japan had secured its frontiers and a
"natural" sphere in northeast Asia. But entering the great-power club "riddled with national
hatreds and rivalries," and with a tradition of **unquestioned nationalism and opportunism**,
also set Japan on the path toward the catastrophe of 1945.

## Historiography

The war is unusually well documented on both sides: Japanese official military histories, the Cambridge History of Japan's synthesis (Iriye), and Russian archival and memoir material (though Russian sources are less represented in this collection than Japanese ones). Contemporary Western observers treated the war as a landmark demonstration of modern industrialized warfare — trench lines, machine guns, and massed artillery at Mukden and Port Arthur anticipating the First World War — a reading later validated by military historians studying the war's tactical lessons. Historiographical debate centers on Japan's long-term trajectory: Mason and Caiger's own framing (that victory set Japan toward "the catastrophe of 1945") reflects a retrospective, teleological reading common in postwar historiography, which more recent scholarship has qualified by stressing the contingency of Japan's later turn toward militarism rather than treating 1905 as inevitably setting that course. The war's reception across colonized Asia — as proof that European power could be defeated — is well attested but drawn mainly from secondary synthesis rather than primary Asian-language sources in this collection.
