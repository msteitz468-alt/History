## Narrative

The Herero–Nama War and associated genocide (January 1904 – March 1907) was the first genocide of the twentieth century, carried out by German colonial forces in German Southwest Africa (present-day Namibia). It remains one of the most debated cases in the history of [[german-colonialism]] and modern genocide studies.

The conflict began in January 1904 as the result of "growing tensions over competing Herero and German claims to land and cattle." Historian Jan-Bart Gewald has established that a German lieutenant fired the first shots; official reports claimed a concerted Herero attack. The initial response was commanded by Governor Theodor Leutwein, who fought with "all the brutality common in European colonial wars." After six months, German command was transferred to General Lothar von Trotha, a veteran of German East Africa and the suppression of the Boxer Uprising in China.

Trotha sought the kind of decisive encirclement battle (*Vernichtungsschlacht*) recommended by standard military doctrine, at the Battle of Waterberg (August 1904). Historian Isabel V. Hull (*Absolute Destruction*, 2005) has shown that Trotha pursued genocidal tactics even after the Herero's military capacity was destroyed — because "the entire Herero people, and not just their soldiers, had already been defined as the enemy."

In October 1904, after surviving Herero escaped German pursuit into the Omaheke Desert, Trotha issued his infamous *Vernichtungsbefehl* (extermination order): every Herero man was to be shot; Herero women and children were to be driven into the desert. The order was cancelled in December 1904 under pressure from Berlin, but survivors were imprisoned in concentration camps where they "continued to die at an alarming rate." The Nama, who rose against German rule shortly after the Herero, were subjected to similar treatment.

**Death tolls**:
- Herero: fewer than 20,000 surviving from a pre-war population of 60,000–80,000
- Nama: more than half of 20,000 killed

## Causal Analysis

Zimmerman's chapter situates the genocide within the political economy of settler colonialism: "while there was often a colonial interest in maintaining an indigenous labor force, there was an even greater interest in seizing land and other resources for white settlers. Such a situation, in which colonial whites have no need for a population that they consider in any case not fully human and, indeed, in which this population competes with them for land, has often led to genocides." Comparative cases cited: Native Americans, Tasmanians.

The racial-ideological framework was the *Naturvolk* concept: Herero people were conceptualized as static, natural, without history — and thus expendable when they competed with German settlers for land. The military culture of total war (*Vernichtungsstrategie*) combined with colonial racial dehumanization produced the conditions for genocide.

The genocide was "so extreme and expensive that it cannot be counted as a successful economic policy for settler colonialism" — it destroyed potential labor, ruined capital investments, and provoked international condemnation.

## Political Consequences

The Herero and Maji Maji wars generated a colonial political crisis in Germany. The Catholic Center Party broke with the government over colonial abuses, leading Chancellor Bülow to dissolve the Reichstag and call elections in 1906–1907 — the so-called "Hottentot election" (a term used at the time). The government branded Catholic and Socialist critics "enemies of the Reich" and won the election by deploying the colonial *Kulturkampf* rhetoric of national civilization.

The election result indicated that colonial brutality could be politically profitable in the short term, but the scandal drove liberal reformers to push for the appointment of Bernhard Dernburg as colonial administrator and a shift toward a reformist "civilizing mission" model.

In the Reichstag debates, Social Democrats and Catholics were the main voices of opposition to genocide. "Left liberals suggested moderation and the consigning of natives to reservations, as was done, they argued, for Indians and wild animals" — a comparison that reveals the transnational racial framework.

## Historiography

The genocide was little studied until the late twentieth century. The major historiographical debates:

**Continuity debate**: Does a causal or structural line connect the Herero genocide to the Holocaust? Jürgen Zimmerer and others argue that colonial genocide established techniques, mentalities, and personnel (officers who served in Africa later served in the East) that informed Nazi genocide. Critics (Madley, others) see the connections as partial — the Holocaust was qualitatively different in scale, bureaucratic rationalization, and ideological driver.

**Hull's military culture thesis**: Isabel V. Hull (*Absolute Destruction*, 2005) argues that German military doctrine's fixation on total destruction (*Vernichtung*) was an institutional feature that led to genocidal outcomes across colonial and European wars — not simply racial ideology.

**Zimmerman's transnational framework**: Chapter 16 in this volume argues the genocide should be seen in the context of settler colonialism generally (comparable to Native American and Tasmanian cases) and transnational racial politics (drawing on American models), rather than as uniquely German.

**Memory and reparations**: Namibia achieved independence in 1990. The German government formally acknowledged the genocide in 2021 and agreed to a development fund of 1.1 billion euros, though without agreeing to legal reparations. The politics of acknowledgment, memory, and compensation remain contested.
