## Narrative

The Irish Civil War lasted from June 1922 to May 1923, fought between the pro-treaty Provisional Government / [[irish-free-state|Irish Free State]] forces and anti-treaty Irish Republican Army units who rejected the Anglo-Irish Treaty of December 1921. The immediate trigger was the pro-treaty vote in the June 1922 election, the subsequent collapse of an agreed pact between Michael Collins and Éamon de Valera, and the Free State government's bombardment of the Four Courts in Dublin — seized by anti-treaty IRA — in late June.

The war was the culmination of the treaty split within Sinn Féin and the IRA, centred on two flashpoints: the oath of fidelity to the British Crown required of members of the new Dáil, and the question of whether the treaty was a stepping-stone to a republic or a permanent settlement. Anti-treaty forces mounted a guerrilla campaign aimed at making the country ungovernable, but, as historian Bill Kissane has argued, they were 'fundamentally weak due to the absence of a constructive alternative to the Free State, serious difficulties in resource mobilization, and, above all, a war-weary public' (Jackson, Oxford Handbook, ch. 32).

The conflict was more violent than the War of Independence that preceded it: estimates of fatalities range from 927 to over 3,000, a figure historians have debated precisely because the Civil War 'lacked attention' — partly because some historians in the Troubles era viewed anti-treaty republicans as precursors of the Provisional IRA and therefore avoided close scrutiny of the Free State's own repressive conduct.

The Free State government executed 77 republican prisoners — more than the British had executed during the War of Independence — in an effort to break resistance. In Kerry in March 1923, eight republican prisoners were tied to a landmine and killed, followed by nine more in four days; Major-General Paddy Daly was implicated. The war ended in April–May 1923 with a republican ceasefire/dump-arms order rather than a surrender.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` anglo irish treaty 1921 — partition of the treaty vote in the Dáil and Sinn Féin
- `caused_by:` oath-of-allegiance dispute — the requirement that Dáil members swear fidelity to the Crown was the irreducible constitutional sticking point
- `contributed_to:` death of Michael Collins (August 1922) — removed the leader who combined republican credibility with pro-treaty pragmatism
- `contributed_to:` fratricidal bonds of the War of Independence — the IRA split pitted comrades against each other, intensifying bitterness

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` [[irish-free-state]] as a hardened, conservative state committed to fiscal retrenchment and security legislation
- `produced:` Cumann na nGaedheal's conservatism — the Civil War compelled the government to rely on British-inherited administrative structures and authoritarian public-safety legislation, foreclosing more radical social programmes
- `produced:` a polarized two-party system — the Civil War cleavage structured Irish politics for decades; Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael emerged directly from the pro- and anti-treaty camps
- `enabled:` de Valera's eventual political triumph — by forcing Fianna Fáil into a strong nationalist opposition posture against the discredited Free State
- `produced:` psychological trauma — in Joe Lee's phrase the fratricidal violence turned 'heart to stone', and the nature of the fighting was 'distorted, misunderstood, but most of all ignored' for generations

## Actors

- **Provisional Government / Cumann na nGaedheal**: W.T. Cosgrave assumed the presidency of the Executive Council after Collins's death; Kevin O'Higgins became the political strong man, advocating ruthless suppression of the anti-treaty campaign.
- **Anti-treaty IRA**: Without a political programme equivalent to the Dáil, anti-treaty forces lacked a constructive alternative; de Valera was associated with them but exercised little military authority.
- **[[eamon-de-valera]]**: Led anti-treaty political opposition; eventually accepted the legitimacy of the Free State by entering the Dáil with Fianna Fáil in 1927.

## Historiography

Historiography of the Civil War has been thin relative to the War of Independence. Michael Hopkinson's *Green Against Green* (1988) remains the principal stand-alone national study. The conflict attracted less scholarly attention partly because anti-treaty republicans were viewed through the prism of the northern Troubles, which introduced anachronistic framing. Bill Kissane's *The Politics of the Irish Civil War* (2005) offered a political-science approach emphasizing variant attitudes to self-determination rather than the Collins-versus-de Valera personal narrative. Recent revisionism has asked questions about the democratic credentials of the Free State itself — notably whether fear of British reoccupation, not genuine consent, drove treaty acceptance. The Bureau of Military History archive (opened 2003) and the Military Service Pensions Collection are transforming knowledge of ground-level experience.

The extent of Free State violence — 77 executions, Kerry atrocities — has been under-analysed. John Regan's work on the 'counter-revolution' argues that the Civil War inaugurated a conservative backlash that consolidated the power of the Catholic middle class for a generation.
