# Battle of Halidon Hill (1333)

Edward III's first major victory in Scotland, fought in support of Edward Balliol. The English used a defensive formation explicitly modeled on the Disinherited's success at Dupplin Moor the previous year. It demonstrated the effectiveness of dismounted men-at-arms in solid lines with archers on the wings against a larger charging Scottish host.

## Narrative

Edward III openly backed Edward Balliol's claim, broke the Treaty of Northampton pretext, and joined the siege of Berwick. To force a battle, English forces hanged Scottish hostages.

The English army (c. 10,000, fighting entirely on foot per contemporary accounts, with a large archer contingent) deployed in three infantry lines arranged in an arc at the base of Halidon Hill, with archers forming the wings. The formation was strictly defensive and directly copied from Dupplin Moor.

The Scots, in larger numbers, took the hill position. A single combat occurred (English champion Robert Benhale vs. a Scottish knight with his dog). After a stalemate/provocations, the Scots charged downhill in mass, aiming for Balliol's position at the rear.

English archers harassed the flanks and front as the Scots descended. The battle became hand-to-hand between masses of dismounted infantry. The Scots were unable to break the English defensive line. The English held and eventually prevailed.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Edward III's decision to intervene militarily for Balliol and to relieve/secure Berwick.
- `contributed_to:` Deliberate English choice of defensive ground and formation (arc + archer wings) learned from Dupplin; Scots' decision to charge from the hill into prepared archer fire and solid infantry.
- Edward III explicitly invoked Dupplin's success to his troops.

## Consequence Analysis

- Crushing defeat for the Scots; many nobles killed.
- Berwick surrendered and was ceded to England.
- Balliol's second, brief restoration.
- Confirmed and publicized the tactical model (dismounted infantry line + flanking archers on good defensive ground) that would be used throughout the HYW.
- Part of the learning curve from Bannockburn (Scottish schiltron success) through Dupplin/Halidon to the mature English system.

## Actors

- Edward III: led in person; chose the defensive setup and cited prior success.
- Edward Balliol: claimant present with the English.
- Sir Archibald Douglas: Scottish commander; his host defeated.

## Historiography

Chronicles (Baker, Brut, Burton, Lanercost, etc.) highlight the formation parallels to Dupplin, the role of archers in disordering the charge, and the English infantry's ability to hold the line. DeVries stresses continuity of the dismounted + archery defensive posture as the decisive factor. The battle is seen as Edward III's "first great victory" and a bridge between Scottish wars and the French campaigns.
