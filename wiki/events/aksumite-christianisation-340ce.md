# Aksumite Christianisation (c. 340 CE)

## Narrative

The adoption of Christianity by the Aksumite king Ezana in the second quarter of the fourth century CE transformed Aksum into one of the earliest formally Christian states in the world. The process was gradual, politically motivated, and initially restricted to the royal court and a Greek-speaking expatriate trading community before spreading through the broader population over the following century.

### The Frumentius account

The primary narrative is preserved by Rufinus of Aquileia in his *Ecclesiastical History* (late fourth century), subsequently repeated with minor additions by Socrates Scholasticus, Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and Sozomen (all early fifth century, all derived from Rufinus):

A Syrian scholar named **Meropius** travelled to "India" (a term in late antiquity loosely applied to Arabia and much of East Africa) with two young relatives, **Frumentius** and **Aedesius**. Their ship was wrecked on the Red Sea coast. Meropius was killed; the two young men were captured and brought to the Aksumite court, where they entered royal service. On the king's death, his widow kept them on as the young prince's tutors and administrators. When the new king assumed full authority, Aedesius returned to Syria; Frumentius travelled to Alexandria and asked Patriarch **Athanasius** to appoint a bishop for the growing Christian community at Aksum. Athanasius consecrated Frumentius himself as bishop — he became known in Ethiopia as **Abba Salama** ("Father of Peace") — who returned to Aksum and made many converts.

Rufinus does not name the two successive Aksumite kings, nor does he explicitly state that the king who converted was Ezana. However, a letter from the Roman Emperor **Constantius II** (reigned 337–61), preserved among Athanasius' writings, was addressed to "Ezana and Sazana, rulers of Aksum" and refers to Frumentius as serving as bishop at Aksum in 356/7 — confirming the identification. The stone inscriptions and coins of Ezana provide the independent corroboration.

### The inscriptions' evidence

Ezana's royal inscriptions at Aksum preserve the trajectory of conversion in real time. The earliest inscriptions (RIE 186, 187, 188) invoke the war-god **Mahrem** as divine father of the king: "son of the invincible Mahrem." The trilingual inscriptions RIE 185/270 and 185bis/270bis (recording a campaign against the Beja) still invoke Mahrem, Astar, and Beher, but the Greek version of 270bis adds "Lord of Heaven and Earth" — possibly an attempt to gloss the pagan deity names for a foreign audience. RIE 189 attributes Ezana's success to "the might of the Lord of Heaven" — monotheist but without explicit Christianity. RIE 190/271 (the Noba and Kasu campaign) is explicitly and emphatically Christian in its Greek text: invoking the Trinity, calling Ezana "servant of Christ," and designating Christ as "the god in whom I have believed."

The coins tell the same story: pre-Christian coins bear the crescent-and-disc symbol; Ezana's later coins replace this with the Christian cross. Intermediate coins bear no religious symbol.

### The broader context

Phillipson emphasises that Aksumite conversion was not an isolated phenomenon: Himyar (the dominant kingdom of southern Arabia) adopted a form of monotheism in the same period, as did the Caucasian kingdom of Iberia/Kartli. The conversion was part of a wider fourth-century trend among states peripheral to the Roman Empire. The political logic was clear: Christianity linked Aksum to the late Roman / Byzantine world and its trade networks and provided ideological prestige. Christianity was initially centred on Greek-speaking expatriates and the royal court; Ezana expressed his conversion more openly in his Greek inscriptions and on internationally circulating gold coinage than in his Ge'ez texts and locally circulating copper coins.

### Spread of Christianity

Archaeological evidence confirms the gradual penetration of Christianity: cross-designs on domestic pottery do not become common until the late fifth and early sixth centuries; the stelae at Anza and Matara (inland from Adulis) still bear the pre-Christian crescent-and-disc, with a probable third-century date. The "Nine Saints" — missionaries from Byzantine territory arriving in the late fifth century — are credited with establishing monasteries beyond the capital. The Abba Garima Gospels at Adwa monastery have been radiocarbon-dated to the sixth or seventh century, confirming that Ethiopian manuscript illumination tradition was active by this period. The Bible was translated into Ge'ez by approximately the sixth century.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[indian-ocean-trade]] — Red Sea commerce brought Christian merchants to Aksumite ports; Rufinus implies a Christian expatriate trading community was already at Aksum before Frumentius' return as bishop.
- `contributed_to:` regional monotheist trend — Himyar, Iberia/Kartli, and Aksum all adopted forms of monotheism in the same decades, suggesting a broader structural shift rather than unique Aksumite circumstances.
- `contributed_to:` Ezana's political calculation — Constantius II's letter implies he viewed Aksum as an ally worth influencing; Aksum presumably received corresponding prestige from the alignment.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` [[aksum]] as a Christian state — one of the earliest in the world, alongside Armenia and Iberia.
- `produced:` institutional link to Alexandrian Christianity, placing Aksum in the Coptic/Monophysite tradition. Frumentius' consecration by Athanasius (himself non-Arian) meant Aksum was aligned against the Arian position favoured by Constantius II. Aksum rejected the Council of Chalcedon (451), maintaining the pre-Chalcedonian Monophysite position — a defining feature of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church to the present.
- `enabled:` translation of the Bible into Ge'ez and the development of Ethiopian Christian literature, including the Kebra Negast (compiled c. 1300 from earlier materials).
- `enabled:` survival of Christianity as the spine of Ethiopian civilisation beyond Aksum's political collapse; the Ethiopian Orthodox Church is the direct institutional heir.

## Actors

- **Ezana of Aksum** — the converting king; his inscriptions and coins are the primary evidence.
- **Frumentius / Abba Salama** — the Syrian-born first bishop of Aksum; consecrated by Athanasius.
- **Athanasius of Alexandria** — Patriarch who consecrated Frumentius; his non-Arian position shaped Aksumite theological inheritance.
- **Constantius II** — Roman emperor whose 356/7 letter provides external confirmation of Frumentius at Aksum; his attempt to assert Arian oversight of the Aksumite church was unsuccessful.

## Historiography

The primary source is Rufinus' *Ecclesiastical History* (late 4th c.), itself deriving from a lost work by Gelasius of Caesarea written in the 380s. All fifth-century accounts derive from Rufinus. Phillipson (2012) notes that Rufinus does not name the Aksumite kings, and Aksum's identification depends on independent epigraphic and numismatic evidence. The date of conversion is debated: the range 337–356 is secure (bounded by Athanasius' elevation and Constantius' letter). Ethiopian tradition dates it to 333 EC (≈ AD 340); Hahn's numismatic analysis has suggested 340 or 347–9. Phillipson concludes the best estimate is "337–50."

Ethiopian tradition attributes the conversion to "Abraha and Atsbaha" rather than Ezana — probably a conflation with the sixth-century king Kaleb (whose alternative name was Ella Atsbaha) and his Arabian viceroy Abraha.

The role of political calculation versus genuine religious conviction in Ezana's conversion is a standing historiographical question. The evidence of the inscriptions — showing the transition playing out over multiple campaigns, with differing Greek and Ge'ez versions targeting different audiences — strongly supports Phillipson's view that the conversion was a deliberately managed, partly politically motivated process.
