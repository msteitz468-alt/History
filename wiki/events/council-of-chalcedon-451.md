# The Council of Chalcedon and the Christological Controversies

The defining religious conflict of late antiquity: the series of **Oecumenical Councils** — **Ephesus I
(431)**, **Chalcedon (451)** and **Constantinople II (553)** — that sought to fix the orthodox doctrine of
**the person of Christ**, and the great **schism** their definitions produced. The Council of **Chalcedon
(451)** is the pivot: its formula of "**one person in two natures**" became the touchstone of
"Chalcedonian" orthodoxy in both Rome and Constantinople — and the cause of the lasting **Monophysite**
breach that split the eastern Church for good. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 27 (**Edward
Hall / G. M. Hall**, the definition and enforcement of orthodoxy).

## The two schools

The controversies grew from a clash of theological traditions: the **Alexandrian school** (allegorical,
stressing the **one nature** of the divine Logos made flesh) against the **Antiochene school** (literal,
stressing the **two natures** in Christ after the union). Each claimed descent from **Nicaea (325)**, and
imperial policy — often seeking **administrative unity** more than truth — repeatedly forced the issue.

## Ephesus I (431): Cyril against Nestorius

When **Nestorius** (Antiochene), patriarch of Constantinople, rejected calling Mary ***Theotokos*** ("God-
bearer") in favour of *Christotokos*, **Cyril of Alexandria** attacked him, attaching **Twelve Anathemas** to
his third letter. At **Ephesus (431)**, Cyril condemned and deposed Nestorius **before** the Antiochene and
Roman delegations arrived; Mary was proclaimed *Theotokos*. Nestorius was banished and his works burnt;
**Nestorian Christianity** took root beyond the empire in **Persia**. A subsequent **Formula of Reunion**
(433) patched Alexandria and Antioch together — the first of many fragile theological compromises.

## The "Robber Council" (449) and Chalcedon (451)

The Constantinopolitan archimandrite **Eutyches** pushed Cyril's "one nature" to the extreme (implying
Christ was *not* consubstantial with humanity). His patron **Dioscorus** of Alexandria, backing him, presided
over the **Second Council of Ephesus (449)** — which **Pope Leo I** branded the *Latrocinium* ("Robber
Council") — that rehabilitated Eutyches and humiliated his opponents. The death of Theodosius II and accession
of **[[eastern-roman-empire-425-518|Marcian]]** reversed everything: Marcian convened the **Council of
Chalcedon (451)**, the largest of all the councils, which **condemned Eutyches and Dioscorus** and adopted a
**Definition** — drawing on **Leo's Tome** — confessing Christ as **one person (hypostasis) in two natures,
unconfused and undivided**. This became the orthodoxy of Rome and Constantinople.

## The Monophysite schism

Chalcedon did not bring peace: vast numbers of monks and faithful — especially in **Egypt, Syria and
Armenia** — rejected its "two natures" as a betrayal of Cyril, becoming the **Monophysites** (Miaphysites).
Imperial attempts to heal the rift — **Zeno's Henotikon (482)**, which provoked the **Acacian Schism** with
Rome (484–519) — only multiplied divisions. **[[justinian|Justinian]]** (with Theodora protecting Monophysite
clergy) pursued reconciliation through the **Three Chapters** controversy: at the **Second Council of
Constantinople (553)** he condemned three Antiochene texts to placate the Monophysites — alienating the West
without winning back the East.

## Consequences

- `produced:` **Chalcedonian orthodoxy** — the shared christological foundation of the Catholic and Orthodox
  churches.
- `produced:` the **permanent Monophysite churches** (Coptic Egypt, Syrian Orthodox/Jacobite, Armenian
  Apostolic) and the **Nestorian Church of the East** — the enduring division of eastern Christianity.
- `produced:` recurrent **East–West ruptures** (the Acacian Schism; western anger over the Three Chapters).
- `enabled:`, in the longer view, the relative ease of the **[[muhammad-and-the-rise-of-islam|Arab
  conquest]]** of Egypt and Syria, whose alienated Monophysite populations had little loyalty to a
  persecuting Chalcedonian empire (a much-debated thesis).

## Historiography

CAH XIV stresses that "orthodoxy" was **constructed and contested**: both sides claimed Nicaea; emperors used
doctrine as an administrative tool; and the *meaning* of Chalcedon shifted between the fifth and sixth
centuries (neo-Chalcedonianism). The councils show the late-Roman state and Church **fused** — doctrine
defined by imperial convocation and enforced by law and force — yet unable to compel belief, the central
paradox of the Christian empire.

## From Tannous, *Making of the Medieval Middle East* (2018)

Tannous reframes the long aftermath of Chalcedon around [[simple-believers|simple believers]]: between Cyril–Nestorius (428) and Constantinople III (680–681), Middle Eastern Christianity splintered into competing churches (Chalcedonian; [[miaphysitism|Miaphysite]]; [[church-of-the-east|Church of the East]]; later Monothelete/Dyothelete split among Chalcedonians) over natures, persons, energies, and wills — while most adherents could not fully understand the speculation. Competition for simple Christians’ loyalties fueled polemic, translations, schools (e.g. [[qenneshre]]), and sacramental discipline. The resulting social landscape — confessional indifference, shared practice across labels, elite disciplinary frustration ([[jacob-of-edessa]]) — is the Christian world Arab conquerors found. See [[post-chalcedonian-confessional-formation]].

## From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

After Theodosius II’s death, Pulcheria and Aspar elevated **Marcian** (who married Pulcheria); Chalcedon **451** reversed Ephesus decisions and asserted dual natures of Christ; Leo of Rome opposed Monophysitism; Constantinople ranked second after Rome; enforcement was a state–Rome–Constantinople coalition culminating Theodosian religious policy; Marcian discontinued Attila-era tribute, reduced taxes, and left a treasury surplus by **457**.

Chalcedon redefined orthodoxy to halt Alexandrian–Antiochene dispute: one and the same Son perfect in Godhead and Manhood, consubstantial with Father as Godhead and with us as Manhood, like us apart from sin. Leo’s Tome: two natures unimpaired in one person; capable of death in one nature not the other. **Canon 28** placed Constantinople on a par with Rome as imperial cities — major East–West political rupture seed. Official two-natures doctrine permanently alienated Monophysite East; subsequent *Henotikon* and Justinianic compromises failed to heal.
