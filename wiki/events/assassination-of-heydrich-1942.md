# Assassination of Heydrich and the Lidice Reprisals (Operation Anthropoid, 1942)

## Narrative

[[heydrich-reinhard|Reinhard Heydrich]] — chief of the Security Police and SD, "the genius of the 'final solution'" in Shirer's phrase, "Hangman Heydrich" in the occupied lands — had himself appointed Acting Reich Protector of Bohemia and Moravia in September 1941, displacing Neurath. On the morning of 27 May 1942 (Shirer gives 29 May; 27 May is the established date), as he drove his open Mercedes from his country villa toward Hradčany Castle, a British-made bomb was thrown at the car by Jan Kubiš and Jozef Gabčík, soldiers of the Czechoslovak army-in-exile parachuted from an RAF aircraft (Operation Anthropoid, prepared with SOE). The bomb shattered his spine; Heydrich died of his wounds on 4 June 1942. The assassins and some 120 Czech resistance members were given refuge in the crypt of the Karel Boromejský (Karl Borromaeus) church in Prague, where the SS besieged and killed them to the last man on 18 June — without, Schellenberg later claimed, the Gestapo ever learning the actual assassins were among the dead.

## The reprisals

A hecatomb followed. Per a Gestapo report cited at Nuremberg, 1,331 Czechs (including 201 women) were immediately executed. Three thousand Jews were removed from the Theresienstadt ghetto and shipped east to extermination; Goebbels had 500 Berlin Jews arrested the day of the bombing, 152 executed on the day of Heydrich's death (Shirer 1960, ch. 27).

On 9–10 June 1942 the village of **Lidice**, near Kladno, was annihilated as a pure demonstration: 172 men and boys over sixteen shot in batches of ten behind the Horák farm (19 more villagers working in the Kladno mines were seized and killed in Prague); 7 women shot in Prague and the remaining 195 sent to Ravensbrück (7 gassed, 3 "disappeared", 42 dead of ill-treatment); the ~90 children sent to a camp at Gneisenau, a handful selected by "racial experts" for Germanization under German names (UNRRA reported 17 traced in Bavaria and returned by April 1947). The village was burned, dynamited, and leveled. Ležáky followed. The commander at Lidice, Max Rostock, was hanged in Prague in August 1951.

Shirer, who covered the postwar scene, remembered "reading the pitiful appeals in the then Allied-controlled German newspapers from the surviving mothers of Lidice asking the German people to help them locate their children" (eyewitness note, autumn 1945).

The Lidice pattern recurred elsewhere in occupied Europe — most infamously at **Oradour-sur-Glane** (10 June 1944, two years to the day after Lidice), where a detachment of the SS division Das Reich killed 642 inhabitants (245 women, 207 children, 190 men — figures per the 1953 French military court), machine-gunning the men in barns and burning the women and children in the church; the ruins were left standing as a memorial. Division commander Heinz Lammerding was condemned to death in absentia and never apprehended.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Heydrich's terror as Protector and the exile government's need (with British backing) to demonstrate Czech resistance after the humiliations of [[munich-agreement-1938]] and [[occupation-of-czecho-slovakia-1939]].
- `produced:` the reprisal massacres; the destruction of much of the domestic Czech resistance network.
- `enabled:` Allied propaganda mobilization — Lidice became a global byword; Britain (August 1942) and Free France formally repudiated Munich.
- `concurrent_with:` the escalation of [[operation-reinhard-1942]] — Heydrich's death gave the deportation program its commemorative name.

## Consequence Analysis

The assassination removed the most capable figure of the SS security empire (Kaltenbrunner succeeded him only in 1943) at the cost of savage collective punishment — a trade-off debated in the exile government at the time and by historians since. Its consequential scale is regional-to-civilizational through the propaganda effect and the Munich repudiation.

## Historiography

Shirer's account (ch. 27) rests on the Nuremberg record (Gestapo reprisal reports, the Czechoslovak government's official Lidice report) and Schellenberg's memoir; he dates the attack 29 May, against the documented 27 May — flagged here as a source discrepancy. Modern treatments (MacDonald, *The Killing of SS Obergruppenführer Reinhard Heydrich*; Gerwarth's Heydrich biography) refine the operational detail and the exile-politics context. The moral calculus of Anthropoid — a demonstrative act with foreseeable mass reprisals — remains a live debate in Czech historiography.
