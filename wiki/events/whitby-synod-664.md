# Synod of Whitby (664)

Council in Northumbria where the church of the region (founded from Iona) adopted the Roman calculation of Easter over the insular (Celtic) tradition. Pivotal in the alignment of Insular Christianity with Rome; marked by debate between Colman (Celtic) and Wilfrid (Roman).

## Historiography

The synod is known almost exclusively through Bede's *Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum* (731), composed by a Northumbrian monk who was himself a committed partisan of the Roman position and wrote two generations after the event; no surviving Ionan or British-tradition account presents the Celtic side's arguments in its own voice, so the debate as recorded (Wilfrid's arguments prevailing over Colman's) is necessarily seen through the eyes of the eventual winners. Bede's dating dispute framing (the Anatolian 84-year cycle versus the Victorian/Dionysiac 19-year cycle) is technically detailed and generally trusted by modern scholars as broadly accurate on the substance of the computistical argument, but his account of King Oswiu's reasoning and Colman's capitulation may be shaped by Bede's own theological commitments. Modern historians debate how decisive Whitby actually was in practice — Iona and Irish-influenced practice persisted in parts of Britain and Ireland well after 664 — versus its later symbolic status as the moment English Christianity aligned with Rome, a status Bede himself did much to construct.
