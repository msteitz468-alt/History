# Formation of the Xiongnu Empire (c. 209–198 BCE)

> *Related: [[modu-chanyu]] · [[xiongnu]] · [[qin-northern-campaigns-215bce]] · [[battle-of-baideng-200bce]] · [[heqin-policy]] · [[ordos-region]]*

The Xiongnu empire emerged between approximately 215 and 198 BCE in three overlapping phases: the Qin military conquest of the Ordos that destabilized the steppe political order; Modu's violent seizure of the chanyu title through patricide; and the rapid institution of a centralized pyramidal government. The resulting state — the first historically documented nomadic empire in world history — transformed East Asian geopolitics, inflicted a crushing defeat on the early [[han-dynasty|Han dynasty]], and forced China into a tributary relationship that lasted until Emperor Wu's aggressive campaigns began in 141 BCE.

---

## Narrative

### Act I — The Qin Expedition (215 BCE)

In 215 BCE the First Emperor of [[qin-dynasty|Qin]], having unified China the previous year, sent General Meng T'ien north with an army of one hundred thousand (or, in other sources, three hundred thousand) men to attack the "Hu" and seize the territory south of the Yellow River. *Shih chi* 110 records the expedition in three variant accounts. The most detailed (*Shih chi* 88, 2565–6) states:

> "After Ch'in unified the empire, [the emperor] sent Meng T'ien to lead an army of three hundred thousand men to the north to drive out the Jung and the Ti, and acquire the territory south of the Yellow River. He built long walls, and constructed fortifications taking advantage of passes, according to the configuration of the terrain, from Lin-t'ao to Liao-tung, stretching over a distance of more than ten thousand li. Then he crossed the Yellow River, and took possession of the Yang Mountains, which wind to the north like a snake."

The Jung and Ti named in this passage are archaisms for northern peoples generally; Di Cosmo notes the reference "may have been a classical allusion" meant to situate Meng T'ien in a tradition of northern expansion going back to the Warring States. The Xiongnu are not named explicitly, possibly reflecting "their relative lack of power and cohesion at this time." The expedition was the "first deep and massive conquest of nomadic territory by a Chinese state" at such scale, and its effects on the Xiongnu were probably devastating.

Meng T'ien was later forced to commit suicide in 210 BCE following the death of the First Emperor, and collapsed with the Qin itself. But the damage had been done: the Ordos had been occupied, forty-four walled county towns erected, garrisons installed, and the Great Wall extended. The Xiongnu had been expelled from their primary pasturelands.

### Act II — Crisis, Militarization, and Modu's Coup (c. 210–209 BCE)

The Qin invasion created a shortage of pastureland that upset the balance of power in the steppe. According to the *Shih chi*, "the Eastern Hu were very powerful and the Yüeh-chih were flourishing" at this moment — meaning that the Xiongnu, weakened by the loss of the Ordos, had become targets. This external crisis corresponded to an internal succession crisis in the Xiongnu ruling house.

Chanyu T'ou-man wished a son of another wife to succeed him and had sent his older son Modu as a hostage to the Wu-sun people, then attacked the Wu-sun hoping they would kill their hostage. Modu escaped, returned home, and his father gave him command of ten thousand cavalry — a decision that proved fatal. Di Cosmo reads the succession struggle as occurring "in a moment of crisis for Hsiung-nu society, as the expedition of Meng T'ien forced the Hsiung-nu to leave their pasturelands, and Modun's creation of an independent military force emerged out of the heightened military mobilization of Hsiung-nu society."

Modu trained his bodyguard through a series of escalating loyalty tests: shooting his favorite horse on command, then shooting his favorite wife, executing those who hesitated at each stage. Once absolute obedience had been demonstrated, he ordered the bodyguard to shoot T'ou-man. Having killed his father, Modu became chanyu around 209 BCE.

The plausible historical core of this narrative, Di Cosmo argues, consists of:
1. The creation of an efficient bodyguard analogous to Persian, Scythian, and later Turco-Mongol guard corps.
2. A coup d'état against the traditional tribal aristocracy.
3. The centralization of political and military power that followed.

The process of centralization "occurred between the invasion of the Ordos by Meng T'ien (215 b.c.) and the rise of Modun to supreme leadership (209 b.c.)."

### Act III — Institutional Structure (c. 209 BCE onward)

Upon becoming chanyu, Modu instituted a new centralized political structure described in detail by Ssu-ma Ch'ien:

> "There are the Left and Right Wise Kings, Left and Right Lu-li Kings, Left and Right Generals, Left and Right Commandants, Left and Right Household Administrators and Left and Right Ku-tu Marquises. . . . Starting from the Wise Kings of the Left and Right, down to the Household Administrators, the most important ones [command] ten thousand horsemen, the least important a few thousand; altogether they are referred to as the twenty-four high dignitaries (*erh-shih-ssu ta ch'en*)." (*Shih chi* 110, 2890–2)

Key structural features:
- **Dual wing organization**: Left (eastern) and Right (western), with the heir apparent titled "T'u-ch'i King of the Left."
- **Pyramidal hierarchy**: The twenty-four *ta ch'en* formed the supreme political council under the chanyu; these were not tribal representatives but "kings" exercising quasi-independent authority over territorial appanages.
- **Hereditary aristocratic clans**: Three families — Hu-yen, Lan, and Hsü-pu — monopolized the highest positions.
- **Decimal military structure**: Units of tens, hundreds, and thousands, each appointing their own sub-commanders; this structure persisted across later Inner Asian states.
- **Court location**: The chanyu's personal domain lay northeast of the Yellow River's Great Bend, in the area of the later Tai and Yün-chung commanderies. Lung-ch'eng (probably southwest of Ulan Bator, in present-day Mongolia) served as the site of the fifth-month sacrifices to ancestors, Heaven and Earth, and the deities.

### Territorial Expansion and the Tributary System

After consolidating internal power, Modu expanded rapidly:
- **East**: Defeated the Eastern Hu; extended the eastern wing from Shang-ku commandery to the Hui-mo and Ch'ao-hsien (northern Korea).
- **South**: Recovered the Ordos and territories south of the Yellow River that had been seized by Meng T'ien; conquered the Lou-fan and the king of Po-yang.
- **North**: Subjugated Hun-yü, Ch'ü-yi, Ting-ling, Ko-k'un, and Hsin-li peoples in northern Mongolia and western Siberia.
- **West** (under Modu's Right Wise King, 170s BCE): Destroyed the Yuezhi, driving them westward into Central Asia.

The Xiongnu adopted a tributary system to support the growing court and military establishment: city-states in the Tarim Basin, defeated nomadic peoples (such as the Wu-huan), and eventually the Han dynasty itself all paid tribute in fixed amounts of luxury goods and staples. Di Cosmo emphasizes that "the unity of the political system could survive only as long as tributes kept coming in."

### The Battle of Baideng and the Ho-ch'in Treaty (200–198 BCE)

The formation event culminates in the Han military catastrophe at [[battle-of-baideng-200bce|P'ing-ch'eng (200 BCE)]]. Modu encircled Han Kao-tsu's army for seven days before releasing it; the Han subsequently ratified the first [[heqin-policy|ho-ch'in treaty]] (198 BCE) committing China to annual tribute payments, diplomatic marriages, and recognition of the chanyu's equal status. Di Cosmo describes this as "a fundamental change in the Chinese conception of foreign relations" and the inauguration of a bipolar world order in which "China, the land of caps and girdles," and the Xiongnu, "all the people who draw the bow," divided the known world between them (*Shih chi* 110, 2902; treaty of 162 BCE).

## Causal Analysis

Di Cosmo's revisionist thesis — the central argument of Chapter 5 of *Ancient China and Its Enemies* — identifies the Qin invasion of the Ordos as the primary external catalyst. The conventional narrative portrays the Xiongnu empire as an outgrowth of nomadic raiding behavior contained by Qin's "defensive" response; Di Cosmo inverts this: the Qin expedition was an act of imperial expansion, not defensive necessity, and "the origin of the conflict between Chinese and Hsiung-nu that would later assume the shape of a war between a defensive China and aggressive nomadic invaders must be sought, more precisely, in the expansion of China into nomadic grazing grounds."

His three-stage causal model:

**Crisis** → The Ch'in invasion created a shortage of pastureland, weakened the Xiongnu vis-à-vis the Eastern Hu and Yuezhi, and triggered the succession crisis that produced Modu's coup. Ssu-ma Ch'ien himself identifies "crisis (*chi*)" as the catalyst for political action among the nomads at the opening of *Shih chi* 110.

**Militarization** → Crisis increased the military participation ratio of Xiongnu society: "every male able to fight became a soldier." The creation of Modu's bodyguard institutionalized professional soldiery and made full-time soldiers of what had been part-time fighters. The growing military establishment required external revenues to support itself, creating pressure for conquest.

**Centralization** → Modu's coup replaced the traditional tribal aristocracy with a pyramidal structure in which hereditary appanages were controlled by the chanyu's lineage and the consort clan, and all high dignitaries owed their positions to imperial appointment. The "tribe-state" that resulted was "not a state controlled by tribal constituencies, but a state that was structured like a tribe in terms of hierarchies and access to power."

Di Cosmo explicitly rejects economic dependency theory (the idea that the Xiongnu formed a state to extract agricultural goods from China by force) as "speculative" and unsupported by the written sources, which "instead point to the creation of a political coalition as a reaction against China's invasion of the nomads' territory."

## Consequence Analysis

1. **Nomadic state template**: The Xiongnu political structure — dual wing, twenty-four high dignitaries, decimal army, sacral investiture — became the founding template for all subsequent Inner Asian empires, from the Türks (who had twenty-eight high dignitaries) to the Mongols.

2. **Bipolar world order**: The recognition in the 162 BCE treaty of two distinct civilizational spheres — nomadic north, settled south — was a qualitative departure from all previous Chinese foreign relations. It acknowledged, for the first time, that China was not the universal civilizational center but one of two "great powers" with demarcated areas of influence.

3. **Ho-ch'in policy**: The tribute system the Xiongnu imposed forced the Han to devise new diplomatic concepts. The architect Liu Ching proposed that the marriage alliance would gradually reduce the chanyu's descendants to a subordinate status; in practice the policy preserved a fragile border stability for half a century while allowing the Han to rebuild its economy and army.

4. **Silk Roads**: The Xiongnu's political domination of Central Asia (the Western Regions), maintained through ruthless control of tributary city-states from the Tarim Basin westward, was the precondition for the trans-Eurasian trade networks the Han would later exploit. The Xiongnu pressure that drove the Yuezhi west ultimately produced the Kushan empire and the first trans-continental commercial axis.

5. **Han military reform**: The inability of Han infantry and chariots to match Xiongnu cavalry forced a long-term transformation of Chinese military doctrine, analyzed by Ch'ao Ts'o (d. 154 BCE) in a memorial to Emperor Wen that Di Cosmo calls "one of the first studies of the military capabilities of nomadic cavalry" in Chinese literature. This reform eventually enabled Emperor Wu's offensive campaigns.

## Actors

- **[[modu-chanyu|Modu Chanyu]]** — founder and first chanyu; the decisive agent of empire formation.
- **[[xiongnu|Xiongnu]]** — the confederation that Modu reorganized into an empire.
- **[[qin-dynasty|Qin dynasty]]** — the involuntary catalyst; General Meng T'ien's expedition displaced the Xiongnu from the Ordos.
- **[[han-dynasty|Han dynasty]]** — the defeated power whose tribute became essential to sustaining the empire.
- **[[liu-bang|Han Kao-tsu (Liu Bang)]]** — first Han emperor; defeated at Baideng; signed the first ho-ch'in treaty.
- **Eastern Hu (Tung Hu)** — first and most thoroughly defeated enemy of the new empire.
- **Yuezhi** — second major victim; their defeat by the Right Wise King in the 170s BCE opened the way for Xiongnu dominance of Central Asia.

## Historiography

The formation of the Xiongnu empire is actively debated on at least three levels.

**Was it a genuine state?** Nobuo Yamada argued the Xiongnu were a loose tribal confederation, not a state. Masao Mori saw it as produced by internal political process: the centralization of the chanyu's clan authority over other tribes. Di Cosmo argues for the "tribe-state" model — organized and functioning as a state but structured internally along hereditary tribal-clan lines. The typology of the "early state" developed by Claessen and Skalník (1978) would classify it as a "typical early state," in Di Cosmo's view.

**What caused it?** Three competing hypotheses: (1) internal political maturation of nomadic society (Eberhard's horse-breeding specialization model); (2) economic dependency on Chinese agricultural goods requiring political organization to enforce access (Barfield, Khazanov); (3) external catalyst of Qin military expansion (Di Cosmo). Di Cosmo specifically targets and refutes (2), noting that neither archaeological evidence nor written sources support the premise that nomads were primarily dependent on Chinese grain, and that the Xiongnu sources "point to the creation of a political coalition as a reaction against China's invasion."

**Was the Qin campaign defensive or expansionist?** The conventional view (Hucker) treats Meng T'ien's expedition as a defensive measure "in order to keep the Hsiung-nu out of raiding range." Di Cosmo argues this mischaracterizes what was in fact "the first deep and massive conquest of nomadic territory by a Chinese state" — a policy of imperial expansion that violated nomadic territorial integrity.

**Ethnic identity**: The language and ethnic affiliation of the Xiongnu remains unresolved. The eighteenth-century thesis connecting them to the European Huns is still debated but no longer accepted as proven; Di Cosmo notes "no evidence that the ruling elite of the Huns, which bore Germanic names, was related to the Asian Hsiung-nu," though a common linguistic root for the name Hsiung-nu / Huna / Hun is acknowledged.
