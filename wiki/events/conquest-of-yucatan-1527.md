# Conquest of Yucatán and the Southern Lowland Maya (1527–1697)

## Narrative

The Spanish conquest of the **lowland Maya** was not a single campaign analogous to the fall of [[tenochtitlan|Tenochtitlan]] but a **protracted, multi-phase process** lasting from first contacts (no later than **1511**) through the capture of the last independent capital, **Nojpeten** (Tayasal), on **13 March 1697** — a century and a half after the founding of Mérida. Grant D. Jones ([[cambridge-history-native-peoples-americas-v2-2|CHNPA II.2 Ch. 20]]) stresses vicious localized resistance, dual Spanish evacuations, genocidal methods in the southeast, and an interior resistance axis whose moral center was the **Itza** polity of Lake Petén Itzá.

### Early contacts (1511–1525)

Yucatán was first contacted by Spaniards no later than **1511** when a Panama→Santo Domingo ship foundered off the eastern coast. Mayas spared **Gerónimo de Aguilar** and nobleman **Gonzalo Guerrero**, living among natives when Cortés stopped at **Cozumel** en route to Veracruz. Expeditions of **1517** (Córdoba) and **1518** (Grijalva) apparently sought slaves for the labor-short Antilles: Córdoba forced ashore at Ekab’/Cabo Catoche, suffered Maya attacks, and sailed the north and west coasts to Chanputun; Grijalva claimed Cozumel, defeated a Maya attack at Campeche, and reached Laguna de Términos and Pánuco.

In **1519** Cortés at Cozumel ransomed Aguilar (fluent Maya). Aguilar and **Doña Marina** (trilingual Chontal-, Yucatec-, and Nahua-speaking, met in Tabasco) enabled the translation chain facilitating the [[spanish-conquest-of-mexico-1519|conquest of Tenochtitlan]]. In **1525**, after the fall of the Mexica capital, Cortés led a large exploratory party from Coatzacoalcos across Chontal Akalan, Kejach, and Itza territory toward Honduras; he met **AjKan Ek’** at Nojpeten, left a cross and a lame horse, and produced the first descriptions of broad Itza territorial/economic influence. The Itza were not officially recontacted by Franciscans for nearly a century — in 1618 Cortés’s cross was said still standing.

### Montejo’s project: 1527–1534 failures

**Francisco de Montejo** (Mexican-conquest participant) received the Crown patent to conquer Yucatán beginning **1527**. Mayas — already likely devastated by epidemics from earlier visitors — put up vicious resistance in the northeast and southeast. Peaceful Cozumel reception by ruler **Naum Pat**; short-lived Salamanca villa near Xelha. A six-month interior “pacification” met strong opposition in Chik’inchel (10–12 Spaniards and many more Mayas killed). Attempt on rich **Chetumal** for gold and a better villa; Guerrero, living there married to a Maya woman, refused to join the Spaniards and was accused of fomenting attack. False news of Dávila’s death; Dávila abandoned Xelha, moved the villa to Xamanha. Montejo left for New Spain; slaving missions from Xamanha to the West Indies.

In **1529** a broader mandate used Santa María de la Victoria (Tabasco) as base; Dávila assisted Montejo and **Francisco Montejo the Younger** in Tabasco conquest. **1530**: Dávila via Akalan to the west coast; friendly Chanputum; villa Salamanca at Campeche; **first encomiendas in Yucatán** issued. **1531**: Dávila across the peninsula to Chetumal (abandoned); Villa Real established then besieged — retreat to Honduras. By **1534** men abandoned for Peru gold; Spanish forces **evacuated Yucatán a second time**. By then Montejo had seriously disrupted Maya life: prisoners of war enslaved — **as many as 50,000** according to one source — exported to New Spain; epidemics already major; survivors braced for return.

### Final northern pacification (1540–1546)

In **1540** Montejo’s son and nephew moved again, better prepared; Mayas exhausted and decimated by disease. Founded **Mérida 1542** (soon attacked by eastern groups). **Campeche**, **Valladolid**, and **Salamanca de Bacalar** established by **1544**. Northernmost peninsula firm; little western resistance, much eastern. Southeastern provinces Chetumal, Waymil, and Tz’ul Winikob’ suffered especially harsh treatment by **Melchor and Alonso Pacheco**. Franciscan Lorenzo de Bienvenida (first Bacalar mission ~1546) compared Alonso Pacheco to Nero — dogs set on captives, breasts cut off women, hands/noses/ears off men, women drowned with squashes for amusement; towns of 500–1,000 houses reduced so that 100 was large; hunger after flight and unsown crops.

These genocidal methods plus early slavery and tribute produced **1546** widespread offensives around Bacalar and Valladolid; months to crush; eastern/SE territories left confused; many fled the unconquered interior. Thence through most of the colonial period, principal resistance came from an interior mix of runaways and hardened autonomists; the **moral if not geographic center was the Itza Mayas on Lake Petén Itzá**. Spanish policy was consciously designed for nearly two centuries to encircle Itza territory and isolate external trade — only partially successful; Itzas recruited frontier neighbors in a war of attrition against Spanish encroachments.

### Character of military resistance and conversion violence

Colonial Yucatán was born in violence and prolonged resistance the Spaniards were ill prepared for. Localized but fierce Maya battles showed sophisticated strategy, guerrilla tactics, and remarkable defensive/offensive skills. Ultimately no match for mounted Spaniards with firearms in the more open, drier, less forested north — rebellion forced underground or relocation to jungles. In southern uprisings, arms and horses were of little value; years before Spaniards learned forest warfare. Late-16th/17th-c. Dominicans in Verapaz tried unarmed Christianization of southern Petén Chols without permanent converts. Northern conversion was complex; supposed resistance to early missions met in **1562** by a harsh auto-da-fé into Maya “idolatry” by Franciscan (later bishop) **Fray Diego de Landa** ([[spiritual-conquest-and-colonial-church]]).

### K’atun diplomacy and the fall of Nojpeten (1618–1697)

Early 1600s Franciscans became convinced that until the Itza stronghold converted and accepted the Spanish crown, complete Christianization/pacification of the peninsula was impossible; Itzas were blamed for draining [[encomienda]] human resources via runaways. Using Maya millennial/**k’atun** prophecy as a weapon, Franciscans visited Nojpeten in **1618 and 1619**, expecting AjKan Ek’ to accept Christian timing of prophecies; he and his priests had a different interpretation — the time for capitulation had not yet come; the friars were lucky to escape.

In **1695** **Andrés de Avendaño** masterminded a second prophetic effort; another Kan Ek’ dynasty ruler initially accepted that **K’atun 8 Ajaw** (begin 1696 or 1697) was the time to enter the Christian fold, but enemies among the Itzas forced the issue; Avendaño in 1696 barely escaped. Colonial embarrassment → troops from Campeche. The Spanish captured Nojpeten in a bloody battle on **13 March 1697**; many defenders killed; survivors fled the forests; Spanish held an abandoned town; repopulation on the lake shores was a **dismal failure**.

The Itza conquest was actually an afterthought to a plan connecting Guatemala with Yucatán: **1695** Guatemalan troops “conquered” Chol communities including **Sakb’ajlan** (Nuestra Señora de los Dolores / Dolores del Lakandon) on the Lacantún River, eastern Chiapas; armed Itza pacification proceeded only after Avendaño convinced the Yucatán governor of the K’atun 8 Ajaw conversion window. Manche Chols, last eastern Cholan group remaining ethnically distinct and autonomous, were forcibly relocated to Alta Verapaz several times, with final removal shortly after 1697.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** Spanish imperial expansion after the fall of central Mexico and Crown patents to Montejo
- **contributed_to:** pre-existing Maya provincial fragmentation after Mayapan (Xiw–Kokom and multi-province structure)
- **contributed_to:** early contact [[virgin-soil-epidemics|epidemics]] and Spanish slave-raiding motives (Córdoba/Grijalva; Montejo-era export of captives)
- **contributed_to:** Spanish framing of Mayas as “New World Moors” requiring conversion and military pacification
- **enabled:** linguistic and military cooperation chains linking Yucatán contacts to the Mexican conquest (Aguilar–Marina)
- **preceded_by:** Postclassic political geography of northern provinces, Itza migration traditions from [[chichen-itza|Chichén Itzá]], and coastal trade networks

The conquest succeeded in the open north through horses, firearms, disease, and attrition; it failed for generations in the forested south, where Spanish arms were of little value and Itza k’atun ideology structured refusal and timing of negotiation.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** Spanish villas and colonial province of Yucatán (Mérida 1542; Campeche, Valladolid, Bacalar by 1544)
- **produced:** first Yucatán [[encomienda|encomiendas]] (from 1530) and long-lived tribute/labor extraction
- **produced:** massive early slavery export and demographic disruption before systematic counts
- **produced:** 1546 eastern/SE revolt and long-term flight into unconquered interior
- **produced:** nearly two centuries of Spanish encirclement policy against the Itza; final 1697 capture and demographic catastrophe (estimated **88%** dead in the Itza area, 1697–1706)
- **enabled:** mature colonial process of accommodation, avoidance, and resistance — [[lowland-maya-colonial-modern]]
- **contributed_to:** destruction of open ritual life, nobility structures, and coastal trade autonomy under colonial control
- **followed_by:** late colonial flare-ups (Sakalum 1624, Belize 1638, Kisteil 1761) and, far later, the [[caste-war-yucatan-1847|Caste War]]

## Actors

- **Spanish:** Francisco de Montejo (Elder); Francisco Montejo the Younger and nephew (1540 return); Alonso Dávila; Melchor and Alonso Pacheco; Cortés (peripheral contacts); Franciscans including Landa and Avendaño; Guatemalan forces in the 1695 Lacandón campaign.
- **Maya:** northern provincial lords (Xiw of Mani, Kokom of Sotuta, and others); Chetumal/Waymil/Tz’ul Winikob’ communities; Guerrero’s Chetumal network; AjKan Ek’ dynasty of Nojpeten; Manche Chols and Chol Lacandón communities; forest runaways who became the long-term resistance hinterland.
- **Intermediaries:** Gerónimo de Aguilar; Gonzalo Guerrero (refused reintegration).

## Historiography

Jones notes that published Spanish colonial sources for Yucatán are **less extensive** than for other Mesoamerican regions. **Robert Chamberlain** *The Conquest and Colonization of Yucatan* remains the most thorough study of the earliest Maya–Spanish encounter. Landmark primary texts: Tozzer’s edition of Diego de Landa *Relación de las cosas de Yucatán*; Garza et al. *Relaciones histórico-geográficas*; Scholes & Adams on the Don Diego Quijada idolatry investigation (1560s); López de Cogolludo *Historia de Yucatán* (1688); Villagutierre Soto-Mayor’s 1701 Itza conquest chronicle; Avendaño’s *Relación* of the 1696 Itza entrances; Valenzuela/Houwald documents on mid-1690s Chol Lacandón conquest. Native literature: Books of Chilam Balam (Edmonson translations controversial but introductory); Edmonson & Bricker guide to post-conquest Yucatecan Maya literature.

Jones’s own *Maya Resistance to Spanish Rule* and *Conquest of the Last Maya Kingdom* are the standard modern treatments of the southern frontier and 1697. Roys’s contact ethnography (*Indian Background*, *Political Geography*) is path-breaking; Quezada revises Roys with early colonial research. Clendinnen *Ambivalent Conquests* frames the mid-sixteenth-century Franciscan idolatry inquisition. Restall *Maya Conquistador* and *Maya World* center Maya-language perspectives. Ethnic historiography of southern Yucatecan speakers remains contested (Thompson’s “Chan Mayas” vs. Postclassic migration vs. deep Yucatecan antiquity — Jones favors great antiquity of Yucatecan presence in much of the southern lowlands).
