# Einstein's Exile from Germany (1933)

**Albert Einstein** was traveling in the United States when Hitler and the Nazi party
seized power in January 1933. Recognizing that return to Germany was untenable given his
Jewish identity, prominent pacifism, and public criticism of the new regime, Einstein
resigned from the Prussian Academy of Sciences, renounced his German citizenship, and
declined to return. His property in Germany was confiscated and his works were among
those burned and banned; Nazi propaganda vilified him by name.

## Settlement in the United States

Einstein accepted a position at the newly founded Institute for Advanced Study in
Princeton, New Jersey, where he remained for the rest of his life, becoming a US citizen
in 1940. His departure was part of a much larger wave of emigration of German-Jewish and
anti-Nazi scientists and intellectuals in the 1930s, which significantly enriched American
and British scientific and academic institutions while devastating German science — a
transfer of talent historians have cited as one of the notable indirect costs of Nazi
racial policy to German scientific preeminence.

## Historiography

Biographers (notably Walter Isaacson) present Einstein's 1933 break with Germany as
consistent with his lifelong nonconformity and civil-libertarian instincts, distinguishing
it from his later, more contested confrontations with American authorities during the Red
Scare (see [[events/einstein-red-scare-resistance-1953]]). The episode is frequently cited
in broader historiography of the "flight of talent" from Nazi Germany and its long-term
consequences for the postwar balance of scientific leadership between Europe and the
United States.
