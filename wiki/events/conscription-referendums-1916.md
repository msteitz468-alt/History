# Australian Conscription Referendums (1916–1917)

## Narrative

The decision to hold popular referendums on conscription for overseas military service was the most consequential political act of the First World War in Australia, producing what the source calls "the most acrimonious and enduring political schism in Australian political history."

**Context.** After very high voluntary enlistment — monthly totals over 35,000 in mid-1915 — rates collapsed to around 6,000 a month a year later. A November 1915 investigation by the Parliamentary War Committee revealed that over 600,000 eligible men had not volunteered. Nine "snowball" recruiting marches in New South Wales over summer 1915–16 attracted only around 1,000 men. In January 1916 Prime Minister [[hughes-billy]] travelled to Britain and returned convinced the empire needed 16,500 Australian men per month — "two-and-a-half times the number then volunteering."

**The legal option Hughes did not use.** Hughes was "constitutionally able to simply introduce conscription by legislation." Instead, "faced [with] trenchant opposition within his own party" he decided to seek popular endorsement. He misrepresented Australia's strategic situation in the campaign. Opening it at the Sydney Town Hall in September 1916, he spoke of "this great hour, when our country and all we hold dear are in deadly peril."

**First referendum (October 1916).** The referendum was narrowly defeated. Hughes was "not deterred" and pressed the Labor Party to hold a second referendum. The divisions within the party "were too great." Hughes then quit the government, taking 23 of the 65 Labor members of the Commonwealth parliament with him, and joined non-Labor members to form a Nationalist Party government.

**Second referendum (December 1917).** The May 1917 election confirmed the Nationalists in power. Hughes initiated a second referendum in December 1917, opening as thousands of Australian families received news of deaths in the Ypres offensive. It was again narrowly defeated, "a fact that came to bolster the claims for the special character of the Anzacs" — the AIF as a uniquely volunteer force.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: Decline in voluntary enlistment after high initial enthusiasm
- caused_by: British pressure and Hughes' personal commitment to the imperial war effort
- contributed_to: Hughes' personal ambition to remain the dominant wartime leader
- contributed_to: Constitutional blockage (Labor's referendum pledge rather than legislation)

## Social Divisions

Those voting **Yes** were predominantly "middle-class, Protestant, from English, Scots and Welsh backgrounds and traditionally voted for non-Labor parties." Those voting **No** were "more often from working-class, Irish Catholic backgrounds, and traditionally voted Labor."

**The Catholic factor.** Archbishop Daniel Mannix in Melbourne was "conspicuous" in the No campaign. The violent suppression of the Easter 1916 rebellion in Dublin by British forces "fanned Sinn Fein and Irish sympathies in Australia, adding further fuel to the No campaign."

**Women.** The source notes (drawing on Glenn Withers) that women crossed traditional lines to some extent: working-class women voted Yes "in greater numbers than their male brethren, anxious to ensure that their sons and husbands overseas received loyal support."

**Soldiers overseas.** Australian soldiers "too tended to divide on lines of class, background and religion" — they were not overwhelmingly in favour of conscription.

## Consequences

- Labor split into pro- and anti-conscription factions; State Labor parties "split everywhere except Queensland over the issue of conscription, condemning Labor to electoral defeat. In 1915 there had been six Labor governments in Australia; by the end of 1916 there was one left."
- The Nationalist Party formed; Hughes won the December 1919 election as "the Little Digger."
- Labor did not regain federal office until World War 2 (with a brief Depression exception).
- The post-war sense among returned men that "despite the nationalist bombast they had been let down by those on the home front" fuelled returned-soldier resentment and loyalist riots.
- The defeat of conscription became embedded in the [[anzac-legend]] as validating the AIF's purely volunteer status — though this was not the only all-volunteer force in the war.

## Historiography

The source (Garton and Stanley) contextualises the referendums within the broader sociology of wartime Australia — inflation, labour conflict, sectarian tension — rather than treating them as simply a test of patriotism. Glenn Withers' cliometric study (1982) on voting patterns is cited. The distinction between those who saw opponents as "traitors and shirkers" and those who saw conscriptionists as "profiteers and warmongers" reflects the depth of social polarisation. Stuart Macintyre's *Oxford History of Australia* Vol. 4 (1986) is the standard treatment of the subsequent political consequences.
