## Narrative

Tobacco (*Nicotiana tabacum* from Brazil and the Caribbean; *Nicotiana rustica* from Atlantic North America) was unknown in Africa before the Columbian Exchange that opened after 1492. Its introduction into the Kongo kingdom — analysed exhaustively by Clist (ch. 8) using over 1,300 pipe fragments recovered from the KongoKing excavations — was a two-stage process that separated the arrival of tobacco as a consumable from the adoption of the pipe as its delivery technology.

**First stage: tobacco without pipes (c. 1583–early seventeenth century).** The earliest Kongo sources are silent on tobacco. Neither Pigafetta/Lopes (1591, based on residence at [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]] 1579–83) nor Father Diogo's report (1583), nor Andrew Battell (who passed through Ngongo Mbata c. 1603) mention it. The first explicit testimony is Samuel Brun's 1612 account of Mbanza Soyo on the Atlantic coast, describing men consuming "magkay" (tobacco), but without specifying pipes. This suggests tobacco smoking, perhaps using rolled cigars, was practised at Atlantic ports before it penetrated the interior.

**Second stage: the pipe (c. 1595–1652, probably c. 1583–1612).** The oldest archaeological pipe fragments from Kindoki date a production context to the sixteenth century and the first half of the seventeenth century (Clist et al. 2015c). The first explicit written mention of pipes is Giacinto Brugiotti da Vetralla (1652–57): "cosi tenendo sospesa con una delle mani la pipa o carimbeo" — the Kikongo term *carimbeo* confirms the practice had already been named in the local language. By 1668, Guattini and Carli at Mbanza Mbamba described pipes "as big as a small cooking pot with a stem of two fathoms long, which were never exhausted."

By the 1683–88 period, Girolamo Merolla da Sorrento documented both men and women in Mbanza Soyo smoking long pipes in the streets and in church, with a particular emphasis on nobles as prominent smokers. His illustration of a noble being carried in a hammock while smoking, and of a noble couple sharing tobacco, confirms that pipe smoking had become a public signifier of aristocratic status within living memory of its introduction.

## The Atlantic Connection and Pipe Form

Clist (ch. 8) argues that the **long-stemmed form** of early Kongo pipes is the key to their origin. Long-stemmed pipes were not common in sixteenth-century southern Europe (where tobacco was more commonly snuffed); they derived from native North American models used by English settlers in coastal Virginia and North Carolina from the 1580s. English pipe production began c. 1575; Dutch production (from Amsterdam, Gouda, Leiden) began c. 1610, with English pipe-makers establishing the first Dutch workshops. English and Dutch traders were already operating at the Congo mouth and Kongo's Mpinda harbour by the late sixteenth century.

Critically, the archaeological record at Mbanza Kongo, Kindoki, and Ngongo Mbata yielded **very few imported pipes**. This contrasts sharply with El Mina on the Gold Coast, where imported English and Dutch pipes dominate the archaeological record. At Kongo sites, Kongo pipe-makers appear to have adopted the long-stemmed form almost immediately and produced their own versions, decorated with local geometric designs. Kongo pipes are characteristically thicker than their European contemporaries, with larger furnaces and almost entirely local decoration. The appropriation was swift and comprehensive.

## Pipe Typology and Social Differentiation

Clist (ch. 8) developed a furnace-based typology of Kongo pipes from sixteenth to eighteenth centuries using a corpus of over 1,300 fragments:

**Clay pipe types (Af1–Af8):**
- Types Af1, Af2, Af3 are the most common and widespread, found at both [[mbanza-kongo|Mbanza Kongo]] and Ngongo Mbata. Their simultaneous presence at the capital and major provincial centres from the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries is evidence of **cultural homogenization** under centralized governance.
- Types Af2C and Af4 may represent early experimental productions before standardization.
- Type Af6 (tentatively of southern origin; clay and fabric different from other types; distribution Luanda–Ngongo Mbata) suggests long-distance pipe exchange within the Atlantic zone.
- Iron oxide pigment was used inside geometric excisions on some Af1B and Af6 pipes, a deliberate colouring technique.

**Stone pipes:** Produced exclusively in and around Ngongo Mbata (Mbata province), exclusively from serpentinite quarried in the Mayombe area over 100 km away. **All datable stone pipe fragments are seventeenth century.** 358 stone pipe fragments at Ngongo Mbata; only 10 at Kindoki (70 km north); only 7 at Mbanza Kongo (112 km southwest). Those 7 at Mbanza Kongo were found at the Lumbu site — the quarter hosting the king's public decision-making court where the most important nobles gathered.

Clist (ch. 8) argues that stone pipes were **intra-elite status markers used by the Mwene Mbata** (ruler of the Mbata province) and his court to distinguish themselves within the kingdom's nobility — not primarily prestige goods desired by the central capital. The few at Lumbu in Mbanza Kongo likely represent the Mwene Mbata's presence at important royal councils. The discovery of a clay furnace at Kindoki made of grey-white clay to imitate the colour of stone pipes confirms the prestige hierarchy: stone pipes were desirable enough to warrant cheaper clay copies, just as luxury raffia cloth was copied in cheaper versions (and Dimba ceramics were copied in clumsier versions — see [[kongo-ceramic-tradition]]).

The characteristic tooth wear on three men buried at Kindoki and Ngongo Mbata, aged 20 to 35, from contexts dated to the seventeenth–eighteenth centuries, provides skeletal evidence for the intensity of pipe smoking that the written sources describe.

## The Typological Divide and Political Fragmentation

Clist (ch. 8) identifies a significant **typological break** separating pipes of the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries from later pipe production:

- **Phase 1 (c. 1595–c. 1718):** Both clay and stone pipes; long-stemmed; rich geometric decoration; standardized types (Af1–Af3) present across the kingdom; reflects political centralization.
- **Phase 2 (c. 1718 onward):** Clay only; no stone pipes; different forms; less standardized.

This break corresponds to the period of the civil wars (1641–1718) that destroyed the centralized Kongo state, particularly the Battle of Mbwila (1665) and its aftermath. The same divide appears in the glass bead and pottery records, confirming it as a material-culture watershed indexed to political collapse. After the [[kongo-decentralization|decentralization]] of the kingdom, the prestige infrastructure that had enabled elaborate pipe production — including the stone quarrying and transport networks centred on Ngongo Mbata — ceased to operate.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** The Columbian Exchange and the Atlantic commercial integration of the Kongo kingdom through Portuguese, English, and Dutch trade contacts. The kingdom's position as a cosmopolitan node — actively seeking foreign prestige goods — created receptive conditions for rapid adoption.

**contributed_to:** Kongo elite culture of display; Kongo nobility's proven capacity for rapid appropriation and local transformation of foreign material forms (paralleled in Christian objects, ceramics — see [[kongo-cosmopolitanism]]).

**produced:** A distinctive Kongo pipe tradition that spread homogeneously across the kingdom's provinces; a social differentiation between clay and stone pipes mapping onto provincial noble hierarchies; tobacco cultivation in Kongo and Angola; eventual democratization of smoking from nobles to commoners.

**preceded_by:** The introduction of Christianity and European material goods in the late fifteenth century — the structural model for Kongo elite appropriation of foreign technologies.

**followed_by:** [[kongo-decentralization]] — the political fragmentation that ended the centralized pipe-production system.

## Historiography

The history of tobacco in Africa has been studied primarily by Africanist archaeologists rather than historians of the Atlantic world. Laufer (1924) and Laufer et al. (1930) established the basic diffusion chronology; Alpern (1995) surveyed West African evidence. Clist's contribution (ch. 8) is the first systematic typological study of Kongo pipes as a corpus, combining over 1,300 fragments from three major sites with radiocarbon dating and historical source analysis.

Key uncertainties: no archaeological work has been conducted at Mbanza Soyo (where tobacco is first attested in 1612) or at most of the kingdom's central and southern provinces; the pipe corpus from Mbanza Kongo excavations used a different strategy from the northern province excavations. The Clist 2018 article provides the detailed typological analysis summarized in chapter 8.

The pipes' significance as markers of Atlantic integration parallels the contemporaneous evidence for the kingdom's adoption of Christianity, European cloth, and glass beads — all part of the same pattern of cosmopolitan appropriation that Clist describes (ch. 8) using the concept of **extraversion** (following Vos, ch. 10).
