# The Mongol Invasions of Japan (1274 and 1281)

The two great seaborne assaults by **Kublai Khan's [[mongol-empire|Mongol empire]]** on
[[japan|Japan]] — the only serious **foreign invasions** of the islands before 1945, and
the supreme test (and ultimately a cause of decline) of the [[kamakura-period|Kamakura
bakufu]]. From Mason & Caiger's [[mason-caiger-history-of-japan|*A History of Japan*]]
(ch. 9).

## Narrative

After making Peking his capital (1264), **Kublai Khan** demanded (from 1268) that the
"King" of Japan acknowledge Mongol overlordship. The Kyoto court wavered; the **Hōjō**
regents in Kamakura simply **refused to reply**, and later beheaded Mongol envoys.

- **1274 (Bun'ei).** A Mongol–Korean fleet landed at **Hakata Bay** in northwest Kyūshū —
  the very region the Japanese had fortified centuries earlier against a Korean attack that
  never came. The samurai faced better-armed veterans, but after a day's fighting the
  Korean seamen urged re-embarkation; a **storm that night scattered the fleet**, drowning
  perhaps as many as had fallen in battle.
- **The interval.** The Hōjō tightened control of Kyūshū and built a **stone rampart**
  along Hakata Bay (it took five years); the Mongols were meanwhile occupied conquering
  south China.
- **1281 (Kōan).** A far larger Mongol force landed on a broad Kyūshū front. The Japanese
  defensive line held for nearly two months until, on **15 August 1281**, a **typhoon**
  destroyed much of the fleet and army as it tried to withdraw — tens of thousands lost.

This second deliverance gave the storms their enduring name: the ***kamikaze*** ("divine
wind"), revived in 1944–45 for the suicide pilots of the Pacific War.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Kublai Khan's demand for submission** as the Mongol empire reached the
  Pacific edge of Eurasia (via conquered Korea).
- `caused_by:` the **Hōjō refusal** to acknowledge inferiority — and their execution of
  envoys, which guaranteed war.
- `contributed_to:` the outcome — **Japanese fortitude and prepared defenses** (the Hakata
  rampart) *and*, decisively, the **typhoons**.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **survival of Japanese independence** — the Mongols never returned,
  though fear of them lingered.
- `produced:` the **fiscal crisis of the bakufu**: a *defensive* victory left **no
  conquered land or booty** to reward the thousands of warriors, temples, and shrines who
  claimed credit, so the Hōjō "could not meet even legitimate demands for compensation,
  and its prestige was lowered" — a major cause of the bakufu's [[kamakura-period|decline]]
  and fall (1333).
- `enabled:` the powerful myth of **divine protection** of the sacred land — fuel for later
  Japanese exceptionalism and nationalism.

## Significance

A rare case where **weather changed world history**: the typhoons preserved Japan from the
Mongols, but the victory's very lack of spoils helped doom the regime that won it. The
**kamikaze** entered Japanese self-understanding as proof of a divinely shielded nation.
