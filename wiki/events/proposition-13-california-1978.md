# Proposition 13 (California, 6 June 1978)

On June 6, 1978, nearly two-thirds of California voters approved Proposition 13, a ballot initiative that rolled back assessed property values to 1975 levels, capped annual increases at 2% (or the rate of inflation), and required a two-thirds legislative vote for any new state taxes or local special taxes.

## Narrative

Howard Jarvis, a longtime antitax activist, led the campaign. The measure passed despite a competing legislative alternative (Proposition 8) and opposition from most elected officials, academics, and business leaders. Support was strongest among whites, homeowners, conservatives, and those expressing racial resentments. It immediately cut property tax revenue by nearly 60%, creating large deficits, forcing reliance on state aid, and hitting education funding hard. It decoupled taxes from current market values, benefiting longtime owners disproportionately.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Combination of soaring home values (assessments rising with market) and organized mobilization that framed taxes as waste and transfers to undeserving groups.

**contributed_to:** The measure succeeded by channeling broader economic anxiety and backlash into a simple tax-cut vehicle, even as polls showed continued public support for higher taxes on the wealthy and corporations.

**preceded_by:** Earlier failed conservative tax-cut efforts and the post-Watergate/Vietnam disillusionment with government.

## Consequence Analysis

Proposition 13 became the model for tax-limitation movements nationwide. It entrenched supermajority requirements, shifted fiscal power, and demonstrated that antitax measures could pass with broad voter support even when they produced visible austerity (especially in schools). It accelerated the decoupling of taxes from ability-to-pay or current values and strengthened the political identity of "taxpayers" as a counterweight to government.

## Actors

- Howard Jarvis and the taxpayer association
- California homeowners (primary beneficiaries)
- Public schools and local governments (primary losers)

## Historiography

Williamson (2025) stresses that Prop 13 was not primarily evidence of a sudden free-market or small-government ideology shift. Public opinion on specific programs remained supportive of government; the measure succeeded by promising relief to existing owners while imposing supermajority hurdles on future revenue. It benefited long-term (often white, suburban) homeowners at the expense of newer buyers, renters, and public services. The racial and class dimensions of the "taxpayer" mobilization are central to the analysis. Subsequent scholarship has documented the long-term effects on inequality in service provision and the precedent for using direct democracy to lock in fiscal constraints.
