# Battle of Cassel (1328)

The battle in which the Flemish rebels, after holding a strong defensive position on a hill near Cassel, abandoned their prepared stance and charged the French army of Philip VI, suffering a crushing defeat that ended the revolt.

## Narrative

The Flemish forces, drawn from maritime Flanders and the towns of Bruges and Ypres, took up a defensive position on a hillside outside Cassel after sighting the approaching French. Philip VI, commanding a large army that included the oriflamme, camped at the base of the hill and refused to attack uphill. Over several days the French harassed the Flemings with artillery and by burning the surrounding countryside and a local monastery, attempting to provoke them into leaving their strong position.

At the hottest part of the day, after the French had retired to rest, the Flemings launched a surprise attack without noise. They swept down, passed through many tents, and reached the king's tent before being beaten back by his soldiers. Philip was so shaken that he needed aid to arm himself. The Flemings were then forced back into a "crown formation" (circular defensive stand) on or near the hill. French knights, rallying around the royal banners, surrounded and defeated them. Leaders including Nikolaas Zannekin were killed. Other rebel armies disbanded upon hearing of the massacre. French casualties were very light.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the ongoing Flemish resistance to French overlordship and fiscal demands after the Courtrai victory and subsequent settlements.
- `contributed_to:` French refusal to assault the hill directly, combined with sustained harassment (burning lands, artillery) that eroded Flemish morale and discipline.
- The decisive factor was the Flemings' abandonment of their prepared defensive posture on the hill, exposing them to the French cavalry they had previously been able to resist from strong ground (as at Courtrai).

## Consequence Analysis

- Crushing defeat ended the 1323–1328 revolt; the rebels submitted.
- Heavy Flemish losses (contemporary claims up to 20,000 or roughly half the force per modern estimates); French losses minimal (as few as seventeen knights in some accounts).
- Restored French military prestige damaged since Courtrai. Unlike Courtrai and Mons-en-Pévèle, few contemporary Flemish sources mourn the defeat or favour the rebels.
- "unlike the battle of Courtrai and Mons-en-Pévèle, few contemporary descriptions of the battle of Cassel favor the Flemings or mourn their defeat." (DeVries range 05)
- Demonstrated the limits of even experienced infantry forces when they left strong defensive terrain and formation.

## Actors

- Flemish rebels: forces from maritime Flanders and the towns; leadership disputed in sources (one account names Zannekin).
- Philip VI of France: commanded in person; chose to wait and harass rather than attack uphill.

## Historiography

Accounts differ on leadership details and exact sequence, but the core narrative is consistent: Flemings held a defensible hill; French refused direct assault and used harassment; Flemings eventually charged and were defeated. The surprise attack reached Philip's tent before being repulsed; the Flemings fell back into a crown formation and were overwhelmed. DeVries stresses the contrast with Courtrai-style successes and argues it is more accurate to blame the infantry for abandoning their defensive doctrine than to credit the cavalry with vindication (contra Oman and Perroy). Source bias is noted: fewer pro-Flemish accounts survive or were written for this battle. Casualty figures vary widely (Flemish dead 9,000–22,000 in chronicles).
