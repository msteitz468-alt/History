## Narrative

### Background: The Insei System and Its Tensions

The Hogen and Heiji disturbances grew out of structural tensions within the insei (cloistered emperor) system of government shaped by Retired Emperor Shirakawa (abdicated 1087, governed until 1129). The system was effective in improving imperial family revenues and reducing Fujiwara power, but "led to tensions within the imperial family. The dominant role of the cloistered emperor in governmental matters as well as family affairs was resented by the reigning emperor and his entourage, and by junior retired emperors who were excluded from the powers of the senior position" ([[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]], p. 688). See [[heian-period]] for the broader context.

Shirakawa had used warrior clans as instruments to balance court factions, deliberately building up the Ise Heishi ([[taira-clan]]) as a counterweight to the [[minamoto-clan]] after the Later Three Years' War (1083–87). By the 1150s, both warrior clans had mature [[bushidan]] organizations capable of rapid military mobilization within or near the capital.

### Hogen Disturbance (1156)

**The factions:** Retired Emperor Sutoku sought military support after his father Toba fell critically ill and was expected to name Go-Shirakawa (not Sutoku's son) as successor. Sutoku allied with Fujiwara no Yorinaga — the failed candidate for regent, who "had been accused of causing the illness of which Emperor Konoe died in 1155 by sticking needles into the eyes of a statue of the emperor" (p. 689) and who harbored bitter grievances against his half-brother Tadamichi. Sutoku's military supporters included Minamoto no Tameyoshi (the Minamoto toryo) and Taira no Tadamasa.

Go-Shirakawa's faction comprised Taira no Kiyomori (Tadamori's son) and Minamoto no Yoshitomo (Tameyoshi's son, on bad terms with his father) — a remarkable alignment pitting father against son within the Minamoto.

**The battle:** "On the night of the eleventh, fighting erupted in a furious attack, launched at nightfall on Shirakawa palace by the forces of Go-Shirakawa. The attacking force included Kiyomori with about three hundred mounted men, Yoshitomo with two hundred, and Yoshiyasu with one hundred. They set fire to the palace, and Sutoku's men fled. Within a few hours both the battle and the war were over" (p. 690). Sutoku was captured and exiled to Sanuki. Yorinaga, hit by a stray arrow, died days later.

**The aftermath — executions:** "Some seventeen of Sutoku's warriors were — against three and a half centuries of bloodless precedent — condemned to execution by imperial order" (p. 690). This decision was urged by Go-Shirakawa's advisor Fujiwara no Michinori (Shinzei). Yoshitomo was ordered to execute his own father Tameyoshi; Kiyomori executed his uncle Tadamasa. "This tsuwamono-like disposition of prisoners stunned the court" (p. 690).

**The meager rewards:** Despite deciding the political future of the court, the warriors were poorly rewarded. Kiyomori received merely the governorship of Harima; Yoshitomo was appointed Provisional Head of the Left Horse Guards, "a post of minimal distinction and probably no power or remuneration whatever" (p. 691). This injustice fueled Yoshitomo's grievances.

### Heiji Disturbance (1159)

**Court politics after Hogen:** After Hogen, Go-Shirakawa's confidant Shinzei (Fujiwara no Michinori, 1106–60) dominated court administration. His power was threatened by the rise of a new Go-Shirakawa favorite, Fujiwara no Nobuyori (1133–60). Emperor Nijo (Go-Shirakawa's son, who succeeded him in 1158 and wished to rule independently) had his own faction — including Minamoto no Yorimasa (1104–80). The combination of Nobuyori's personal rivalry with Shinzei and Yoshitomo's resentment at meager Hogen rewards created a volatile coalition.

**The coup:** "Toward the end of the first year of the Heiji era, on a day corresponding to a date in January 1160, Taira no Kiyomori, in fulfillment of a vow, left the capital accompanied by his eldest son, Shigemori (1138–79), and a small body of followers on a pilgrimage to Kumano" (p. 692). Just five days after his departure, Nobuyori and Yoshitomo moved. They forced Go-Shirakawa to vacate his Sanjo palace and held both Go-Shirakawa and Emperor Nijo under house arrest at the Greater Imperial Palace. They fired the Sanjo palace and Shinzei's residence. Shinzei fled to Nara, was caught, attempted suicide, but was executed by his captors — his head brought back for public display.

**Kiyomori's counter:** Kiyomori received news of the events while on the Kii peninsula. He returned to his Rokuhara residence with roughly four hundred men. When Emperor Nijo, "disenchanted with Nobuyori's highhanded ways," slipped out of the imperial palace disguised as a woman and joined the Taira at Rokuhara, "Yoshitomo and Nobuyori were clearly defined as rebels and the Rokuhara Taira acquired the legitimacy of an imperial army" (p. 694). Go-Shirakawa also escaped and fled to the Ninnaji Temple.

**The battle and its outcome:** Taira forces under Shigemori attacked the Greater Imperial Palace. After "fierce fighting" they withdrew, luring the Minamoto out, then occupied the palace. "The dispossessed Minamoto advanced on Rokuhara, where Kiyomori waited to do battle with them, crushing them and sending them into headlong retreat toward their homelands in the east. After only a few hours of actual fighting between troops of several hundred horsemen, the conflict was over" (p. 694). Nobuyori was caught and executed near Rokuhara. Yoshitomo fled east and was betrayed and killed in Owari Province. His two older sons were executed. Yoritomo, thirteen years old, was spared at the plea of Kiyomori's stepmother, Ike no Zenni, and "banished to Izu, placed in the custody of a trusted Taira ally, Hojo Tokimasa" (p. 379). The infant Yoshitsune was assigned to the priesthood.

## Causal Analysis

Both disturbances had the same structural cause: the insei system created irresolvable succession conflicts that rulers could no longer manage through exile and maneuver alone. "The fraternal quarrels between Go-Shirakawa and Sutoku and between Tadamichi and Yorinaga would likely have been resolved in earlier centuries by maneuver, political manipulation, and a judicious use of exile, but the ready availability now of military forces was a temptation too great for either side to resist" (p. 689). The existence of mature warrior [[bushidan]] organizations near the capital made military resolution possible for the first time.

The Heiji Disturbance differed significantly from Hogen: "unlike the Hogen incident, its action was determined not by commands issued by emperors or court officials, but by warrior chieftains acting on their own personal ambitions" (p. 694). This marks a qualitative shift toward warrior initiative.

## Consequence Analysis

**Immediate consequences:**
- [[taira-no-kiyomori]] became supreme warrior figure, without serious rivals
- Minamoto leadership (Tameyoshi, Yoshitomo) destroyed; clan appeared finished
- Court demonstrated that it could not protect itself without warrior dependence
- Execution of warriors as criminals — rather than exile — established a new precedent

**Long-term consequences:**
- Kiyomori's two-decade dominance (1160–1181), culminating in his appointment as Chancellor (1167)
- The term bushi came into regular use for warriors "at about this time, when the military and political landscape was changing and warriors acted more on their own initiative" (p. 394)
- Yoritomo's survival in Izu laid the foundation for the [[genpei-war-1180]] and the [[kamakura-period]]
- "The events revealed to the warriors the powerlessness of the court and the first hints of the political realities of the warriors' military power" (p. 394)

## Historiography

The primary narrative sources are Hogen monogatari and Heiji monogatari — semifictional accounts compiled no later than the 13th century, surviving in Muromachi-period versions. These are literary works with dramatic shaping, though they preserve historical detail. [[cambridge-history-japan-v2-heian]] notes that "the narrative related here generally follows Hogen monogatari and Heiji monogatari" and cites documentary diary evidence (Heihanki of Taira no Nobunori, the diary Sakeiki of Minamoto no Tsuneyori) for cross-checking. The key interpretive question — whether these events mark "the beginning of warrior rule" — is treated as premature by the chapter's authors: the warriors were still dependent on imperial legitimacy even in their moment of triumph.
