## Narrative

The Siege of Port Arthur (August 1904–January 2, 1905) was the costliest Japanese land operation of the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]], and the fall of the fortress was one of the war's decisive turning points. Port Arthur — the "Gibraltar of the Far East, the great fortress which, symbolizing Russian determination in the China Seas, crowns the extremity of the Liao-tung Peninsula" — was both strategically essential (it was the base of Russia's Pacific Fleet) and symbolically loaded: the same Liaodong Peninsula Japan had been forced to return to China (and which Russia then leased) after the [[first-sino-japanese-war-1894-1895|Sino-Japanese War]].

General [[nogi-maresuke|Nogi Maresuke]]'s Third Army conducted a series of massed frontal assaults against heavily fortified Russian positions. [[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]'s private reaction to the siege was recorded by chamberlain Hinonishi Sukehiro: "I am sure that Port Arthur will fall, sooner or later, but it's terrible killing soldiers that way. Nogi's a good general, but the way he kills soldiers is really upsetting" (Keene, ch. 54). The siege lasted roughly five months, with devastating casualties on both sides.

### The Surrender

On January 2, 1905, the Russian garrison commander General Anatolii Stoessel telegraphed Czar Nicholas: "The Japanese are in possession of all our lines. We cannot hold out much longer; we shall have to surrender…. The constant struggle for eleven months has worn down our strength. Three-quarters of the garrison are in the hospitals or the cemeteries. The last quarter is holding no less than 27 versts and cannot take turns even to snatch a short rest. The men are shadows" (Keene, ch. 54).

General [[yamagata-aritomo|Yamagata Aritomo]] conveyed Emperor Meiji's wish that the defeated Russian officers retain their swords, in recognition of their "gallant conduct" — a gesture widely noted in European press as illustrating Japan's adherence to the laws of war.

### Political Impact in Russia

The fall of Port Arthur had immediate political consequences in Russia. On January 19, an attempt was made on the czar's life; on January 21, 140,000 workers paraded the streets of St. Petersburg in general strike. The Russian government began to pin its last hopes on the Baltic Fleet (still at Madagascar), which French diplomat Paléologue observed was "already on its way toward its fatal rendezvous with the Japanese fleet off Tsushima" (Keene, ch. 54).

## Causal Analysis

Port Arthur was Japan's most urgent strategic objective at the war's outset because the Russian Pacific Fleet based there threatened Japan's control of the sea lanes essential for supplying the Manchurian campaign. Blockading Port Arthur while conducting siege operations was simultaneously the most costly and the most necessary operation of the war. Nogi's frontal assault tactics were standard for the period but proved exceptionally costly against modern fortifications — a lesson European armies failed to fully absorb before 1914.

## Consequence Analysis

The fall of Port Arthur transferred the Port Arthur leasehold to Japan — the specific territory Japan had been forced to return in 1895. At [[treaty-of-portsmouth-1905|Portsmouth]], Russia formally ceded the leasehold as one of the three "absolutely indispensable" Japanese terms. The human cost — "tens of thousands of the men who had died in the repeated all-out attacks" — left deep psychological marks, expressed in [[nogi-maresuke|Nogi]]'s post-war poetry of shame and in the broader post-war disillusionment that characterized Japanese intellectual life after 1905.

## Historiography

Keene's account emphasizes the contrast between the public celebration of victory and the private anguish of both Emperor Meiji and General Nogi at the human cost. The siege is a central exhibit in the broader argument about the gap between the Meiji state's wartime narrative and the realities of industrial warfare. The magnanimous treatment of Russian prisoners is highlighted by both Japanese and foreign observers as evidence of Japan's absorption of the laws of war — a point that figured prominently in international commentary at the time.
