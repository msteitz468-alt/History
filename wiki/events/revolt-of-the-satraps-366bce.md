# The Revolt of the Satraps, c. 366–360 BCE

## Narrative

The **Revolt of the Satraps** was the loose series of rebellions by which several powerful
governors of the western Achaemenid empire — **Datames** in Cappadocia, **Ariobarzanes** in
Hellespontine Phrygia, **Orontes** in Armenia, and (to a calculated degree)
**[[mausolus|Mausolus]]** of Caria — defied or distanced themselves from
**[[artaxerxes-ii|Artaxerxes II]]** in the 360s. [[lewis-cah-v6-1994|CAH Vol. VI]] (Ch. 3,
Hornblower) stresses how poorly it is documented: Diodorus compresses a movement that
"surely lasted several years" into three chapters under a single year (362), so that
"Persian and satrapal history seem[s] more jerky and episodic" than it was.

## Interpretation: strain or strength?

Hornblower cautions against reading the revolt as proof of Persian "**decadence**" (see
[[achaemenid-empire]]). The rebellions were **uncoordinated**, riddled with **mutual
treachery**, and largely collapsed of their own accord — Datames was assassinated,
Orontes betrayed his fellows and returned to allegiance, and Mausolus, though he "shared"
in the revolt, remained essentially loyal. Diodorus' claim that "half the King's revenues
were cut off" is exaggerated. Within the framework of [[achaemenid-satrapy|satrapal
quasi-feudalism]], such "flexibility at the margins... can be seen as a sign of Persian
strength, not weakness": the system absorbed the unrest and the loyal western satraps
would line the Granicus against Alexander a generation later.

## Connections

The revolt overlapped with the chronic problem of **Egypt**, in revolt since c. 404 (see
[[late-period-egypt]]): the rebel Egyptian pharaohs and dissident satraps encouraged one
another, and Greek powers fished in the troubled waters — both **Sparta** (Agesilaus
serving Ariobarzanes for pay) and **Athens** (Timotheus) intervened in 366. The episode is
thus part of the wider fourth-century pattern in which the line between satrapal autonomy,
Greek mercenary service, and outright rebellion was blurred.

## Consequence Analysis

caused_by: the ambitions and insecurity of over-mighty western satraps; weak central grip
on the seaboard; encouragement from Egypt and Greece. produced: a wave of rebellions that
**failed for want of coordination**, demonstrating both the strains and the underlying
**resilience** of the Achaemenid system on the eve of its fall to Macedon.
