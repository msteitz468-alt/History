## Narrative

The Thirty Years War began in 1771 when Nguyen Nhac, a betel merchant and tax collector from the upland village of Tay Son in Binh Dinh Province, raised a rebellion at An Khe against the corrupt regency of Truong Phuc Loan, who dominated the child Nguyen Lord Nguyen Phuc Thuan at Phu Xuan. Nguyen Nhac drew support from uplanders, Chams, Chinese merchants, and impoverished Vietnamese peasants worn down by generations of conscription and taxation to sustain Nguyen military and Mekong-frontier ambitions. He seized the port of Qui Nhon in 1773 and, within two years, controlled territory from the Gia Dinh border in the south to Quang Nam in the north.

The war escalated in 1774 when the Trinh lord Trinh Sam, seeing opportunity in southern chaos, sent the general Hoang Ngu Phuc across the Gianh River; Phu Xuan, weakened by famine and factional collapse, fell to the Trinh with little resistance, and Truong Phuc Loan was captured and handed over. The surviving Nguyen Phuc leadership fled to Gia Dinh (Saigon), while Nguyen Nhac's forces, briefly caught between the advancing Trinh in the north and Nguyen Phuc counterattacks from the south, negotiated a nominal submission to the Trinh — rendered moot when an epidemic forced Hoang Ngu Phuc's withdrawal in 1775 and ended any further Trinh advance.

Nguyen Nhac proclaimed himself "King of Tay Son" at Cha Ban in 1776. His younger brother [[nguyen-hue|Nguyen Hue]] led a decisive 1777 campaign that captured Saigon and killed nearly the entire Nguyen Phuc royal family; only the teenage Nguyen Phuc Anh — later [[gia-long|Gia Long]] — escaped, beginning a decade-plus of exile, guerrilla resistance from Gia Dinh, and periods of refuge in Siam. Nguyen Phuc Anh proclaimed himself king at Saigon in 1780 but was driven out repeatedly by Tay Son counter-invasions (1782–1783, 1785). In 1785 he appealed to King Chakri of Siam for a rescue expedition; the resulting Siamese fleet was destroyed by Nguyen Hue at the Battle of Rach Gam-Xoai Mut, sending Nguyen Phuc Anh back into Siamese exile.

Meanwhile the Tay Son turned north: in 1786 Nguyen Hue seized Thuan Hoa and then, without his elder brother's authorization, marched on Ke Cho, ending Trinh rule (Trinh Khai's suicide) under the banner of "restoring the Le." The brothers fell out over this insubordination, fought each other in 1786–1787, and partitioned Tay Son territory — Nguyen Hue taking Thuan Hoa and Quang Nam, Nguyen Nhac retaining Binh Dinh as "emperor of the center." Nguyen Phuc Anh, exploiting the split, slipped back from Siam in 1787 and began retaking Gia Dinh. In 1788, when the Le dowager appealed to the Qing court, a Qing expeditionary force occupied Ke Cho to restore the Le king; Nguyen Hue, having proclaimed himself emperor (the Quang Trung reign title) at Phu Xuan, marched north and routed the Qing army at the New Year 1789 in the celebrated victory of Dong Da/Ngoc Hoi, then negotiated a face-saving peace with the Qing court.

Nguyen Hue ruled from Phu Xuan until his sudden death in 1792 at age 40, leaving an 11-year-old heir, Nguyen Toan, under a regency wracked by generals' feuds. Nguyen Phuc Anh, meanwhile, spent the 1780s–1790s methodically building an administration, navy, and army at Saigon — with French volunteers recruited by the missionary Pierre Pigneau, Chinese, Cham, Khmer, and Siamese auxiliaries, and an innovative shipbuilding industry — and campaigning northward each year with the seasonal monsoon winds. He took Qui Nhon and built the fortress of Dien Khanh (1793), held it through repeated Tay Son sieges, took Binh Dinh (renamed for "pacified," 1799–1802), captured Phu Xuan (1801), and finally took Ke Cho in 1802 without a battle, ending Tay Son rule and Nguyen Toan's life. He then proclaimed the reign title Gia Long, founding the [[nguyen-dynasty|Nguyen dynasty]] as the first ruler of a unified Vietnam stretching from the Qing border to the Gulf of Siam.

## Causal Analysis

The rebellion's proximate cause was Truong Phuc Loan's corrupt regency and the accumulated fiscal exhaustion of the Nguyen Phuc state, itself `contributed_to` by decades of currency debasement (copper-to-zinc coinage, inflation) and heavy wartime taxation of Binh Dinh Province specifically. The rebellion's rapid escalation into a war destroying both ruling houses was `enabled` by the simultaneous, structurally unrelated exhaustion of Trinh government in the north — a population and administration, per Taylor, worn down by generations of failed reform and eunuch misrule, with no capacity to seize the opportunity southern chaos presented even when Trinh armies briefly occupied Phu Xuan in 1774–1775. Nguyen Hue's insubordinate 1786 march on Ke Cho `caused` the final destruction of Trinh rule, an outcome Nguyen Nhac had not intended or wanted.

## Consequence Analysis

The war `produced` the political and territorial unification of Vietnam for the first time under a single regime (briefly under the Tay Son, permanently under Gia Long), `produced` the modern southern border of Vietnam via consolidation of Nguyen-Phuc-era Mekong delta annexations, and `enabled` the definitive incorporation of the far south (Gia Dinh/Saigon, the Mekong delta) into a unified Vietnamese state rather than remaining a separate southern polity. It also `contributed_to` the eighteenth-century emergence of a Siamese-Vietnamese rivalry over Cambodia that would continue into the nineteenth century. Taylor stresses a further consequence often overlooked in nationalist historiography: the north's near-total passivity throughout the war — "northerners... played hardly any role at all in these events" — marking the definitive political eclipse of the Red River plain as the seat of Vietnamese power, a role it would not fully regain.

## Historiography

Sourced from K. W. Taylor, *A History of the Vietnamese* (Cambridge, 2013), which draws on Vietnamese court annals from both the Tay Son and later Nguyen dynastic perspectives (the latter necessarily shaped by Gia Long's post-1802 destruction of Tay Son records and disinterment/execution of Nguyen Hue's remains and descendants — a source-reliability problem Taylor notes explicitly, e.g. regarding claimed Tay Son use of vernacular Nom in administration, evidence for which "supposedly" was destroyed by Nguyen Phuc Anh). Taylor's interpretive framing — the Tay Son as a failed regional experiment in unification rather than a proto-national liberation movement, and Nguyen Nhac's provincialism as a legitimate contemporary alternative to pan-Vietnamese unity rather than parochial obstruction — runs pointedly against standard Vietnamese nationalist historiography, which celebrates Nguyen Hue/Quang Trung as a founding national hero for his victories over Siam and the Qing. This is an attributed interpretive stance, not adjudicated consensus, and should be read alongside more nationalist Vietnamese secondary literature not yet ingested here.
