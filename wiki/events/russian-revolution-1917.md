# The Russian Revolution (1917)

The **Russian Revolution** of 1917 destroyed the tsarist autocracy and the liberal Provisional
Government that replaced it, and brought the **Bolsheviks** to power — founding the world's
first **communist state** and a new ideological pole that would shape the whole twentieth
century. Deep-read from [[new-cambridge-modern-history-v12|*The New Cambridge Modern History*,
Vol. XII]] (Isaac Deutscher's chapter).

## Narrative

The First World War broke a regime already strained. The **[[february-revolution-1917|February Revolution (1917)]]** — bread riots and strikes in **[[saint-petersburg|Petrograd]]**, the mutiny of the garrison — toppled **[[nicholas-ii|Nicholas II]]**; a liberal **[[provisional-government|Provisional Government]]** shared uneasy **[[dual-power-1917|Dual Power]]** with the **[[petrograd-soviet|Petrograd Soviet]]** (workers' and soldiers' councils). Its fatal choice to continue the war, amid land hunger and economic collapse, eroded its support. **[[lenin-vladimir|Vladimir Lenin]]** (returned in April) and the **[[bolshevik-party|Bolshevik Party]]**, with the slogan "Peace, Land, Bread" and "All power to the soviets," seized power in the **[[october-revolution-1917|October Revolution (1917)]]**. They made peace with Germany (**[[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918|Treaty of Brest-Litovsk]]**, 1918), but faced a brutal **[[russian-civil-war-1918|Russian Civil War (1918–1920)]]** against the "Whites" and Allied intervention, which the Red Army (under **[[trotsky-leon|Leon Trotsky]]**) won — at the cost of famine, terror, and **[[war-communism|War Communism]]**. Paxton and Hessler ([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]], pp. 117–120) put the scale of Allied intervention at over 100,000 troops from fourteen countries: British, French, and American forces at Murmansk and Archangel; over 72,000 Japanese and 7,000 American troops at Vladivostok; British forces in the Caucasus; French and Greek troops at Odessa. The civil war spilled into a Russo-Polish War in which the Red commander Mikhail Tukhachevsky drove to the gates of Warsaw before being repulsed; the **Peace of Riga** (March 1921) ended that war and fixed the Polish–Soviet border until 1939. The **[[soviet-union|Soviet Union (USSR)]]** was formally organized in December 1922 as a nominally federal but in practice unitary state — Russia, White Russia (Belarus), Ukraine, and Transcaucasia at founding, joined by Uzbekistan, Turkestan, and Kazakhstan from 1925 — with real power centralized in Moscow throughout ([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]]).

## Causal Analysis

Deutscher (NCMH XII) stresses deep **structural** causes alongside the war:

- `caused_by:` the **strains of the First World War** — mass mobilization that stripped farming
  of "nearly half its fit manpower," military defeat, and runaway **inflation** (the cost of
  living at 700% of pre-war by 1917).
- `caused_by:` **peasant land hunger** (the Stolypin reforms too little, undone by war) and
  **industrial backwardness** — yet with a paradoxically **highly concentrated proletariat**
  (over half in factories of 500+), giving the workers exceptional organizational and "striking
  power" in the revolution.
- `caused_by:` the **bankruptcy and rigidity of the tsarist autocracy**, discredited and
  financially broken.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **fall of the tsar and the Provisional Government** and the **Bolshevik
  seizure of power** — the world's first avowedly communist state.
- `produced:` the **Civil War**, "war communism," the Red Terror, and the founding of the
  **USSR (1922)** — a one-party dictatorship that would industrialize under Stalin.
- `produced:` a **new global ideological pole** — the inspiration and organization of world
  communism (the Comintern), the great twentieth-century rival to liberal capitalism and (later)
  fascism, and the antagonist of the [[cold-war|Cold War]].
- `produced:` the **[[comintern|Third International (Comintern)]]**, founded March 1919, and — via
  its July 1920 Twenty-One Points — the definitive split of European socialism into a communist
  and a social-democratic wing ([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]]).
- Its failure to **spread** (the crushing of the Spartakists in Berlin and the short-lived
  Bavarian and Hungarian soviet republics) left the revolution isolated in Russia. `contrasts_with:`
  **[[kun-bela|Béla Kun's]]** failed Hungarian Soviet Republic (1919) — see Historiography.

## Actors

- The [[bolshevik-party|Bolsheviks]] ([[lenin-vladimir|Vladimir Lenin]], [[trotsky-leon|Leon Trotsky]]) and the [[petrograd-soviet|soviets]], against the [[romanov-dynasty|tsarist old regime]] ([[nicholas-ii|Nicholas II]]) and then the [[provisional-government|Provisional Government]]; the peasantry and concentrated industrial proletariat as the social base; the [[white-movement|Whites]] and Allied interventionists in the [[russian-civil-war-1918|civil war]]. ## Historiography

The classic divide is between the **liberal/totalitarian** reading (October as a Bolshevik coup
imposing dictatorship) and the **social-historical** reading (a genuine popular revolution from
below); Deutscher's Marxist account stresses the structural ripeness of Russia. The
relationship between the revolution and the later Stalinist terror — continuity or betrayal —
remains the deepest dispute.

**Why Russia and not elsewhere: a comparative precondition analysis.** Paxton and Hessler
([[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]], Ch. 5, "The Aftermath," pp. 133–137) frame Feb/Oct 1917
comparatively, asking why social revolution succeeded only in Russia and not in the other
war-shattered states of 1917–1920. Their answer identifies two preconditions: (1) a
**land-hungry peasant mass** willing to seize landed estates outright — in contrast to Britain
and France, where a stratum of small landholding farmers was, in Paxton's phrase, "inert if not
hostile" to urban revolutionary unrest; and (2) a regime that had come to **offend national
pride**, which was "quickly swept aside" once the war discredited it — nationalism, not class
dynamics alone, decided which regimes fell. They quote Trotsky: "History has moved along the
line of least resistance. The revolutionary epoch has made its incursion through the least
barricaded gates." Russia possessed both preconditions in extreme form; most of the rest of
Europe possessed neither.

This comparative frame sharpens the contrast Paxton draws between **[[lenin-vladimir|Lenin]]**
and **Rosa Luxemburg** on the land question — and, by extension, between the Russian and
Hungarian revolutions. Lenin's pragmatism tolerated the peasants' spontaneous seizure of estates
even while nominally "nationalizing" land on paper, marrying Bolshevik power to the peasantry's
own agrarian revolution. Luxemburg, by contrast, objected on principle: land should be
nationalized and managed collectively, not simply handed over to peasant proprietors. Paxton
treats this Lenin/Luxemburg divide as the conceptual key to why **[[kun-bela|Béla Kun]]**'s
Hungarian Soviet Republic collapsed within months in 1919 (`contrasts_with`) — Kun's regime,
lacking Lenin's willingness to concede land to a land-hungry peasantry, never secured the rural
base that anchored the Bolsheviks in Russia.
