# Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact
## Narrative
On August 23, 1939, Soviet Foreign Commissar Viacheslav Molotov and German Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop signed a treaty of strict neutrality and nonaggression in Moscow. While the public treaty declared peaceful relations between the two ideologically opposed regimes, it was accompanied by a **secret protocol** that divided Eastern Europe into spheres of influence. Strengthened by the pact, Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939. On September 3, Great Britain and France declared war on Germany, initiating the Second World War. On September 17, Soviet troops entered eastern Poland, and subsequently annexed the Baltic states and Bessarabia.

From Cambridge History of Russia Vol. III (Barber/Harrison, ll. 6006–6011): The pact 'bought two more years of peace, although this was peace only in a relative sense and was mainly used for further war preparations. While selling war materials to Germany Stalin assimilated eastern Poland, annexed the Baltic states and the northern part of Romania, attacked Finland and continued to expand war production and military enrolment. In the summer of 1940 Hitler decided to end the “peace”... he launched the greatest land invasion force in history against the Soviet Union.' `contributed_to` Soviet territorial gains and preparations; `followed_by` 1941 invasion (preceded_by pact). Added to Narrative/Causal.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` the **extreme Soviet suspicion of the Western democracies** following the Munich Agreement (1938), from which the USSR was excluded (Line 6243); the **failure of Anglo-French-Soviet negotiations** in the summer of 1939 due to Western hesitation and Polish refusal to allow Red Army transit; and **Stalin's desire to divert the impending war** away from Soviet borders while gaining territorial buffers.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the **outbreak of World War II** by freeing Hitler to invade Poland without fear of a two-front war.
- `produced:` the **Soviet occupation and partition of Poland**, incorporating western Ukraine and western Belarus into the USSR (Line 2577).
- `produced:` the **Soviet annexation of the Baltic states** (Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania) in July 1940, and the annexation of **Bessarabia and northern Bukovina** from Romania in the summer of 1940.
- `followed_by:` the **Finnish Winter War (1939–1940)** after Finland rejected Soviet territorial demands to move the boundary near Leningrad.
- `concurred_with:` a temporary economic cooperation where the Soviet Union exported grain and raw materials to Germany in exchange for industrial equipment.

## Actors
- **Joseph Stalin**, who authorized the alliance; **Viacheslav Molotov**, who signed the treaty; and Nazi Germany's Foreign Minister **Joachim von Ribbentrop**. See [[stalin-joseph]], [[molotov-viacheslav]].

## Historiography
Riasanovsky notes that both Hitler and Stalin signed the pact without any illusions, expecting temporary advantages (Line 6255). Molotov's replacement of Maxim Litvinov (who was Jewish and associated with collective security) in May 1939 had signaled this sudden shift in Soviet alignment.

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

Additions (ingested 2026-07-02), which call the treaty the "Nazi-Soviet
Pact" throughout: the secret protocol, signed August 23 and announced
August 22, 1939, divided Eastern Europe between the two powers — the USSR
was to receive Finland, Estonia, Latvia, most of Lithuania, eastern Poland,
and Bessarabia; Germany was to receive the rest of Poland and Lithuania.

**Causal chain**: German-Soviet economic negotiations began in spring 1939;
Stalin's dismissal of the pro-League, Jewish foreign minister Maxim Litvinov
on May 3, 1939 signaled the coming reorientation (already noted above); the
negotiations became serious only in August 1939, once German diplomats
pressed urgently, convincing Soviet leadership that a German invasion of
Poland was imminent. Stalin's rationale, per the source's framing: the
Allies offered "war without gain" — mobilizing to defend Poland alongside
Britain and France — while Hitler offered "gain without war" — neutrality
plus territorial expansion — an "obvious opportunistic choice." A
contributing factor was the ongoing Soviet-Japanese border fighting in
Manchuria (Nomonhan/Khalkhin Gol), 1938–39, which made a two-front scenario
unattractive to Moscow.

The British and French Moscow negotiations of early summer 1939 sent
low-level officials by slow boat, cited by critics as evidence of
insufficient seriousness — though Paxton and Hessler note genuine
structural difficulties underneath the optics: Poland and Romania refused
transit rights for Soviet troops, and Britain and France would not force
the issue; Western governments also suspected Stalin's proposed "indirect
aggression" clause was a pretext to annex the Baltic states.

**Immediate sequel**: Chamberlain signed the formal Anglo-Polish guarantee
on August 25, 1939; Hitler briefly delayed the invasion, hoping for British
neutrality or Mussolini's mediation, but ordered the invasion for September
1 after judging Chamberlain was bluffing. Britain and France declared war
on September 3, 1939.

Link discipline: `caused_by: [[appeasement]]`'s failure is too strong a
claim for this pact — it is better framed as `contributed_to`, since
Western distrust of the USSR and the refusal to grant Poland and Romania
transit rights were necessary but not sufficient conditions. The decisive
reason, per the source, was that "Hitler could offer Stalin something the
Western democracies could not" — territorial gain without war. Source:
[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## From Evans, *The Third Reich in Power* (2005)

Additions (ingested 2026-07-02): [[ribbentrop-joachim-von|Ribbentrop]] pushed
hardest for the Soviet option and signed in Moscow in the early hours of
24 August 1939; on the Soviet side the Red Army's post-purge unreadiness
drove the need for time. Goebbels's diary: "The Leader has made a brilliant
move." Evans notes that though the pact lasted under two years, "the
boundary it drew in Poland... was to prove permanent, while the Soviet
occupation of the Baltic states was to last until near the end of the
twentieth century." A grim consequence he documents: ~4,000 German nationals
in the USSR — including 1,000–1,200 Communists and Jewish exiles — were
handed over to the Gestapo by Stalin's regime (Margarete Buber-Neumann to
Ravensbrück; the composer Hans Walter David handed over in May 1940 and
murdered by the SS). European Communist parties, initially stunned, fell in
line. Source: [[evans-third-reich-in-power-2005]]

## From Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008) — the Soviet zone in Poland

The USSR occupied 201,000 km² with 13 million people from 17 September 1939 (Stalin having waited to close out the Nomonhan conflict with Japan). Of ~200,000 Polish POWs, ~15,000 officers and officials were deported into the USSR; in April–May 1940 the NKVD shot them, ~4,443 individually in the back of the head at Katyn — see [[katyn-massacre-1940]]. Roughly half a million Poles were imprisoned; ~1.5 million deported in cattle trucks to Kazakhstan and Siberia in the first half of 1940 (targets included political-party members, officers, Catholic laity, landowners, refugees, even "esperantists or philatelists"); perhaps a third died before the post-Barbarossa amnesty. Jews (1.2 million residents plus 350,000 refugees from the German zone) initially welcomed Soviet rule as protection from both German racism and Polish antisemitism — feeding the "Judeo-Bolshevism" trope — but repression soon reached them: as many as one in three Polish deportees to Siberia was Jewish (~100,000 deaths). Evans's comparative judgment: "From Stalin's point of view, what was being carried out in occupied Poland was a social revolution for the benefit of the majority; from Hitler's point of view . . . an ethnic revolution for the benefit of a small minority." `contrasts_with: [[germanization-of-occupied-poland-1939-1944]]`. Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

**Documentary run-up.** Ribbentrop's "Case White" order of 3 April 1939 (top secret, 5 copies) set 1 September 1939 as the operational trigger date for the Polish invasion: "Preparations must be made in such a way that the operation can be carried out at any time from September 1, 1939, onward" — the pact negotiations that followed were conducted against this fixed deadline, not the reverse (Shirer 1960).

At the 23 May 1939 Chancellery conference (14 officers present, including Göring, Raeder, Brauchitsch, Halder, Keitel; minutes taken in shorthand by adjutant Lt. Col. Rudolf Schmundt, no other copies made), Hitler stated plainly: "Danzig is not the subject of the dispute at all. It is a question of expanding our living space in the East... There is no question of sparing Poland and we are left with the decision: To attack Poland at the first suitable opportunity... We cannot expect a repetition of the Czech affair. There will be war. Our task is to isolate Poland." He also ordered total secrecy even from the Army General Staff itself: "Our studies must not be left to the General Staff. Secrecy would then no longer be assured." Shirer frames this meeting explicitly as a second, larger repeat of the 5 November 1937 [[hossbach-memorandum-1937|Hossbach conference]], with the crucial difference that by 1939 the generals offered no objection at all (Shirer 1960).

**The telegram exchange.** Hitler's telegram to Stalin, 20 August 1939, personally requested Ribbentrop be received urgently: "I therefore again propose that you receive my Foreign Minister on Tuesday, August 22, but at the latest on Wednesday, August 23... A crisis may arise any day," signed "ADOLF HITLER." Stalin's reply, received 9:35 p.m. on 21 August: "I thank you for the letter... The Soviet Government have instructed me to inform you that they agree to Herr von Ribbentrop's arriving in Moscow on August 23. J. STALIN" (Shirer 1960). [EYEWITNESS] Shirer reports hearing the announcement personally over German radio shortly after 11 p.m. Berlin time that same night, when a music program was interrupted: "The Reich government and the Soviet government have agreed to conclude a pact of nonaggression with each other. The Reich Minister for Foreign Affairs will arrive in Moscow on Wednesday, August 23, for the conclusion of the negotiations."

**Secret Protocol text.** Shirer gives the Secret Additional Protocol verbatim: the Baltic states' spheres were divided at Lithuania's northern frontier; Poland's spheres were divided along the Narew–Vistula–San river line; the Polish state's continued independent existence "can be definitely determined only in the course of further political developments," to be resolved by "friendly understanding" between Germany and the USSR. Bessarabia was noted as a Soviet sphere of interest, with Germany disclaiming interest there — a concession Ribbentrop "later was to regret" (Shirer 1960).

**The signing.** At the Kremlin signing (night of 23 August), per a secret German delegation memorandum, Stalin proposed a toast to Hitler — "I know how much the German nation loves its Fuehrer. I should therefore like to drink to his health" — and, as Ribbentrop was leaving, took him aside to say the Soviet government took the pact "very seriously" and could "guarantee on his word of honor that the Soviet Union would not betray its partner" (Shirer 1960).

**Stalin's later justifications.** Stalin defended the pact in a broadcast of 3 July 1941 (after the German invasion): "We secured peace for our country for one and a half years... as well as an opportunity of preparing our forces for defense if fascist Germany risked attacking our country in defiance of the pact. This was a definite gain for our country and a loss for fascist Germany." Stalin later told Churchill (August 1942, per Churchill's own account) his underlying reasoning: he had asked how many divisions France and Britain would mobilize against Germany ("about a hundred" vs. Britain's "two, and two more later"), then asked how many divisions the USSR would need on its own front if it went to war with Germany alone: "More than three hundred" (Shirer, citing Churchill, 1960).

**Economic dimension (Sept 1939–1941).** Shirer documents substantial German-Soviet economic cooperation flowing from the pact, distinct from and supplementing the territorial protocol: the USSR provided Germany with Arctic port facilities (Teriberka, near Murmansk) for refueling and repairing German ships, including submarines, evading the British blockade — one of the "best-kept secrets of the war," per Shirer, recovered from captured papers; Molotov agreed on 10 November 1939 that the USSR would pay freight charges on German goods on Russian railways. German military leaders (Göring, Raeder, Keitel) protested in November–December 1939 that Soviet demands for war material were excessive, but Hitler ordered on 30 March 1940 that deliveries to Russia take priority even over the German armed forces. A trade agreement signed in Moscow on 11 February 1940 set a minimum 640 million Reichsmarks over 18 months: Germany supplied the unfinished cruiser Lützow, plans for the battleship Bismarck, Messerschmitt 109/110 fighters, and Ju-88 dive bombers, in exchange for one million tons of cereals, 900,000 tons of oil, 100,000 tons of cotton, 500,000 tons of phosphates, plus transit of Manchurian soybeans. German trade delegate Dr. Schnurre's summary memo called the agreement "a wide-open door to the East" that decisively weakened the British blockade (Shirer 1960).

**Partition mechanics of the Soviet invasion of Poland.** A documentary chain of captured Schulenburg–Ribbentrop cables (5–17 September 1939) shows Moscow deliberately delayed its own invasion until Warsaw's fall neared and stage-managed its justification. Molotov told Schulenburg on 16 September the Soviet government would justify intervention as protecting Ukrainian/White Russian "brothers," asking Germany "not to stumble over this piece of straw" even though the pretext was aimed at Germany itself as the only plausible "third power." Stalin personally rewrote the wording of the joint German-Soviet invasion communiqué on 17 September because the German draft "presented the facts all too frankly" — [SHIRER-THESIS] "For cynicism Hitler had met his match in Stalin." The initiative for full partition of Poland (rather than a rump buffer state) came from the Soviet side: Molotov proposed the Pissa–Narew–Vistula–San partition line to Schulenburg on 19 September. Meeting Schulenburg on 25 September, Stalin offered to trade Polish territory (the Province of Warsaw to the Bug) for German abandonment of claims to Lithuania, explicitly linking this to "the solution of the problem of the Baltic countries." The German-Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty, signed 5 a.m. on 29 September (officially dated 28 September), reassigned Lithuania to the USSR in exchange for the Lublin/Eastern Warsaw provinces to Germany, with both parties agreeing to "tolerate ... no Polish agitation" and to suppress it jointly. Arnold Toynbee called this the "fifth partition" of Poland; Shirer calls it the fourth (not counting an earlier partition that omitted Austria). [SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer judges Stalin the "greater winner" of the Poland campaign — the USSR gained nearly half of Poland and a stranglehold on the Baltic states while blocking Germany from Ukrainian wheat and Rumanian oil, whereas Hitler needed Soviet neutrality merely to face Britain and France in the West (Shirer 1960).

**Breakdown (November 1940).** The Molotov–Hitler/Ribbentrop talks in Berlin, 12–13 November 1940, exposed the practical collapse of Nazi-Soviet cooperation: Ribbentrop proposed dividing the world into four "spheres of interest," suggesting Russia's outlet lay toward "the Persian Gulf and the Arabian Sea"; Molotov icily replied "Which sea?" and refused to be diverted from Europe and the Straits. [EYEWITNESS] Shirer's Berlin diary entry for 12 November: "A dark, drizzling day, and Molotov arrived, his reception being extremely stiff and formal. Driving up the Linden to the Soviet Embassy, he looked to me like a plugging, provincial schoolmaster." During the 13 November banquet at the Soviet Embassy, a British air raid forced Hitler, Ribbentrop, and guests into shelters mid-toast; Molotov's retort to Ribbentrop's insistence Britain was defeated: "If that is so, why are we in this shelter, and whose are these bombs which fall?" Stalin's formal counter-proposal of 26 November 1940 to join a Four-Power Pact demanded immediate German troop withdrawal from Finland, Soviet base rights at the Bosporus/Dardanelles, recognition of the area south of Batum/Baku toward the Persian Gulf as Soviet sphere, and Japanese renunciation of Sakhalin coal/oil concessions; Hitler rejected this as too high a price, telling his generals: "Stalin is clever and cunning... A German victory has become unbearable for Russia. Therefore: she must be brought to her knees as soon as possible" — the documentary bridge to [[operation-barbarossa-1941|Barbarossa]] (Shirer 1960).
