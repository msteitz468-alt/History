# The Grand Coalition of 298–296 BCE

The wartime alliance assembled during the Third Samnite War (298–290 BCE) uniting the Samnites with the Etruscans, Umbrians, and Senone Gauls of northern Italy — peoples with no natural common cause beyond shared opposition to continued Roman expansion — into a single combined force against Rome.

## Narrative

Per Livy's account, the coalition was principally the achievement of the Samnite general [[actors/gellius-egnatius|Gellius Egnatius]], who led a Samnite army on a long march north through Roman and allied territory to link up with Etruscan and Umbrian forces already facing Roman pressure, and to draw in Senone Gallic warriors from the north as well. The resulting combined army was the largest and most geographically dispersed coalition Rome had yet faced in Italy, forcing Rome to commit both consuls and multiple legions simultaneously rather than fighting each people separately.

## Causal Analysis

The coalition was `caused_by` shared alarm at Roman expansion across multiple previously-unconnected Italian and Gallic peoples, and it was `contributed_to` directly by Gellius Egnatius's personal diplomatic and military initiative in physically uniting forces that were otherwise separated by considerable distance and had little prior coordination.

## Consequence Analysis

The coalition `produced` the Battle of Sentinum (295 BCE), where it was decisively defeated by Roman consular armies; its collapse there `enabled` Rome's continued, largely uncontested consolidation of central Italy over the following years, ending the Third Samnite War in Rome's favor by 290 BCE.

## Historiography

As with much of the early Samnite Wars narrative, the coalition and the scale of forces involved are known chiefly through Livy, writing centuries later; modern historians treat his casualty and troop figures with skepticism while generally accepting the coalition's basic existence and its defeat at Sentinum as a real turning point in Rome's control of central Italy.
