# The German Interregnum (c. 1250–1273)

The **Interregnum** — "often misnamed," since it lacked emperors but "not kings" — is the
period of disputed and ineffective German kingship between the death of
**[[frederick-ii-hohenstaufen|Frederick II]]** (1250) and the election of
**[[rudolf-of-habsburg|Rudolf of Habsburg]]** (1273). [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]
(Michael Toch, Ch. 9a) reframes it not as a "dreadful," lawless interlude (the nineteenth
century's *kaiserlose, schreckliche Zeit*) but as a **period of transition** in which the
real action lay in the territories (*Landesgeschichte*), and German kingship was permanently
transformed.

## Narrative

After Frederick II's death the Hohenstaufen cause "slowly ebbed": **Conrad IV** left for
Sicily (1251) and died in 1254; **Manfred** and **Conradin** died in Italy (1266, 1268). The
**double election of 1257** produced two foreign kings — **Richard of Cornwall** (brother of
[[henry-iii-of-england|Henry III]] of England, backed by Cologne) and **Alfonso X of
Castile** (backed by Trier and the pope) — neither of whom could rule. Richard, "equipped
with great sums of money," was crowned at Aachen but spent less than four years in Germany,
"almost never venturing eastwards of the Rhine"; Alfonso "never set foot in Germany." Power
devolved to the princes amid a "great number and high intensity of feuds."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **extinction of the Hohenstaufen dynasty** and the absence of an
  undisputed heir.
- `contributed_to:` the **long absentee kingship** of Frederick II and the **papal–imperial
  struggle**, which had left royal power dependent on princely consensus; and the
  **accelerating aggrandisement of the territorial princes**, "another feature almost as old
  as the imperial–Italian nexus."

## Consequence Analysis

Toch identifies the lasting transformations:

- **From hereditary to elective monarchy**: the crown became firmly **elective**, and the
  right to choose the king was, by the precedent of 1257, "henceforth confined to seven"
  electors — the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne and Trier, the king of Bohemia, the Rhenish
  count-palatine, the duke of Saxony and the margrave of Brandenburg. The formal **college of
  electors** (*Kurfürsten*) would crystallise over the next century.
- **"Small kings"**: the later-thirteenth-century rulers were underequipped *reges Allamanniae*
  of comital rank, dependent on dwindling, often pawned crown lands, in an "open
  constitution" of minimal institutionalisation.
- **The rise of Hausmacht**: real power could now be built only on the **local and regional
  level** — a "Hausmacht kingship" based on consolidated dynastic territory, "strong enough
  to tip the balance of power, not in the whole of Germany, but rather in one of its major
  political regions." This was the strategy that the **[[rudolf-of-habsburg|Habsburgs]]** and
  later the Luxemburgs would master.
- **Shifting geography of power**: from the old Hohenstaufen base on the upper Rhine to the
  middle and lower Rhine (the great ecclesiastical electors), and finally to the south-east
  (Bavaria, Austria, Bohemia) — together with the **Ostsiedlung** colonisation that added
  vast territory east of the Elbe.

## Historiography

Toch follows recent German scholarship (Moraw) in rejecting both the romantic "terrible time"
of Schiller's poem and the older nationalist lament for lost royal power. The transformation
is read as a "learning process" producing a new, regionally based form of kingship that came
into its own in the fourteenth century. Recent work has also **rehabilitated** Richard of
Cornwall and Alfonso X as "mature and able statesmen of European stature."
