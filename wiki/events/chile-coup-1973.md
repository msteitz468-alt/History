## Narrative

[[allende-salvador|Salvador Allende]], a Marxist candidate, won a plurality (36.6% to 35.3%) in Chile's September 1970 presidential election by a margin of roughly 40,000 votes and was inaugurated on 3 November 1970 after congressional ratification. At a meeting on 15 September 1970, President Richard Nixon ordered the CIA to prevent Allende from taking power or to unseat him; per notes taken by CIA director Richard Helms, Nixon said: "1 in 10 chance perhaps, but save Chile! ... make economy scream, 48 hours for plan of action," and approved a $10 million budget. The following day Helms cabled station chief Thomas Karamessines: "The President has asked the Agency to prevent Allende from coming to power or to unseat him." This "Track II" effort ran alongside a broader "Track I" campaign of political and economic pressure.

As part of this campaign, CIA-linked plotters (Generals Roberto Viaux and Camilo Valenzuela), armed and funded via military attaché Col. Paul Wimert with weapons and $250,000 in cash, ambushed and fatally shot army commander-in-chief René Schneider, an opponent of military intervention, on 22 October 1970; Helms cabled the station chief "excellent job" in response. The assassination failed to prevent Allende's congressional confirmation days later.

Allende's government nationalized the country's copper mines in July 1971 (a unanimous vote of the US Congress had earlier noted the industry's importance); the State Department calculated that Anaconda and Kennecott had made $744 million in "excess profits" in Chile between 1955 and 1970. Over the next three years the CIA spent roughly $3.5 million on political opposition, including about $2 million in propaganda (chiefly funneled through the newspaper El Mercurio) and $1.5 million for civic and right-wing groups, including $38,500 to the neo-fascist paramilitary group Patria y Libertad.

On 11 September 1973, the Chilean military — led by newly installed army commander-in-chief [[pinochet-augusto|Augusto Pinochet]], who had become commander after the CIA-encouraged ouster of General Carlos Prats — carried out a coup that toppled Allende's government in roughly seven hours. The navy secured Valparaíso by 8 a.m.; tanks surrounded the presidential palace, La Moneda; aerial strafing killed several of Allende's bodyguards; Allende died by suicide with an assault rifle (reportedly a gift from Fidel Castro) around 2 p.m. as troops closed in.

In the coup's immediate aftermath, roughly 60,000 people were arrested; many were held and tortured at the National Stadium, where hundreds were shot. Some twenty interrogation centers operated nationwide, including Villa Grimaldi. In October 1973, General Sergio Arellano Stark led the "Caravan of Death," a helicopter tour of military bases that executed approximately 100 political prisoners. A later government investigation found that Pinochet's purge killed 3,065 people, tortured or arrested 40,018, and drove more than 200,000 (roughly 2% of the population) into exile.

Chilean folk singer Víctor Jara, a cultural icon of the Popular Unity government, was tortured for five days at Estadio Chile and shot by an interrogator, his body struck by 44 bullets.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by`: sustained US covert destabilization (Track I/Track II) initiated by the Nixon administration in September 1970.
- `contributed_to`: the 1970 assassination of René Schneider, which removed a key military obstacle to future intervention.
- `preceded_by`: Allende's 1970 election and his nationalization of the copper industry.
- `produced`: Pinochet's 1973–1990 dictatorship, the DINA secret police, and Operation Condor.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: formation of DINA under Colonel Manuel Contreras and, from November 1975, Operation Condor, a transnational assassination and intelligence-sharing network among Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay. A 1992 archive of 700,000 documents found in Paraguay (McCoy's cited figures) attributes to Condor 30,000 disappearances, 50,000 assassinations, and 400,000 imprisonments over fifteen years; the CIA provided computer, encryption, and telex ("Condortel") support and maintained direct contact with Contreras from 1974–77 via General Vernon Walters.
- `enabled`: the "Chicago Boys" economic restructuring of Chile, advised by economists trained under Milton Friedman (who visited Chile in March 1975 recommending "shock treatment").
- Chile's 1991 National Commission for Truth and Reconciliation found the dictatorship responsible for roughly 3,400 cases of kidnapping, disappearance, or killing; a 2004 follow-up commission documented 38,254 people imprisoned and tortured 1973–1990; by 2012, 799 state agents had been convicted.

## Historiography

Alfred W. McCoy's *[[mccoy-cold-war-five-continents-2024|Cold War on Five Continents]]* (2024) draws on declassified Nixon-administration records (Helms's meeting notes, State Department cables) to document direct US planning of the destabilization campaign; Henry Kissinger's own memoirs denied any US coup involvement after November 1970, a claim McCoy states "defies the documentary evidence." McCoy also situates Chile within his broader "Jakarta model" comparative thesis (see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]] and [[indonesian-mass-killings-1965]]), arguing that Chile's comparatively lower death toll (~3,400 confirmed vs. Indonesia's much larger toll) helps explain Chile's stronger post-Cold War democratic recovery — this comparative-causal argument is McCoy's own synthesis and should be read as a position, not settled historiography.
