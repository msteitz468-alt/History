# The Achaean War and the Sack of Corinth (146 BCE)

The final, futile revolt of the [[achaean-league|Achaean League]] against Rome,
ending in the destruction of **[[corinth]]** and the extinction of Greek freedom —
in the same year as the destruction of Carthage. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH
VIII]] Ch. 9 (Derow).

## Narrative

After the surviving Achaean detainees returned (150), the old Sparta–Achaea dispute
flared. With Rome distracted by Andriscus' Macedonian revolt and Carthage, the
League under the popular leaders **Critolaus** and **Diaeus** pressed its case. In
147 the Roman envoy **L. Aurelius Orestes** delivered a crushing order: Sparta,
Corinth, Argos and other cities were to be **detached from the League** — in effect
its dismemberment. The announcement provoked fury and violence against Spartans and
near-violence against the Romans.

Repeated Roman embassies offered the League a last chance to **obey**; the
Achaeans, with wide popular support across Greece, chose war. In spring 146 the
assembly at Corinth declared war "nominally against Sparta but in reality against
Rome" (Polybius). **Q. Caecilius Metellus**, sweeping down from Macedonia, defeated
and killed Critolaus at **Scarpheia**; the consul **L. Mummius** then routed Diaeus
at the Isthmus. **Corinth opened its gates**; on the Senate's decree the city was
**sacked, burned, its art and population carried off or sold**, and its site left
desolate.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the Roman order to **dismember the League** — the logical endpoint of the
policy, visible since the 160s, of reducing Achaean power; and the accumulated
**grievance** of decades of subordination (the Callicrates regime, the deportations).
contributed_to: the contingency of timing — Rome, freed by the defeat of Andriscus
and the imminent fall of Carthage, could now turn on Greece.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **destruction of Corinth** (parallel to [[third-punic-war-149-146bce|
Carthage]] — "a hard year for commercial cities"); the **dissolution of the Greek
confederacies**; democracy ceased to be the normal form of government.
enabled: **Macedonia** became a Roman province (its governor's brief covering
southern Greece, not yet a separate province); Greek autonomy was permanently ended.

## Historiography

[[polybius]] — present at the fall, and afterward a mediator regulating Achaean
affairs — is the central source, his disdain for the "manual labourers and artisans"
who drove the war a revealing class marker. Derow makes the war the terminus of his
account of Roman *imperium* as the demand for obedience. The destruction of Corinth,
like that of Carthage, is read as the expression of a Roman willingness to
annihilate that had hardened since Pydna.
