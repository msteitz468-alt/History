## Narrative

The Treaty of Portsmouth, signed on **September 5, 1905**, ended the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] (1904–05). It was negotiated at the Portsmouth Navy Yard in New Hampshire under the mediation of US President Theodore Roosevelt and signed by Japanese plenipotentiary [[komura-jutaro|Komura Jutarō]] and Russian plenipotentiary [[witte-sergei|Sergei Witte]].

### Path to the Conference

The push for peace came from both sides after the [[battle-of-tsushima-1905|Battle of Tsushima]] (May 27–28, 1905) annihilated Russia's Baltic Fleet. Japan, though victorious at sea and on land (Port Arthur, Mukden), was financially exhausted: Army Minister Terauchi Masatake had quietly approached the American minister in Tokyo as early as March 8, while the Battle of Mukden still raged, to signal that "the time had come for the war to cease" (Keene, ch. 55). Russia, meanwhile, was convulsed by revolution: workers' uprisings, the Potemkin mutiny, and the assassination of Grand Duke Sergei had shaken the regime.

President Roosevelt had cultivated close ties to both belligerents. His intermediary with Japan was [[kaneko-kentaro|Kaneko Kentarō]], a Harvard graduate and trusted Japanese envoy in Washington, who had met Roosevelt repeatedly throughout the war. After Tsushima, Foreign Minister Komura formally asked Roosevelt on June 1, 1905 to invite the belligerents to "come together for the purpose of direct negotiations" — and Roosevelt obliged. Russia accepted, though the czar characteristically used language suggesting only "preliminary discussions." On June 10 Japan formally accepted; the conference site was settled as Portsmouth after Roosevelt rejected Washington (too hot in summer) and Komura flatly refused any European location (Keene, ch. 55).

### The Negotiations

Delegates began deliberations on **August 10, 1905**. Japan presented twelve demands. The two that caused intractable difficulty were:
1. **An indemnity** — Russia paying Japan the costs of the war.
2. **Cession of Sakhalin** — the entire island to pass to Japan.

Czar Nicholas II was immovable on both: "Russia has never paid an indemnity; I shall never consent to this" — with "never" underlined three times on the draft instructions. On Sakhalin he was equally firm, refusing to yield "one square inch of Russian soil," though Keene notes the irony that Sakhalin "had been owned by Russia only since 1875" when Japan had traded it for the Kuriles (Keene, ch. 55).

Komura was equally inflexible, insisting on both items even after Tokyo's cabinet had privately determined they were not "absolutely indispensable." The three non-negotiable Japanese demands were: (1) Russian recognition of Japan's complete freedom of action in Korea, (2) mutual evacuation of Manchuria, and (3) transfer of the Port Arthur leasehold. On August 26, Komura telegraphed Tokyo announcing his intention to break off negotiations.

### The Genrō Decision

The telegram precipitated an extraordinary council on **August 28**: the prime minister convened a meeting of cabinet members and three genrō — Itō Hirobumi, [[yamagata-aritomo|Yamagata Aritomo]], and Inoue Kaoru — in the presence of [[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]. They concluded, after hours of debate, that "military and economic conditions compelled Japan to negotiate for peace, regardless of the loss of an indemnity and Sakhalin." Japan's basic objective — settlement of the Korea and Manchuria questions — had already been achieved. Komura was directed to yield on the indemnity and to accept the Japanese occupation of Sakhalin as a *fait accompli*, using a face-saving device: if Russia refused, Komura should ask Roosevelt to recommend that Japan withdraw its Sakhalin claim "for the sake of peace and humanity" (Keene, ch. 55).

Members of the Japanese delegation wept when they received these instructions.

### Final Settlement

At a secret meeting on August 29, Witte agreed to cede the southern half of Sakhalin (at the 50th parallel). Komura, following instructions, accepted. At the formal session the same morning, Komura theatrically asked for the whole island, was refused by Witte, then magnanimously accepted the division "for the sake of peace and humanity" — Keene notes this was "just so much playacting for the benefit of spectators." The treaty was signed **September 5, 1905**.

### Terms

- Russia recognized Japan's **paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea**
- Mutual evacuation of Manchuria by both armies
- Transfer of the **Port Arthur leasehold** from Russia to Japan
- Cession of the **southern half of Sakhalin** to Japan
- Restriction of Russian railway use in Manchuria to commercial purposes
- No indemnity

## Causal Analysis

The treaty's terms were shaped by three intersecting constraints: Japan's financial inability to continue the war (despite military success), Russia's internal political crisis (the 1905 Revolution made prolonging the war untenable), and Roosevelt's active mediation which created face-saving mechanisms for both sides. The decision to accept peace without indemnity or all of Sakhalin was made by the genrō council against Komura's instincts — an example of strategic realism overriding diplomatic honor.

## Consequence Analysis

The treaty produced immediate popular fury in Japan: when its terms were published, some 30,000 protesters assembled at Hibiya Park on September 5, broke through police barricades, burned streetcars, and stormed police boxes. Martial law was declared in Tokyo and not lifted until November 29 — the [[hibiya-riots-1905|Hibiya Riots]]. The public had expected a large indemnity and had not been told Japan's financial limits. President Roosevelt, who received telegrams of thanks from both Meiji and Czar Nicholas, won the 1906 Nobel Peace Prize for his mediation.

Diplomatically, the treaty conferred great-power recognition on Japan. Russia acknowledged Japanese primacy in Korea, enabling the formal [[korea-protectorate-1905|Korean Protectorate]] (November 1905) and eventually the annexation of Korea in 1910. The transfer of the Port Arthur leasehold gave Japan control of the strategically vital tip of the Liaodong Peninsula — the same territory Japan had been forced to disgorge after the Sino-Japanese War.

The Russian reaction was one of surreal optimism in defeat: Witte and Rosen "drank champagne toasts" because they had avoided paying an indemnity and yielded only half a "bleak island" the Japanese had already occupied. Komura returned home to face the riots. Roosevelt wrote to the American minister in Beijing just before signing: "I was pro-Japanese before, but after my experience with the peace commissioners I am far stronger pro-Japanese than ever" (Keene, ch. 55).

## Actors

- **[[komura-jutaro|Komura Jutarō]]** — Japanese foreign minister and chief plenipotentiary; insisted on indemnity beyond Tokyo's instructions; directed by the genrō council to yield.
- **[[witte-sergei|Sergei Witte]]** — Russian chief plenipotentiary; agreed to divide Sakhalin against the czar's explicit order; regarded by the Russians as having performed a miracle.
- **[[kaneko-kentaro|Kaneko Kentarō]]** — Japanese envoy to Roosevelt; key backchannel throughout the war; relayed the famous "kill a bear" conversation suggesting Tsushima's augury.
- **[[yamagata-aritomo|Yamagata Aritomo]]** — Genrō member of the August 28 council that directed Komura to accept peace.
- **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** — Genrō member of the August 28 council; had been the preferred choice for chief plenipotentiary but was kept in Tokyo by the emperor.
- **[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]** — Presided over the August 28 council; gave assent to the peace terms decided by cabinet and genrō.

## Historiography

Keene's account is based on detailed diplomatic records on both sides and draws on Russian memoirs (Witte, Rosen, Paléologue) as well as Japanese archives. The key historiographical question is whether Japan gave away too much: at the time, many Japanese believed so, fueling the Hibiya Riots. Subsequent scholarship has generally agreed that Japan's financial position made peace without indemnity the only sustainable outcome, and that Komura's obstinacy nearly sacrificed the achievable gains for the unachievable. Roosevelt's mediation role is treated by American historians as his greatest foreign policy achievement; Japanese historiography is more ambivalent about the constraints it imposed.
