# The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)

The **Cuban Missile Crisis** of October 1962 was, in James Hershberg's words in
[[leffler-westad-cold-war-v2|*The Cambridge History of the Cold War*, Vol. II]], "the Cold
War's most perilous passage — humanity's closest brush with the ultimate man-made
catastrophe." For thirteen days the United States and the Soviet Union stood "eyeball to
eyeball" over Soviet nuclear missiles in **Cuba**, with the world at the edge of
thermonuclear war. From the [[cold-war|Cold War]]'s [[nuclear-arms-race|nuclear]] confrontation.

## Narrative

In summer 1962 Soviet leader **Nikita Khrushchev** secretly shipped **nuclear missiles** to
**Fidel Castro's** Cuba — to deter a feared second US invasion after the **Bay of Pigs**
(1961), to defend a vulnerable revolutionary ally, and to redress the strategic imbalance (in
1962 the US held some 27,000 warheads to the USSR's ~3,300). US reconnaissance **discovered
the missiles on 14 October**. President **John F. Kennedy** and his "ExComm" rejected an
air strike or invasion in favour of a naval **"quarantine" (blockade)** of Cuba, announced on
22 October, demanding the missiles' removal.

For a week the superpowers stood at the brink, forces on high alert, with the danger — as
both leaders recognized — that any clash, "whether intentional or accidental," could spiral
beyond control ("such is the logic of war," Khrushchev wrote Kennedy). The crisis was
resolved on **28 October** by a deal: the USSR would **withdraw the missiles** in return for
a public US pledge **not to invade Cuba** and a **secret** American agreement to remove its
**Jupiter missiles from Turkey**. The world stepped back from the precipice.

## Causal & Consequence Analysis

- `caused_by:` Khrushchev's secret deployment; the US Jupiters in Turkey; the defense of Cuba
  after the Bay of Pigs; the logic of the [[nuclear-arms-race]].
- `produced:` a sobering recognition on both sides of nuclear danger — the **Washington–Moscow
  "hotline"** (1963), the **Partial Test Ban Treaty** (1963), and the first real movement
  toward **[[detente]]**.
- `produced:` Khrushchev's weakened standing (a factor in his 1964 fall) and a Soviet
  determination never again to be caught in strategic inferiority — fueling the arms build-up
  that produced rough **parity** by the 1970s.

## Historiography

Archival openings (and the famous Kennedy ExComm tapes) have revised the heroic "Kennedy
faced down Khrushchev" narrative: the **secret Turkey deal** shows the resolution was a
**mutual, negotiated climbdown**, not a one-sided US victory, and later revelations (tactical
nuclear weapons already on Cuba; near-launches by Soviet submarines) suggest the world came
even closer to catastrophe than contemporaries knew. Hershberg stresses the "ludicrous
dissonance" between the crisis's near-apocalyptic stakes and the ephemeral interests at play.
