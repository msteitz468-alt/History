# Cuban Missile Crisis
## Narrative
In the summer of 1962, hoping to check American nuclear superiority and protect the new revolutionary government of Fidel Castro in Cuba, Soviet First Secretary Nikita Khrushchev ordered the secret installation of medium- and intermediate-range nuclear missiles in Cuba. In October 1962, American U-2 spy planes photographed the construction of the launch sites, triggering the crisis. President John F. Kennedy announced a naval "quarantine" of the island and demanded the immediate withdrawal of the missiles. For several tense days, the world stood on the brink of thermonuclear war. The crisis ended when Khrushchev agreed to dismantle and withdraw the missiles in exchange for a public US pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret agreement to remove US Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` **Khrushchev's erratic overreaching** (his belief that the United States was on the decline and would accept a fait accompli; Line 6721); and the **US nuclear superiority** which forced the Soviets to seek a forward-deployed missile base to balance the strategic equation.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the **peaceful withdrawal of the missiles**, resolving the immediate nuclear threat.
- `produced:` a **major political humiliation for Khrushchev**, which weakened his standing in the Kremlin and contributed to his overthrow in October 1964.
- `enabled:` the **institution of direct communication channels** between the superpowers, symbolized by the **Moscow-Washington "hot line"** (Line 6721).
- `followed_by:` the **signing of the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963)** and the beginning of the nuclear non-proliferation agreements.
- `contrasts_with:` the later Soviet military policy under Brezhnev, which avoided erratic strategic adventures and instead focused on a steady, massive build-up of domestic nuclear forces to achieve parity with the US.

## Actors
- **Nikita Khrushchev**, who initiated the missile deployment; and US President **John F. Kennedy**, who instituted the blockade. See [[khrushchev-nikita]].

## Historiography
Riasanovsky notes that the crisis was the most dangerous confrontation of the Cold War, demonstrating that in the nuclear age, both superpowers had to measure their reactions carefully to avoid mutual destruction (Line 6721).
