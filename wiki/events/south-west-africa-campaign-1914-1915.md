# South-West Africa Campaign (1914–1915)

The Union of South Africa's conquest of the German colony of South-West
Africa (modern Namibia), one of the earliest Allied campaigns of the First
World War and the first fought largely by an African Dominion force under
its own command.

## Narrative

Prime Minister Louis Botha and Defence Minister Jan Smuts led South African
forces (drawn partly from recently defeated Boer rebels, whose 1914
Maritz Rebellion had briefly threatened the campaign before it began) in a
multi-pronged invasion of the sparsely defended German colony. Superior
numbers, mounted infantry mobility suited to the terrain, and control of the
coastline allowed South African forces to isolate and reduce German
positions systematically. The German commander capitulated at Khorab on 9
July 1915 after the loss of the colony's interior.

## Causal Analysis

The campaign followed directly from South Africa's obligations as a British
Dominion at the outbreak of war, but its progress was complicated internally
by the Maritz Rebellion, an armed uprising of pro-German, anti-British Boer
elements that Botha's government had to suppress before the invasion could
proceed at scale.

## Consequence Analysis

South African occupation was formalized as a League of Nations Class C
mandate in 1920, beginning a South African administration of the territory
that lasted until Namibian independence in 1990 — an outcome with major
long-term consequences for Southern African decolonization.

## Historiography

The campaign is documented mainly through South African and British
official histories; the collection's coverage of Southern Africa in this
period is comparatively thin, and the campaign is more often treated as a
sideline to the Western Front narrative than as a subject in its own right,
despite its long-term significance for the region's colonial history.
