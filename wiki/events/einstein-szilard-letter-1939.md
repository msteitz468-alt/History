# Einstein–Szilard Letter to Roosevelt

**August 2, 1939**. Letter signed by Albert Einstein, initiated and driven by Leo Szilard, alerting President Franklin D. Roosevelt to the military implications of recent nuclear fission discoveries and the risk that Nazi Germany might develop atomic weapons first.

**Related detailed analysis:** [[hubs/biographies/world-wars-era/einstein-albert|Albert Einstein — Detailed Biography]] (Signature Decisions)

## Narrative

In the summer of 1939, Hungarian-born physicist Leo Szilard, a refugee from Nazi persecution, sought out Albert Einstein at his vacation cottage in Peconic on Long Island, New York. Szilard had been working on the possibility of a nuclear chain reaction and was alarmed by the implications of the fission work of Otto Hahn, Fritz Strassmann, and others in Germany.

Einstein and Szilard (with Edward Teller present for part of the discussion) drafted a letter warning Roosevelt. Contrary to some later popular accounts that portrayed Einstein as merely affixing his signature to a document prepared by others, primary records show Einstein was actively engaged. Szilard brought a draft; Einstein dictated a new version in German based on it (itself a translation of an earlier draft Einstein had dictated two weeks prior). The notes of the conversation made by Teller that day, along with the German draft, are preserved in the Teller archives and reprinted in Nathan and Norden. Einstein alone signed the final letter.

The letter described the possibility of “extremely powerful bombs of a new type” constructed from uranium and urged the U.S. government to secure supplies of uranium ore and support experimental work by physicists. A longer version went to the Franklin Roosevelt archives in Hyde Park (copy in AEA 33-143); a shorter version is in the Szilárd papers.

Szilard also attempted to enlist aviator Charles Lindbergh to deliver the letter or support the effort, but Lindbergh delivered an isolationist speech on September 15, 1939, and later did not recall receiving the letters. Alexander Sachs, an economist and advisor to Roosevelt, ultimately delivered the letter to the President on October 11, 1939. Sachs recounted a story (possibly garbled) of Napoleon and Robert Fulton to illustrate the risks of ignoring scientific advice. Roosevelt responded by letter on October 19, 1939: “I have read your letter of August 2nd...” and ordered action.

Einstein followed up in early 1940 with letters to Sachs (March 7) and Lyman Briggs (April 25), the head of the newly formed Advisory Committee on Uranium. Despite the letter’s role in spurring U.S. interest, Einstein was never granted security clearance for the Manhattan Project and had no direct involvement in the weapon’s development. The FBI maintained extensive files on him and ironically interviewed him in 1940 regarding Szilard’s own clearance.

A follow-up letter from Einstein to Roosevelt dated March 25, 1945 (AEA 33-109) addressed ongoing nuclear matters.

## Causal Analysis

The immediate driver was Szilard’s initiative and the refugee scientists’ accurate assessment of the military potential of fission, combined with fear that Germany—then leading in some areas of nuclear physics—would weaponize it first. Einstein’s unparalleled international prestige as the author of relativity made his name the most effective vehicle for gaining presidential attention. Einstein’s prior public pacifism made his willingness to sign all the more striking; he viewed the Nazi threat as exceptional enough to justify the warning.

Isaacson’s notes emphasize that while Szilard “prompted and propelled the discussions,” Einstein “was fully involved in writing the letter that he alone signed.” Some later accounts (including occasional comments by Einstein himself) downplayed his role, but the contemporary records contradict minimization.

## Consequence Analysis

The letter directly contributed to the establishment of the Advisory Committee on Uranium (the “Uranium Committee”) and the beginning of organized U.S. government-funded nuclear research. This infrastructure later fed into the Manhattan Project.

Einstein himself remained outside the classified program. After the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki he expressed horror and pivoted immediately to public advocacy for international control of atomic energy and world government. The episode became a central element in his postwar identity as an atomic-age moral voice and was later cited both by those praising scientists’ civic responsibility and by critics of his political interventions.

The letter also entered FBI and security lore; the same agencies that acted on its warning had long viewed Einstein himself with suspicion due to his earlier pacifist and leftist associations.

## Actors

- **Primary**: Albert Einstein (signatory, active drafter); Leo Szilard (initiator, co-drafter).
- **Secondary**: Franklin D. Roosevelt (recipient); Alexander Sachs (deliverer); Edward Teller (note-taker at meeting); Lyman Briggs (early committee head).
- Later context: J. Robert Oppenheimer and others in the Manhattan Project (Einstein excluded).

## Historiography

Isaacson (drawing on Weart and Szilard 1978, Rhodes, Nathan and Norden, FBI files obtained via FOIA, and Teller archives) corrects the oversimplified “Einstein merely signed” narrative. Szilard’s own detailed recollection and Teller’s contemporaneous notes establish Einstein’s substantive participation. Popular histories sometimes romanticize or minimize the episode depending on the author’s view of Einstein’s politics. The letter’s causal weight in launching the Manhattan Project remains debated: it opened the door but the program’s scale and success owed far more to later industrial mobilization, émigré scientists inside the project, and the exigencies of total war.
