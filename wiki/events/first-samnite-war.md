# First Samnite War (343–341 BCE)

A brief, inconclusively-resolved war between the Roman Republic and the Samnites over influence in Campania, known to us almost entirely through Livy's later and (per most modern historians) partly unreliable and patriotically-shaped narrative. According to Livy, Capua appealed to Rome for help against Samnite incursions; Rome, previously allied with the Samnites against the Gauls, reversed that alliance to accept Capua's submission and fought several battles against its former ally.

## Narrative

Livy records Roman victories at Mount Gaurus and Suessula, but modern historians (following Livy's own acknowledgment of disputed sources) treat the war's military narrative with caution, noting Livy's tendency to inflate early Roman successes. The war ended without a decisive settlement; Rome and the Samnites made peace, in part because Rome faced a more pressing crisis closer to home — the revolt of its own Latin allies, who resented Rome's unilateral expansion of power and territory at Capua's expense without consulting the Latin League.

## Causal Analysis

The war was `contributed_to` by the earlier 354 BCE Roman-Samnite treaty against the Gauls, which had left both powers as neighbors with overlapping ambitions in Campania once the Gallic threat receded. It `preceded` and `contributed_to` the [[events/latin-war-340-338bce|Latin War]] that followed almost immediately: Rome's diplomatic reversal — abandoning the Samnite alliance to protect Capua, then making peace with the Samnites while retaining influence over Campania — left the Latins convinced that Rome intended to treat its allies as subjects rather than equals.

## Consequence Analysis

The war `enabled` Rome's continued southward reach into Campania without immediately breaking Samnite relations, but it `contributed_to` the far more consequential Latin War, in which Rome's own allies rose against it.

## Historiography

Livy is effectively the only surviving connected narrative source, writing over two centuries after the events with access to earlier (now lost) Roman annalistic histories of uncertain reliability; the war's scale and even some of its battles are treated by many modern historians as plausibly exaggerated or partly invented to fill a thin annalistic record. Collection coverage is limited to what is embedded in broader Roman Republic and Samnite Wars narratives.
