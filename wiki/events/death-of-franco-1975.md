# The Death of Franco, 20 November 1975

[[franco-francisco|Francisco Franco]] died in Madrid on 20 November 1975 — the 39th anniversary of [[primo-de-rivera-jose-antonio|José Antonio]]'s execution — after a five-week terminal agony (heart attacks, hemorrhages, repeated surgery) that Payne (1987) narrates as a grim metaphor for the regime itself, artificially sustained past natural life. Two days later [[juan-carlos-i|Juan Carlos]] was proclaimed King, and within three years [[actors/francoist-spain|Francoist Spain]] had been dismantled by legal reform from within (the 1976 Political Reform Law, 1977 elections, 1978 Constitution).

## Detail (Payne 1987, chs. 21–22)

The terminal sequence Payne narrates: the MATESA scandal (1969) and "monocolor" cabinet; the Burgos trial (December 1970, nine ETA death sentences commuted after unanimous cabinet advice — but the publicity expanded ETA's support); Franco's visible decay (Walters, 1971: "old and weak," left hand trembling; the portable golf seat at the 1972 victory parade); Carrero's premiership and assassination (1973), during which the Civil Guard chief's shoot-if-necessary order had to be countermanded by the acting president; Arias Navarro's first all-civilian cabinet (January 1974) and the February 12 "apertura" promises; the Añoveros affair (February 1974 — the regime nearly deported a bishop); the Puig Antich execution (March 1974); the Carnation Revolution (Franco: "What can be expected of an Army that is led by its supply corps?"); the July 1974 thrombophlebitis (triggered, Payne records, by tight army-issue shoes), the temporary transfer of powers to Juan Carlos and Franco's abrupt September resumption; and the final agony of October–November 1975 amid the Green March. The pardon of 1 October 1971 (camouflaging the MATESA pardons among 3,000 political ones) and "todo está atado y bien atado" (New Year's Eve 1969) frame the regime's self-image of a completed succession.

## Narrative and Consequence Analysis

Franco's last year was marked by terminal drift: the ineffective Arias Navarro government, economic crisis after 1973, ETA and FRAP terrorism, the September 1975 executions that drew world condemnation, and the Moroccan Green March on the Spanish Sahara during his final illness. Payne's verdict: the regime was strictly personal — its institutions ("atado y bien atado") transferred authority but not legitimacy, and performance legitimacy had already migrated to a society transformed by the [[spanish-economic-miracle|desarrollo]] that no longer resembled the Spain of 1939. produced: the Spanish transition to democracy; enabled: the consolidation of the constitutional monarchy.

## Historiography

Debate centers on whether the transition was the regime's unintended achievement (modernization thesis: development created the middle-class society that made democracy possible) or its repudiation (the transition succeeded against, not because of, Francoist institutions). Payne leans toward the former without denying the latter's force.
