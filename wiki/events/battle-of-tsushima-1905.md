## Narrative

The Battle of Tsushima, fought on May 27–28, 1905, in the Korea Strait between Japan and Korea, was the decisive naval engagement of the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]] and one of the most consequential naval battles in modern history. Admiral [[togo-heihachiro|Tōgō Heihachirō]]'s Japanese Combined Fleet annihilated the Russian Second Pacific Squadron (the Baltic Fleet), which had sailed approximately 18,000 miles from the Baltic Sea to reach Far Eastern waters.

### The Russian Fleet's Odyssey

Russia had dispatched its Baltic Fleet — thirty-five ships — under Admiral Rozhestvensky in October 1904 to reinforce the besieged Port Arthur squadron and contest Japanese control of the sea. The voyage was a catastrophe from its outset. On the night of October 21, 1904, crossing the Dogger Bank in the North Sea, the squadron mistook a fleet of British fishing trawlers for Japanese destroyers and opened fire. One trawler sank; several were damaged. The Russian admiral then continued without stopping to rescue survivors, causing outrage in Britain and nearly precipitating a British declaration of war against Russia. The incident was settled by French mediation, but it illustrates the fleet's state of confusion (Keene, ch. 54).

A French naval attaché in St. Petersburg had assessed the fleet's quality with brutal honesty: "The naval and military value of the 2nd Squadron is less than mediocre. It is not a homogeneous, cohesive organism but a motley collection, a hodge-podge of ships of all ages and types…. The efficiency of the crews is no higher than that of the fleet. There are few experienced officers; the engineering staff is of poor quality; there are no petty officers and most of the sailors have had no military training nor ever been to sea" (Keene, ch. 54).

After a prolonged sojourn in Madagascar (over a month), then French Indochina (where the squadron's presence provoked anti-French demonstrations in Japan), the fleet entered Japanese waters on May 26, 1905 — too late to relieve Port Arthur, which had surrendered in January.

### The Battle

Admiral Tōgō's Combined Fleet blocked the Russian squadron's path in the Korea Strait. The engagement on May 27–28 resulted in the near-total annihilation of the Russian fleet. By May 29, French observers learned that "the 2nd Squadron of the Pacific Ocean had ceased to exist" (Keene, ch. 54). The scale of destruction exceeded any naval battle since Trafalgar (1805), a comparison made immediately by Kaiser Wilhelm II, by Kaneko Kentarō (who called it "the greatest naval victory of the world's history"), and by President Roosevelt, who replied to Kaneko: "This is the greatest phenomenon the world has ever seen. Even the battle of Trafalgar could not match this. I could not believe it myself, when the first report reached me. As the second and third reports came, however, I grew so excited that I myself became almost like a Japanese, and I could not attend to official duties" (Keene, ch. 55).

### Reaction

In Russia: "a shadow of gloom and consternation" spread through the country. The defeat was the tipping point for the czar's war party and accelerated negotiations toward peace. French diplomat Maurice Paléologue predicted that "the naval battle off the island of Tsushima would mark the end of Russian domination in Asia" (Keene, ch. 54).

In Japan: the news was received with controlled jubilation. Emperor [[emperor-meiji|Meiji]] issued a rescript acknowledging the fleet's "unswerving loyalty" and noting the achievement was worthy of being "reported to the divine spirits of Our ancestors" — notably more restrained than the triumphalist language of Western monarchs after comparable victories. Roosevelt opened a letter to Kaneko on May 30 with "Banzai" followed by three exclamation points.

Internationally: even Kaiser Wilhelm, who had previously encouraged Russia to fight, congratulated the Japanese. British enthusiasm was intense (Japan was Britain's ally under the [[anglo-japanese-alliance-1902|Anglo-Japanese Alliance]]). The battle catalyzed the negotiations that led to the [[treaty-of-portsmouth-1905|Treaty of Portsmouth]] (September 1905).

## Causal Analysis

Tsushima was the product of Russian strategic miscalculation: sending an inferior, ill-prepared fleet on an impossibly long voyage to contest waters where Japan had already established naval dominance through the Port Arthur campaign. The Japanese navy, equipped with recently purchased Italian cruisers (*Kasuga* and *Nisshin*) and operating in home waters, held every tactical and logistical advantage. The Dogger Bank Incident had further damaged Russian morale and international standing before the fleet even rounded the Cape of Good Hope.

## Consequence Analysis

Tsushima ended Russian naval power in the Pacific for a generation and made it impossible for Russia to continue the war without recovering control of the sea — something it had no means to do. It was the immediate trigger for Japan's peace overture to Roosevelt (June 1, 1905) and for the czar's decision to enter peace negotiations. The battle was also the proximate cause of Russia's formal recognition at Portsmouth of Japanese primacy in Korea, enabling the subsequent [[korea-protectorate-1905|Korean Protectorate]]. More broadly, it demonstrated to the world that a non-European power could decisively defeat a European great power's naval forces — a fact with profound implications for anti-colonial movements across Asia and Africa.

## Actors

- **[[togo-heihachiro|Tōgō Heihachirō]]** — Commander of the Japanese Combined Fleet; the battle made him the most celebrated Japanese military figure of the era.
- **Admiral Rozhestvensky** — Russian squadron commander; wounded during the battle; the fleet's defeat was partly the product of the squadron's chronic inadequacy.
- **[[kaneko-kentaro|Kaneko Kentarō]]** — Japanese envoy in New York who notified Roosevelt and leveraged the victory into accelerated peace diplomacy.

## Historiography

Keene's account draws on French diplomatic and military sources (Paléologue's diary, the French naval attaché's assessment) as well as Japanese court records and Russian memoirs (Rosen's memoirs on the Dogger Bank incident). The battle is consistently described in contemporary and later sources as historically unprecedented. The comparison to Trafalgar reflects contemporaries' sense that Tsushima was a civilizational watershed — the first time Asian naval power had destroyed a European fleet of comparable size in open battle.
