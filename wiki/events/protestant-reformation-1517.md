# The Protestant Reformation (1517–1648)

## Narrative

The Reformation was the second of the two profound crises that Latin Christianity
survived in the early modern era (the first being the Great Schism of 1378–1415),
and the one that **permanently divided** Western Christendom (R. Po-chia Hsia, CWH
VI). It opened in 1517 when **Martin Luther**, professor of theology at Wittenberg,
attacked the sale of **indulgences** — certificates sold in lieu of penance, one
issue authorized by Leo X to fund the new St. Peter's. Luther's critique, swiftly
printed in inflammatory German treatises for the laity, escalated into a wholesale
attack on the papacy (as Antichrist), on Catholic doctrine, and on "good works,"
asserting justification **by faith alone** and the authority of **scripture
alone**. It found a "deafening echo" among urban merchants, artisans, and peasants,
and the protection of German princes (Frederick of Saxony).

The movement radiated and fragmented: **Lutheranism** across Germany and
Scandinavia; **Zwingli's** reform in Zurich; the **Anabaptists** (crushed at
Münster, 1535); and, most influentially, **John Calvin's** systematic theology and
disciplined Geneva, which spread to France, Scotland, the Netherlands, Hungary,
and beyond. Charles V's military attempt to compel obedience ended in the
**Peace of Augsburg (1555)**. The Catholic Church responded with the **Council of
Trent (1545–63)**, which rejected compromise, clarified doctrine, and launched the
Catholic (Counter-)Reformation. The period 1517–1648 was "the most bloody in the
history of Christianity," ending only with the **Peace of Westphalia (1648)**.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the structural grievance of **anti-clericalism** — a Latin Church
split between a legally privileged clergy and a resentful laity — focused by the
scandal of indulgences. `contributed_to:` the new **university-trained clerical
stratum** (Luther, Zwingli, Melanchthon) that supplied the reform's leaders;
**printing**, which gave Luther's German tracts unprecedented reach; and prior
reform currents (`preceded_by:` John **Wycliffe** and the Bohemian **Hussite
Revolution** after Jan Hus's burning in 1415). `enabled:` the political
fragmentation of the Holy Roman Empire, which left princes free to protect
reformers.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **permanent confessional division** of Western Christianity; the
**wars of religion** (state repression, social violence, and warfare engulfing
Europe to 1648); **confessional state-building** (clergy swearing allegiance to
political rather than ecclesiastical masters); the **Catholic Reformation** and
its missionary energy; and — through the rivalry of competing Christianities — the
**global expansion** of the faith overseas. `contributed_to:` the
[[comparative-religion|"discovery of religion"]] (Stroumsa: the wars of religion
shook Christianity's claim to perennial truth) and the broader crisis of European
consciousness.

## Historiography

Hsia frames the Reformation within the longer story of Latin Christianity's
"repeated recoveries from deep crises" and its improbable trajectory from the
divided, embattled church of 1400 to global dominance by 1800. The Reformation is
treated more fully as a strand of [[christianity]] in the wiki; it belongs to the
standing transition register only implicitly, but its world-historical
consequences (confessionalization, religious war, missionary globalization)
warrant the `transition` tag.
