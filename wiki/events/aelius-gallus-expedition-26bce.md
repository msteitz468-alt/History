## Narrative

In 26/25 BCE, with Egypt newly under Roman control, [[augustus]] ordered **Aelius Gallus**,
the Prefect of Egypt, to lead a military expedition into Arabia. Strabo — whose friend
Gallus was — says the aim was to "explore the tribes and the places" of Arabia; behind this
lay the lure of the South Arabian luxury trade (frankincense, myrrh, spices funnelled
through ports such as **Qani**, "the royal warehouse of the Hadramawt"), a wish to secure
the Red Sea and India–Mediterranean routes, emulation of Alexander, and possibly a desire
to offset Parthian influence in eastern Arabia. The [[nabataean-kingdom|Nabataeans]] and
Judeans supplied substantial contingents.

The expedition ended in failure. Gallus captured **Najran** and Baraqish (Yathil) in 24 BCE
and apparently besieged the Sabaean capital **Marib**, but the siege collapsed for want of
water. His army was wracked by scurvy, thirst, and poor route knowledge; casualties came
overwhelmingly from illness and exhaustion rather than combat. Strabo blames the Nabataean
guide **Syllaeus**, who (he says) led the Romans astray, falsely denied the existence of a
land route from Egypt, and induced Gallus to waste time building an unnecessary war-fleet
and then transports to ferry troops to Leuke Kome — sabotage, some scholars think, to
protect Nabataea's trade position. Gallus retreated to Roman territory with the survivors.

## Causal Analysis

- `contributed_to`: Augustan expansionism and the mythic reputation of Arabia's wealth.
- The immediate military failure is attributed variously to logistics/environment and to
  deliberate Nabataean misdirection — a point of ancient and modern dispute (see
  Historiography).

## Consequence Analysis

Though Strabo judged the expedition a costly failure that "did not profit us," Fisher
(following Sartre, Sidebotham) argues the longer-term consequences were positive: it
stimulated durable Roman interest in Arabia; **Juba of Mauretania** wrote a (lost) book *On
the Arabian Expedition* to prepare a never-executed mission by Gaius Caesar; South Arabian
kingdoms sent embassies to Rome (the *Periplus* calls "Charibael"/Karabil, king of the
Homerites/Sabaeans, "friend of the emperors"); Indian ambassadors reached Augustus; and
post-expedition coins from Sanaa bear Augustus's head. **No further Roman military mission
was ever sent into Arabia** — Augustus's will urged his successors to keep within existing
bounds, and thereafter Rome approached the Peninsula through allies (e.g. [[aksum]]) and
diplomats rather than conquest.

## Historiography

Strabo (embarrassed on his patron's behalf) pins the debacle on Syllaeus; Pliny the Elder's
account is more flattering and glosses over the lack of results — Jan Retsö judges that
"most of the heroic military operations described by Pliny probably never occurred." The
episode is a classic case of how the same failed venture is refracted through the differing
interests of its ancient reporters.
