# Foundation of the Fatimid Caliphate (909–910)

The **foundation of the [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimid Caliphate]]** in 909–910 established the **earliest
sustained Shi'i caliphate** of the medieval Muslim world. In the space of a year the clandestine
[[ismaili-movement|Ismaili]] [[dawa|da'wa]] overthrew the Sunni [[aghlabid-dynasty|Aghlabids]] of Ifriqiya,
freed its hidden Imam, and proclaimed him **Commander of the Faithful**.

## Narrative

For two decades the da'i [[abu-abd-allah-al-shii|Abu Abd Allah al-Shi'i]] had built a mass movement among the
**[[berbers|Kutama Berbers]]** of the Kabylia mountains (from c. 893), fortifying the refuge of **Ikjan** as a
*dar al-hijra*. From 902 he warred against the Aghlabids, and in **909** his armies took the capital
**[[qayrawan|Qayrawan]]**, forcing the last emir to flee. Meanwhile the hidden Imam **Abd Allah** — who had
fled Abbasid pursuit from Salamiyya across a seven-year journey — had been sheltering at **[[sijilmasa|Sijilmasa]]**
in Morocco, where the local rulers now arrested him. The Kutama marched ~1,000 km west and freed him.

On **26 August 909**, outside Sijilmasa, Abd Allah made his public **manifestation (*zuhur*)** as the awaited
**[[mahdi]]**; Abu Abd Allah kissed his stirrup in tears. Taking the regnal title **al-Mahdi bi'llah**, the
Imam progressed east through Ikjan to Qayrawan, entering the city on **4 January 910**. The next day the Friday
**khutba** was pronounced for the first time in the name of a Shi'i Ismaili Imam rather than the Abbasid caliph
— "Abu Muhammad al-Imam al-Mahdi bi'llah, Commander of the Faithful." "The age of the Fatimids had begun."

## Causal Analysis

- **contributed_to:** the twenty-year [[ismaili-movement|Ismaili]] mission among the [[berbers|Kutama]] supplied
  both the army and the ideology of legitimacy — the proximate enabling cause.
- **enabled:** the **decline of [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]] central authority** (unjust taxation, factional
  strife, trade disruption in the late 9th century) opened space for provincial and sectarian challengers.
- **contributed_to:** widespread **Shi'i messianic expectation** of a *mahdi* who would "fill the world with
  justice" gave the movement mass resonance.
- **caused_by:** the immediate collapse of [[aghlabid-dynasty|Aghlabid]] military resistance in 909.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** a Shi'i caliphate that lasted until 1171 and, by the later 10th century, a **triple-caliphate**
  Islamic world (Abbasid Baghdad, Fatimid Ifriqiya, Umayyad Córdoba).
- **enabled:** the **[[fatimid-conquest-of-egypt-969|conquest of Egypt]]** and the founding of **[[cairo|Cairo]]**
  and **al-Azhar** two generations later.
- **produced:** an immediate internal crisis — the 911 execution of the founding da'i Abu Abd Allah — that
  fixed the primacy of the Imam-caliph over the da'wa.

## Historiography

The narrative rests overwhelmingly on **insider Ismaili sources** recovered by modern scholarship — the
eyewitness *Sirat Ja'far al-Hajib* (by al-Mahdi's companion), al-Naysaburi's *Istitar al-Imam* ("Concealment
of the Imam"), and above all [[qadi-al-numan|Qadi al-Nu'man]]'s *Iftitah al-da'wa* — which frame the founding
as the fulfilment of divine inheritance (opening on Qur'an 21:105). Because the Fatimid libraries were largely
destroyed under the Ayyubids, the dynasty's story long survived mainly in **hostile Abbasid-Sunni chronicles**;
Jiwa (following Brett, Halm, Daftary) reconstructs it from the Ismaili material. A live scholarly question is
the **genealogical claim** itself — the Fatimids' descent from Ali and Fatima, denied by their Abbasid and
Umayyad rivals (the "Sabbah" polemic) and debated by modern historians — though the claim's political function
is clearer than its verifiability.
