## Narrative

One week after returning to Okazaki from his flight through Iga, Ieyasu marched toward the capital to avenge Nobunaga against Akechi Mitsuhide. He had barely set out when a messenger from Hideyoshi announced that Akechi had already been defeated and killed at the battle of Yamazaki. Ieyasu returned to Okazaki.

The confusion following Nobunaga's death had left Kai province ungoverned. Kawajiri Hidetake, who had held Kai for Nobunaga after the defeat of Takeda Katsuyori, had behaved arbitrarily toward the local population. With Nobunaga dead, the people of Kai rose against Kawajiri; when he attempted to march out, they killed him and his retainers. Ieyasu moved immediately to fill the vacuum: "Ieyasu did not hesitate, but stepped in and took possession without more ado."

Hōjō Ujimasa of Odawara responded by marching a large army — approximately fifty thousand men — through Shinano into Kai. Ieyasu had only seven thousand, but Sadler notes "there was no comparison between the leaders." Hōjō's force was "led a goose chase by Ieyasu's generals until he was tired of it." Negotiations followed, brokered partly by Hōjō Ujinori, who had previously been a hostage with Ieyasu via the Imagawa. The settlement gave Ieyasu Kai and Shinano, while the Hōjō received Kazusa. Ujinori was married to Ieyasu's daughter, consolidating the arrangement.

Ieyasu extended his domain to five provinces: Mikawa, Tōtōmi, Suruga (already held), and the newly acquired Kai and Shinano. He governed Kai with a mixed structure, retaining local Kai retainers as advisers alongside his own officers, and restored the Takeda temple and built a shrine for the fallen Katsuyori — winning the loyalty of the former Takeda samurai who now served him. Hideyoshi, meanwhile, had defeated Shibata Katsuie and consolidated control over much of Nobunaga's former domain, setting the stage for future rivalry.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[assassination-of-oda-nobunaga-1582]]` — Nobunaga's death removed Kawajiri's patron and created the provincial vacuum.
- `caused_by: [[iga-flight-1582]]` — Ieyasu's survival and return to Mikawa enabled swift action.
- `contributed_to:` Hōjō Ujimasa's reluctance to press his numerical advantage, and the availability of Ujinori as a diplomatic bridge, made negotiated settlement possible.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Ieyasu as lord of five provinces, the second most powerful daimyo in Japan after Hideyoshi.
- `enabled: [[komaki-nagakute-campaign-1584]]` — The expanded military base made it possible to contest Hideyoshi militarily when the confrontation came.
- `produced:` Integration of former Takeda retainers into the Tokugawa force, bringing military and administrative expertise from Shingen's celebrated system.

## Actors

- [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] — seized the opportunity with characteristic speed
- [[hojo-clan]] — rival claimant, ultimately negotiated out; Ujinori became Ieyasu's son-in-law
- Kawajiri Hidetake — Nobunaga's governor of Kai, killed in popular uprising

## Historiography

The episode is narrated in the Mikawa Go-Fudoki and other Tokugawa chronicles. Sadler emphasizes the contrast between Ieyasu's decisive action and Hōjō's "unprogressive and sluggish" leadership. The acquisition of the Takeda retainers — described as bringing "all the experience in military and administrative affairs they had acquired from their former lord" — is treated as a significant force multiplier, though modern historians debate how fully the Takeda administrative system was actually transplanted.
