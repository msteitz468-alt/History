# Revolution of 1905

The **Revolution of 1905** was a wave of mass political and social unrest that swept across the Russian Empire from 1905 to 1907. Triggered by defeats in the Russo-Japanese War and long-standing social grievances, the revolution included strikes, peasant uprisings, military mutinies, and the creation of the first soviets. It forced Emperor Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, establishing a legislative assembly (the State Duma) and initiating Russia's constitutional period.

## Narrative

The revolution began on January 22, 1905 (January 9, Old Style), when imperial troops fired on a peaceful demonstration of workers led by Father George Gapon marching to the Winter Palace to petition the tsar. This event, known as **Bloody Sunday**, shattered the traditional image of the tsar as the protector of the people and sparked immediate nationwide protests.

From Zelnik (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II, ll. 17701–17891): Labour unrest "began to be taken more seriously ... only in the 1870s". 1870 Nevsky cotton-spinning factory strike (St Petersburg's largest, visible downtown location, prolonged, adversarial trial enabled by 1864 judicial reforms, press coverage by 1865 censorship reforms): "Strikes would henceforth be a political issue." 1872 Kreenholm (largest in Europe, riverine island, multiethnic [Russians/Estonians/Germans/Finns], violence/deaths): "propelling a number of disgruntled Kreenholm workers into the ranks of the radical movements". Worker-intelligentsia (studenty) interaction "a central element in the evolution of the revolutionary movement" from 1872; phases populist then SD/SR; workers "repeatedly torn between a positive and a negative perception of the studenty". By early 20th c. "fierce ... competition for worker allegiance" between radicals, liberals, govt; workers "pivotal players".

Zubatov police-sponsored orgs (Moscow/Pale, lectures/tea rooms to divert from socialists); Gapon (spin-off) "Assembly of Factory Workers" in St Petersburg. Putilov firings late Dec 1904 -> illegal strike -> Gapon citywide protest. "On 9 January 1905, thanks to nervous, trigger-happy troops and a government that simply did not get the picture, unarmed workers and their families ... were repeatedly fired upon, with over a hundred demonstrators killed and many more injured. The day has gone down in history as Russia’s notorious ‘Bloody Sunday’, the opening salvo of the revolution of 1905." Petition: class demands (8-hour day, unions) + "something closely resembling the political programme of Osvobozhdenie" (Constituent Assembly, four-tailed suffrage, freedoms) + redemption payments elimination + "hostile reference to the ‘capitalist-exploiters of the working class’" + "visible parading and display of religious icons and portraits of the tsar" reflecting "mixed, labile, internally contradictory state of mind of Russia’s most ‘advanced’ workers". `produced:` [[revolution-of-1905]] opening; `caused_by:` Gapon + Putilov + troops. Throughout 1905, the crisis deepened. Over two million workers participated in strikes; peasant bands burned noble manor houses in central Russia; and non-Russian national minorities demanded autonomy. In June, the crew of the battleship *Potemkin* mutinied. As military defeats in the Russo-Japanese War continued, public confidence in the regime collapsed.

The crisis culminated in October 1905 with a massive, spontaneous **General Strike** that paralyzed the empire. In the capital, workers organized the **St. Petersburg Soviet of Workers' Deputies** (with Leon Trotsky playing a key leadership role) to coordinate the strike and assume local authority. 

From Zelnik (ll. 17907–17951): Shidlovskii Commission (late Jan attempt harness StP workers via elected male reps chosen by workers, no employers in first stage): "the first basically free elections ever held in Russia by workers"; boycotted by workers, "contributed to what became the most important revolutionary innovation to emerge from the labour movement in 1905 – the soviet (sovet)". "the Petersburg soviet was the direct result of the October general strike ... launched on 8 October by Moscow printers and spread by railway workers"; "the first in which the members saw themselves as being not only a local strike committee, but an unauthorised instrument of local self-government". "At least in St Petersburg, the brief period of the October general strike and the soviet’s subsequent dominance of the city should be seen as the workers’ moment of greatest triumph in 1905." Bolsheviks "more reluctantly" recognised it. `produced:` soviets as 1905 innovation; `part_of:` worker self-organisation. Pages: russian-empire.md, saint-petersburg.md.

Faced with the collapse of his regime, Nicholas II accepted the advice of Sergei Witte and signed the **October Manifesto** on October 30, 1905. The manifesto promised civil liberties, freedom of speech and assembly, and the establishment of a legislative assembly, the **State Duma**, without whose approval no law could take effect. 

The manifesto split the opposition. Moderate liberals (the Octobrists) accepted it, and progressive liberals (the Cadets) prepared for elections, while radical socialists (Social Democrats and Socialist Revolutionaries) rejected it as insufficient. In December 1905, the government arrested the St. Petersburg Soviet and crushed an armed workers' uprising in Moscow.

Although the government re-established order through military expeditions and the field courts-martial of Pyotr Stolypin, unrest continued until June 16, 1907 (June 3, Old Style), when Stolypin executed the Coup of June 1907, dissolving the Second Duma and altering the electoral law to secure a conservative majority.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Russo-Japanese War Defeat:** The humiliating military defeats of 1904–05 destroyed the prestige of the autocracy and exposed its administrative incompetence.
- `caused_by:` **Peasant Poverty and Land Hunger:** The incomplete nature of the 1861 Emancipation had left peasants with shrinking allotments and heavy redemption debts, leading to agrarian desperation.
- `caused_by:` **Industrialization and the Proletariat:** Witte's rapid industrialization had created a highly concentrated, exploited, and politically conscious working class in St. Petersburg and Moscow, which served as the vanguard of the strikes.
- `caused_by:` **Bloody Sunday Massacre:** The violent suppression of Gapon's petition acted as the direct catalyst, radicalizing moderate elements and prompting general strikes.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **The October Manifesto:** The autocracy was forced to grant constitutional concessions, ending the era of absolute autocracy.
- `produced:` **The State Duma and Fundamental Laws:** Russia entered its "constitutional period" with the establishment of the State Duma. However, the **Fundamental Laws of 1906** limited the Duma's authority, ensuring the ministers remained responsible only to the tsar.
- `produced:` **Legal Political Parties:** The revolution legalized political activity, leading to the formation of the Constitutional Democratic (Cadet) Party, the Union of October 17 (Octobrists), and the open participation of Marxist and populist parties.
- `produced:` **Stolypin's Reforms:** The agrarian uprisings convinced the government that the peasant commune was no longer a reliable bulwark of order, leading to the **Stolypin Land Reform** to establish private peasant property.

## Actors

- **Nicholas II:** Succeeded in preserving his autocracy by making temporary concessions.
- **Sergei Witte:** The statesman who negotiated the Treaty of Portsmouth and drafted the October Manifesto to divide the revolutionary forces.
- **The St. Petersburg Soviet:** The first workers' council, which demonstrated the self-organizing power of the industrial proletariat.
- **Leon Trotsky:** Reached national prominence as the vice-chairman and chief organizer of the St. Petersburg Soviet.

## Historiography

Soviet historians treated the 1905 Revolution as the "dress rehearsal" for 1917, analyzing it as a bourgeois-democratic revolution in which the proletariat already played the leading role. Western liberal historians, by contrast, focus on 1905 as a missed opportunity for Russia to develop into a stable, constitutional monarchy on the British or German model, analyzing the failure of the liberals and the autocracy to find a compromise during the Duma period.
