## Narrative

The Habsburg Empire perished in the war it had provoked in 1914. Paxton
treats its collapse as close to inevitable by 1918, quoting historian C. A.
Macartney: "in 1914 the future of the monarchy was at best problematical."
Federalist reform projects existed — Archduke Franz Ferdinand himself had
favored extending to the Czechs and South Slavs the local self-rule already
granted Austrian Germans, Hungarian Magyars, and (partially) Galician Poles
and Hungarian Croats — but any new autonomy had to be carved from the
existing shares of Austria and Hungary, both of which held mutual veto power
over further division under the 1867 Compromise.

Through 1916 dynastic loyalty remained surprisingly durable: many Polish
nationalists still hoped for a Habsburg Kingdom of Poland; Catholic Croats
were loyal; even advanced Czech nationalists were still largely federalist.
The war eroded this steadily. Italy's declaration of war (May 1915) and
Romania's (August 1916) gave Adriatic Italians and Transylvanian Romanians an
Allied path to national gratification. Emperor Franz Josef's death at the end
of 1916 severed a personal bond of loyalty. The February 1917 Russian
Revolution gave Poles new hope for statehood on Allied rather than
German-Habsburg terms. The Bolshevik October Revolution had a mixed effect —
it briefly relieved the eastern front, letting Austria-Hungary defeat Italy at
Caporetto (October 1917), but in the long run demonstrated to war-weary
Habsburg subjects how fragile autocracy could be. The first wartime meeting of
the Austrian parliament (Reichsrat), on May 20, 1917, saw Czech and South Slav
delegates openly call for "a federal state of free national states with equal
rights." Belated 1918 Habsburg offers of cultural autonomy came too late, and
disaffected the dominant German and Magyar elites without satisfying the
minorities.

The Allies did not set out to destroy Austria-Hungary; they hastened its
collapse through propaganda for subject-peoples' independence once the
Russian Revolution and American entry made democratic reform a stated Allied
war aim. Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 1918) still favored a federal
solution for Austria-Hungary; by June 1918 the United States had gone further,
promising the South Slavs "complete freedom." The Allies recognized the
Polish and Czech national committees in Paris as sovereign that summer.

Military collapse followed in October 1918: an Allied army from Greece forced
Bulgaria's armistice on September 26, opening a third front against the
Habsburgs; Italy defeated Austria at Vittorio Veneto at the end of October.
When Austria-Hungary requested an armistice on November 4, 1918, much of its
territory was already under the control of de facto Polish, Czech, and South
Slav governments — the Czech national committee had set itself up in Prague
in October, the South Slavs formed the National Committee of Serbs, Croats,
and Slovenes on October 6.

Nation-building, not social revolution, absorbed most of the region's
revolutionary energy. Paxton argues this contrasts sharply with Russia and
Germany: ethnic pride and the excitement of national creation or revival
neutralized class conflict across most of the former empire. Social Democrats
or agrarian parties, not Bolshevik-style movements, took charge in
Czechoslovakia, Poland, Romania, and Yugoslavia. Peasant land seizures in the
region — as in the future Czechoslovakia, where large landlords were mostly
German or Hungarian — read as national liberation as much as class action.

Hungary was the exception. National independence brought no satisfaction
there. Magyar leaders declared independence of the Habsburg tie on October
16, 1918, as a conservative move to preserve Magyar predominance, but could
not secure a favorable armistice; power passed on October 31 to Count Mihály
Károlyi, leader of the small wartime pacifist opposition, who proclaimed an
independent republic on November 16. Károlyi's calculation — that a
democratic Hungary would be rewarded with its historic borders — was
mistaken: the French-backed Allied commander General Franchet d'Esperey let
Hungary's neighbors seize its territory (Romania took Transylvania, Slovakia
joined Czechoslovakia, the South Slavs of Hungary joined the new Yugoslav
kingdom). When Franchet d'Esperey ordered Hungarian troops on March 20, 1919
to withdraw behind a line Hungarians feared would become the permanent
border, Károlyi resigned, and power passed to Béla Kun — see [[kun-bela]] for
the Hungarian Soviet Republic that followed.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by`: military defeat (Vittorio Veneto, the Bulgarian collapse
  opening a southern front) in the final weeks of the war.
- `caused_by`: the cumulative breakdown of dynastic loyalty among Poles,
  Czechs, South Slavs, Italians, and Romanians within the empire, driven by
  Italy's and Romania's 1915–1916 entries into the war, Franz Josef's death,
  and the February 1917 Russian Revolution.
- `contributed_to` (not sole cause): Allied wartime propaganda and
  recognition of national committees (Polish, Czech, Yugoslav) from 1917
  onward, which the Allies extended only once the Empire's collapse was
  already likely — Paxton is explicit that the Allies "hastened," rather than
  caused, the dissolution.
- `preceded_by` (temporal, causation not asserted for the earlier stage): the
  1867 Ausgleich (Austro-Hungarian Compromise), which structurally prevented
  federal reform by giving Austria and Hungary mutual veto power over further
  subdivision.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: the new or greatly enlarged states of Czechoslovakia, Poland,
  Yugoslavia (Kingdom of Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes), and an enlarged
  Romania; rump, German-speaking Austria and rump Hungary.
- `produced`: the [[kun-bela]] Hungarian Soviet Republic, born directly from
  Hungary's territorial and diplomatic isolation after Károlyi's failed
  gamble on Allied goodwill.
- `enabled`: the successor-states' nationality problems analyzed at length in
  [[treaty-of-versailles-1919]] (Teschen, Sudeten Germans, Slovak
  subordination within Czechoslovakia) — see that page's Eastern European
  Settlement material for full treatment.
- `contributed_to`: the postwar economic fragmentation of the former
  Habsburg single market into seven mutually protectionist states.

## Historiography

Paxton stresses the fatalism appropriate to this collapse: "the old monarchy
could no more have been reassembled in 1918 than Humpty Dumpty," citing
C. A. Macartney, *The Habsburg Empire, 1790–1918* (1969). He explicitly
rejects the older "Allies destroyed a viable federal Danubian order" thesis as
overstating Allied agency, while still crediting Allied recognition of
national committees with hastening the timeline. Standard narrative sources
he cites: Z. A. B. Zeman, *The Break-up of the Hapsburg Empire, 1914–1918*
(1961); Arthur G. May, *The Passing of the Habsburg Monarchy 1914–1918* (2
vols., 1966); and Lewis B. Namier's 1920 essay "The Downfall of the Habsburg
Monarchy" in *Vanished Supremacies* (1958).

Paxton's comparative framework — why nation-building rather than social
revolution prevailed in most of the successor states, with Hungary as the sole
exception — draws on Otto Bauer's contemporary warning that independence for
every ethnic group would impede socialism absent a prior international
socialist economy, and treats ethnic/national identity as empirically more
powerful than class solidarity among both workers and peasants in the
Habsburg successor lands. This is a substantive interpretive claim worth
flagging for Controversies treatment alongside the "Great Man vs.
structural causation" standing controversy in CLAUDE.md, since it bears
directly on why the 1917–1920 wave of European revolution succeeded only in
Russia and (briefly) Hungary and Bavaria.
