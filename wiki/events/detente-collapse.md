# Collapse of Superpower Détente, 1975–1980

The breakdown of the détente of the early 1970s, marked by renewed tensions, proxy conflicts, the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, the Iranian Revolution, and the shift to a "second Cold War."

## Detailed Analysis

To be populated from CHCW Vol. III (Njølstad chapter and related).

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] add a specifically European
dimension to the collapse: the **December 1979 NATO decision to deploy Pershing II and
cruise missiles** in Britain, West Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Italy provoked
the largest Western European protests of the 1980s, organized under the banner of
**European Nuclear Disarmament (END)**, founded by veterans of the older CND. They frame
**Reagan's** (1981–89) approach as an acceleration-not-relaxation strategy — vindicated
in Western retrospect by the events of 1989 but viewed by most contemporary Europeans as
reckless. See [[detente]] for the Washington/Bonn distinction that frames this collapse
differently depending on which "détente" is understood to have failed.

## Historiography

Scholars dispute whether détente "failed" or was abandoned: one school blames
Soviet Third-World interventions (Angola, Ethiopia, Afghanistan) for
destroying American support; another stresses the asymmetry of expectations —
Washington read détente as a management regime, Moscow as ratification of
parity and continued ideological competition. [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]]
adds the European dimension: Western European governments (and Ostpolitik's
architects) never accepted the American verdict that détente had died, a
divergence that strained NATO through the early 1980s.
