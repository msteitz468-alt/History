## Narrative

### Background and Pretext

The immediate pretext for the Osaka campaigns was the temple bell inscription affair of 1614, in which Ieyasu's advisers found in an inscription on a bell at the Hōkōji temple — ordered cast by Hideyori — characters that could be read as a curse on the Tokugawa house (the phrase *kokka ankō* rendered as splitting Ieyasu's name). Though almost certainly a fabricated grievance, it gave Ieyasu the legal and moral cover to demand that Hideyori either quit Osaka Castle and accept relocation, or dismiss his large garrison of masterless samurai (ronin). Hideyori refused both options, receiving instead tens of thousands of ronin from across Japan who had flocked to his banner.

### The Winter Campaign (1614)

Hideyori issued an appeal for daimyo assistance but received none; all the great lords were bound to Edo through hostage arrangements. He relied on roughly ninety thousand freelance fighters to defend the castle. The Tokugawa forces — drawn from all over the country — numbered approximately ninety-four thousand men. Ieyasu led one army from Sumpu and Shogun Hidetada the other from Edo; before long Osaka was completely surrounded.

The outer defenses were formidable: "about eight and three-quarter miles round, and everywhere it bristled with guns of all sizes, from the two great cannon called Taro and Jiro, that were mounted in the tower before the Sakura Gate in the Ni-no-maru, and are described as the biggest in the Empire" (*Sadler*). Among the principal defenders, Sanada Yukimura constructed a celebrated barbican — the *Sanada no Dejiro* — before the Hachome Gate, with a moat, threefold palisades, and towers.

No major assault was attempted. Ieyasu's strategy was described as "gripping the throat and stroking the back" — simultaneous bombardment and negotiation. The Osaka commanders debated a bold offensive to seize Kyoto and key strategic points (Uji, Seta, Fushimi), advocated by Ono Harunaga and Sanada, but the majority rejected this course. Ieyasu meanwhile worked to undermine morale: arrows carrying surrender appeals were shot into the castle; a hundred great guns began systematic night bombardment; and a cannonball struck Yodogimi's apartment, smashing her tea-cabinet and killing two ladies-in-waiting in a later shot. These shocks made Yodogimi receptive to peace overtures.

Ieyasu directed diplomatic pressure through women: he sent Acha-no-tsubone to work on Yodo's sister (the Christian-baptized Maria, Buddhist posthumous name Jōkōin), wife of Kyogoku Tadataka. The terms agreed — after some days of negotiation — required that the Ni-no-maru and San-no-maru outer enclosures be levelled, reducing the castle to its inner keep (Honmaru) alone. Hideyori signed a sworn statement renouncing rebellion. Hostages (sons of Oda Yuraku and Ono Harunaga) were delivered.

The critical deception followed: Ieyasu concentrated workers to fill the moats night and day "so that all the defenses except the inner ones were levelled." Whether the defenders genuinely believed only the outer moats were meant, or were simply outmaneuvered, remains disputed. The "Winter Campaign of Osaka" became, as Sadler notes, "a byword ever since for the folly of pacifists."

### The Summer Campaign (1615)

The peace lasted only months. Reports reached Ieyasu that Osaka had begun clearing and rebuilding its moats quietly, recruiting more ronin, and collecting gold. Furuta Oribe, a celebrated tea-master, was put to death with his son and pupils on suspicion of involvement in a plot connected to Osaka. By spring 1615 Osaka's defiant posture was open.

On the fourth day of the fourth month, with Hidetada in nominal supreme command, the Tokugawa armies marched west again. Ieyasu traveled in a palanquin wearing a white lined garment and a tea-colored haori, carrying a bamboo staff cut for him on the road — pointedly without armor, projecting absolute confidence.

The battle was extremely fierce. The Osaka commanders' plan had called for Akashi to loop through the city to attack the Tokugawa rear while Mori Katsunaga, Sanada, and Ono Harunaga made a frontal assault with Hideyori himself appearing at the head of the troops. In the event, only the frontal assault materialized. Mori Katsunaga broke through the Honda and Ogasawara divisions in a devastating charge that rolled on to Ieyasu's troops behind them. The Echizen division wavered and broke, partly from suspicion that Asano was defecting. Sanada Yukimura ordered his men to charge into the gap; the situation became critical enough that Ieyasu, watching with only one attendant, personally directed his staff to hold their men in successive ranks to absorb the shock.

Sanada's last stand became the defining moment of the battle: exhausted, he dismounted, sat on his campstool to rest, removed his helmet, and declared himself "too exhausted to fight any more" before being killed by Nishio Nizaemon of the Echizen army. The Satsuma Chronicle called him "without doubt the first warrior in the Empire, and in the ancient histories we hear of none greater." Kimura Nagato, Goto Matabei, and Suzukida Hayato also fell in the fighting.

Meanwhile Ieyasu is credited with having a messenger sent to Hideyori warning him (falsely) of treachery inside the castle, while an agent set fire to the kitchen so that flames spread. The Tokugawa forces pressed in, setting fire to further buildings. Hideyori, his mother Yodogimi, and their staff retreated to the keep, where they committed suicide. Ieyasu subsequently had Hideyori's eight-year-old son Kunimatsu beheaded at the Rokujo Kawara execution ground — a deliberate act of dynastic extinction: "his determination to leave none of Hideyoshi's line alive to be a possible menace to the house of Tokugawa in the future."

The aftermath was systematic: Chosokabe (former lord of Tosa) and seventy-two others were beheaded; the heads of ronin fighters were displayed on planks between Kyoto and Fushimi in eighteen rows, some with as many as a thousand heads per row (Jesuit missionary accounts). Ieyasu also recovered the remnants of Hideyoshi's bullion hoard from the castle, estimated at roughly half a million sterling.

## Causal Analysis

The Osaka campaigns flowed directly from the structural contradiction of the Tokugawa settlement after Sekigahara: Ieyasu had not extinguished the Toyotomi line in 1600, and Hideyoshi's enormous endowment of Osaka Castle — 650,000 koku income plus a fortified base — left a rival power center that could attract discontented elements, particularly the large pool of masterless samurai displaced by Tokugawa pacification. The temple-bell pretext (`contributed_to`) formalized what Ieyasu had been maneuvering toward for years; the Winter Campaign truce, by eliminating the outer defenses through diplomacy rather than assault, accomplished the military objective without the heavy losses a direct storm would have required.

The Summer Campaign was `caused_by` Osaka's violation of the Winter Campaign terms (re-fortifying the moats) and `enabled_by` the destruction of the castle's outer defenses in the preceding winter. It is likely that Ieyasu anticipated and perhaps deliberately provoked Osaka's post-truce fortification activities precisely to create the justification for a final campaign.

## Consequence Analysis

The fall of Osaka in 1615 was the definitive military event of the Early Modern Japanese order. It:
- Extinguished the Toyotomi house as a political force
- Eliminated the last large pool of organized ronin resistance
- Demonstrated that no daimyo would rally to an alternative power center
- `produced` the [[edo-period]] as an effectively uncontested Tokugawa order
- `enabled` the subsequent Buke Shohatto (Laws for Military Houses) and full institutionalization of the Tokugawa system

Tokutomi's summary, quoted by Sadler: "He was the same at Osaka at seventy-four as he had been at Mikata-ga-hara at thirty-one. His environment had not altered him one whit."

## Actors

- **[[tokugawa-ieyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]]**: supreme strategic commander; conducted diplomatic and military pressure simultaneously
- **[[toyotomi-hideyori|Toyotomi Hideyori]]**: commander of the defense in name; largely passive; committed suicide at the castle's fall
- **[[yodogimi|Yodogimi (Lady Yodo)]]**: Hideyori's mother and effective political authority in Osaka; targeted by Ieyasu's diplomatic manipulation through female intermediaries; died in the keep
- **[[sanada-yukimura|Sanada Yukimura]]**: the outstanding military commander of the Osaka forces; designed the Sanada Barbican; his charge nearly broke the Tokugawa army in the Summer Campaign
- **Ono Harunaga**: chief Osaka councilor; favored offensive strategy; ultimately fled after the battle's loss
- **Kimura Nagato, Goto Matabei**: Osaka commanders who fell in the Summer Campaign fighting

## Historiography

Sadler draws on multiple sources: the letters of the Buddhist monk-adviser Suden, Japanese chronicles of the siege, and the accounts of Jesuit missionaries. The missionary accounts are explicitly flagged as unreliable in Sadler's reading — they inflate Tokugawa losses (claiming thirty thousand attackers killed) and suggest Ieyasu was "forced" to make peace due to near-defeat, a narrative Sadler dismisses against the Japanese evidence. The Satsuma Chronicle, from the Tokugawa side, paradoxically provides the most eloquent tribute to Osaka's fighters, confirming that the battle was far closer than the outcome suggests. The question of whether Hideyori was ordered to commit suicide or did so on his own initiative remains contested in the sources Sadler cites; he notes three distinct contemporary accounts.

The collection coverage through [[sadler-shogun-tokugawa-ieyasu-1937]] is narrative and detailed for both campaigns but lacks modern archaeological or demographic analysis.
