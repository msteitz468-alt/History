# The Fall of Granada (1492)

The Castilian conquest of the **Nasrid kingdom of Granada**, the last Muslim state in Iberia, which
surrendered on **1–2 January 1492** — completing the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] after nearly eight
centuries. Treated by Angus MacKay in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 24b). Its coincidence
within a single year with the **expulsion of the Jews** and **Columbus's** Atlantic crossing has made
1492 the conventional threshold of a "modern" age — though MacKay insists the conquest was, in spirit,
"thoroughly medieval."

## Narrative

The systematic war (from 1482) was waged by the **[[catholic-monarchs|Catholic Monarchs]]** with the
resources of a newly consolidated Castile — noble levies, the *Santa Hermandad*, siege artillery, and
papal **crusade (*cruzada*) revenue** — against a [[nasrid-granada|Nasrid]] emirate weakened by
internal dynastic strife (Muley Hacén, his brother al-Zagal, and his son **Boabdil**, Muhammad XII).
Town by town the kingdom was reduced; the capital, with the **Alhambra**, capitulated, Boabdil
handing over the keys. **Columbus**, MacKay notes, "was present at the surrender of Granada" before
sailing west that October.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the reassertion of royal authority under the [[catholic-monarchs|Catholic Monarchs]],
which made a sustained, expensive war finally possible (earlier Castilian kings, like Enrique IV, had
squandered *cruzada* funds without pressing the war). `contributed_to:` **crusade ideology and
messianic prophecy** — Ferdinand cast as the *Encubierto*/"Last World Emperor" destined to take
Granada, all Islam, and Jerusalem — and the **internal division** of the Nasrid dynasty.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **completion of the Reconquista** and the end of Islamic political power in western
Europe; the **expulsion of the Jews** (decree of 31 March 1492) and, later, the forced conversion and
expulsion of the Muslims, in a drive toward confessional uniformity; and — by freeing Castilian
energies and prestige — the launch of **[[age-of-discovery|Atlantic expansion]]** (Columbus's
voyage), making the event's true scale *consequential* hemispheric rather than merely regional. The
fall of Granada is the Iberian counterpart to the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman conquest of
Constantinople]] (1453) — the two faiths' frontiers moving in opposite directions in the same
half-century.

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

After a ten-year sporadic campaign, Ferdinand and Isabella received Muslim Granada’s surrender **2 January 1492**; solemn entry on Epiphany. Capitulation allowed continued Muslim practice, yet reconquest was complete—**Islam no longer a western European presence**. Henry VII ordered thanksgiving at St Paul’s; Paris praised; the pope soon named them “**Catholic Monarchs**”; victory buttressed Spanish Catholic triumphalism. Three months later (31 March) followed the [[spanish-jewish-expulsion-1492|expulsion of all Jews]]. Logan treats 1492 as pedagogical end-point, not true end of the Middle Ages—“historical heresy.” Genoese Columbus, who entered Granada with the Catholic Monarchs, landed in the Indies (Logan: 12 Nov 1492), named the land San Salvador, and wrote of easy conversion “by love rather than by force”—a “tectonic shift” in world and Christian history.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] ends his Islamic history of the Crusades at Granada. The last Muslim victory on the peninsula was late **December 1481** (Nasrid capture of Zahara fortress, ~100 garrison prisoners). The kingdom fell when internal division met reunited militant Christian monarchs: **Fernando V of Aragon** (from 1479) and **Isabella of Castile** planned a protracted war to extinguish Granada.

In **July 1482** Abu al-Hasan was ousted by his son **Abu ʿAbd Allah (Boabdil)**, splitting the realm (Granada vs Malaga exile). In **1483** Boabdil was captured raiding Castile and forced into humiliating concessions; by **1485** his uncle **Muhammad al-Zaghal** held the Malaga side; by end **1489** al-Zaghal surrendered Almería and Guadix, later sold his claims and sailed to Algeria. Boabdil’s vizier surrendered Granada at **Santa Fe on 25 November 1491**; months later Boabdil formally relinquished the Alhambra. Ferdinand granted lands in the Alpujarras; Boabdil crossed to Fez (d. 1538; descendants said to be beggars by the 1630s per al-Maqqari). Ferdinand broke the capitulations and forced conversions, producing mudejar revolts and mass emigrations to North Africa and the Ottoman Empire; some Andalusis at Salé became maritime *jihadis* against “enemies of God.”

**End-point thesis:** With Granada’s conquest, Islamdom’s Frankish problem was irrevocably reconfigured — Sicily and al-Andalus under Franks; Near Eastern Frankish states and Cyprus destroyed; Byzantine Empire eliminated; Mongols/Tamerlane gone from Mediterranean shores. Only Ottomans and some Barbary pirates still much cared about Franks; later Ottomans focused on Hungary/Habsburgs as a regional European power, not as foes of crusading Franks. The world of Mehmed’s successors “bore little resemblance” to that of Ibn al-Thumna, the Almoravids, or Saladin. Against Jerusalem-centric narratives that declare “Muslims won the Crusades,” Cobb stresses permanent costs: **Sicily taken; al-Andalus lost.**

## Historiography

NCMH VII (MacKay) insists the conquest was “thoroughly medieval” in spirit despite its coincidence with Columbus and the Jewish expulsion; Logan uses 1492 as a **pedagogical** end for a church history while warning against treating any single date as the true end of the Middle Ages. Debates continue over how far the capitulation’s guarantees of Muslim practice were ever meant to hold, and how tightly to couple Granada’s fall with confessionalization and Atlantic expansion. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] uses 1492 as the chronological and analytical terminus of a Mediterranean-wide Islamic history of the Crusades, not merely the end of Iberian Reconquista.
