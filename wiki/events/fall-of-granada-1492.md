# The Fall of Granada (1492)

The Castilian conquest of the **Nasrid kingdom of Granada**, the last Muslim state in Iberia, which
surrendered on **1–2 January 1492** — completing the [[reconquista|Reconquista]] after nearly eight
centuries. Treated by Angus MacKay in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 24b). Its coincidence
within a single year with the **expulsion of the Jews** and **Columbus's** Atlantic crossing has made
1492 the conventional threshold of a "modern" age — though MacKay insists the conquest was, in spirit,
"thoroughly medieval."

## Narrative

The systematic war (from 1482) was waged by the **[[catholic-monarchs|Catholic Monarchs]]** with the
resources of a newly consolidated Castile — noble levies, the *Santa Hermandad*, siege artillery, and
papal **crusade (*cruzada*) revenue** — against a [[nasrid-granada|Nasrid]] emirate weakened by
internal dynastic strife (Muley Hacén, his brother al-Zagal, and his son **Boabdil**, Muhammad XII).
Town by town the kingdom was reduced; the capital, with the **Alhambra**, capitulated, Boabdil
handing over the keys. **Columbus**, MacKay notes, "was present at the surrender of Granada" before
sailing west that October.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the reassertion of royal authority under the [[catholic-monarchs|Catholic Monarchs]],
which made a sustained, expensive war finally possible (earlier Castilian kings, like Enrique IV, had
squandered *cruzada* funds without pressing the war). `contributed_to:` **crusade ideology and
messianic prophecy** — Ferdinand cast as the *Encubierto*/"Last World Emperor" destined to take
Granada, all Islam, and Jerusalem — and the **internal division** of the Nasrid dynasty.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **completion of the Reconquista** and the end of Islamic political power in western
Europe; the **expulsion of the Jews** (decree of 31 March 1492) and, later, the forced conversion and
expulsion of the Muslims, in a drive toward confessional uniformity; and — by freeing Castilian
energies and prestige — the launch of **[[age-of-discovery|Atlantic expansion]]** (Columbus's
voyage), making the event's true scale *consequential* hemispheric rather than merely regional. The
fall of Granada is the Iberian counterpart to the [[ottoman-empire|Ottoman conquest of
Constantinople]] (1453) — the two faiths' frontiers moving in opposite directions in the same
half-century.
