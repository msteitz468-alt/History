# Kapp Putsch (March 1920)

The attempted right-wing overthrow of the Weimar government by
[[freikorps|Free Corps]] units facing dissolution under Versailles, led
politically by Wolfgang Kapp — the former German Fatherland Party founder —
with General Ludendorff in sympathy.

## Narrative

Per Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]): the Ehrhardt Brigade
occupied Berlin on 13 March 1920; the government fled. General Hans von
Seeckt — replacing the loyal Walther Reinhardt — refused to let the army fire
on the putschists ("Reichswehr does not shoot on Reichswehr" in the
traditional account; Evans stresses the army "banned army units from opposing
the putschists"). The putsch collapsed within days before a massive general
strike and the refusal of the civil service to execute its orders — though
Evans notes the same duty-to-the-Reich abstraction that kept officials
working against Kapp "would also keep them working when the state was
overthrown" in 1933.

## Paxton & Hessler (2011) Corroboration

Paxton and Hessler confirm the core mechanics: the [[freikorps|Erhardt
Brigade]] marched into Berlin displaying swastikas on their helmets, and
General von Seeckt refused to order the army against the putschists,
maintaining a studied "neutrality" that in practice let the coup proceed
unopposed by the regular military. What actually broke the putsch was
civilian, not military: bureaucratic non-cooperation from the civil
service combined with the largest general strike in modern German history
to that point, which ended the putsch within four days. Source:
[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: the Versailles-mandated dissolution of the Free Corps and the
  officer corps' hostility to the Republic; Seeckt's conduct revealed the
  army's conditional loyalty to an abstract "Reich," not the Republic.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: the Ruhr "Red Army" workers' rising against the putsch, then its
  suppression by Free Corps and regular army units — "well over a thousand
  members of the Red Army were slaughtered, most of them prisoners 'shot
  while trying to escape'" (Evans; see [[november-revolution-1918]],
  [[freikorps]]).
- Judicial epilogue (Evans): a participant received only brief fortress
  confinement for "selfless patriotism" — an exhibit in Weimar's asymmetric
  political justice.
- contrasts_with: [[destruction-of-german-trade-unions-1933]] — the general
  strike that saved the Republic in 1920 was impossible in 1933 amid mass
  unemployment, a contrast Evans draws explicitly.
