# September 23 Coup in Saigon (1945)

On 23 September 1945, with the active facilitation of British commander General Douglas Gracey, French forces executed a coup that re-established French control over key installations in Saigon. This triggered immediate Viet Minh counter-action and the massacres of French and Eurasian civilians on 25 September, widely regarded as the beginning of the open Franco-Vietnamese war in the south.

## Narrative

Gracey arrived with the 20th Indian Division on 12 September. He ignored the Viet Minh Committee of the South, declared martial law on 21 September, and on 23 September allowed or enabled French troops (previously disarmed by the Japanese) to retake public buildings and raise the French flag. The Viet Minh responded with strikes and attacks; on the night of 25 September groups of Vietnamese killed scores of French and Eurasian civilians in their homes and on the streets.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Gracey’s interpretation of his mission as primarily to restore French authority rather than to negotiate with the Viet Minh provisional government.

**contributed_to:** Long pent-up Vietnamese resentment combined with the sudden reappearance of French power.

## Consequence Analysis

The coup and the subsequent massacres destroyed any remaining possibility of a peaceful transition in the south and marked the effective start of the First Indochina War in Cochin China. It also gave the French a bridgehead from which they expanded control over most towns south of the 16th parallel by the end of 1945.

## Actors

- British: Douglas Gracey.
- French: Local forces and officials (including those released from Japanese internment).
- Viet Minh: Tran Van Giau and the Committee of the South.

## Historiography

Logevall presents the episode as a critical escalatory step for which British policy (and Gracey personally) bears significant responsibility. French accounts often downplay the coup aspect and emphasize Viet Minh “terrorism.” Vietnamese histories treat 23–25 September as the moment the French showed they intended to reconquer rather than negotiate.
