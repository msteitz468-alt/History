# Battle of Pease River (18 December 1860)

## Narrative

In December 1860 a Texas force under Lawrence Sullivan ("Sul") Ross struck a Comanche camp on the **Pease River** / Mule Creek confluence. The fight was small in numbers but large in consequence: soldiers and rangers seized [[actors/parker-cynthia-ann|Cynthia Ann Parker]] (Nautdah) and her infant daughter Prairie Flower (Toh-tsee-ah). Texan reports celebrated the killing of [[actors/peta-nocona|Peta Nocona]]; [[actors/parker-quanah|Quanah]] — a boy who escaped with brother Peanuts — later insisted his father was not killed there. Cynthia Ann was returned to white kin as a public spectacle and never successfully reintegrated.

### Context (Gwynne)

October 1860: Cynthia Ann lived with husband Peta Nocona and three children — Quanah (~12), Peanuts, and toddler Prairie Flower — in a large Comanche operations camp of up to **~500 people** near Mule Creek / Pease River, ~125 miles west of the Fort Worth settlement line (village a mile back from where crystalline Mule Creek met the salty, gypsum-laced Pease). Through summer–early fall 1860 Peta Nocona led devastating night raids (under a "Comanche moon") into counties between present Fort Worth and Wichita Falls, including Parker County; Middleton T. Johnson's Ranger regiment failed (horses stampeded; prairie fired). After Sherman-raid panic emptied frontier counties, **Charles Goodnight** tracked Nocona's ~150 stolen horses to Nautdah's Mule Creek village, then turned back.

### Force and attack

**13 December 1860**, Fort Belknap: Ross (age 23) commanded ~40 Rangers, 21 Second Cavalry soldiers from Fort Cooper, ~70 local volunteers (Goodnight scout). Attack (Gwynne narrative sequence after the 13 Dec muster; page date retained as the conventional **18 December 1860** — some narrative detail clusters on 19 Dec) on **~15 Indians packing to leave**: federal sergeant's men killed squaws "nearly in a pile"; fight lasted only a few minutes — "more of a butchery than a pitched battle." Dogs shot defending the camp.

### Capture of Cynthia Ann; disputed Peta death

Ross pursued the last three Indians; captured a woman with child (breast-show / "Americano!"); shot the rear rider (a woman) then fought a large warrior identified as chief — broke arm with pistol, shot twice through the body; Mexican boy finished with buckshot (Ross account; other accounts add interpreter dialogue / spear attempt). **Anton Martinez** (former captive, claimed slavery in Peta Nocona's family) identified the dead man as **Peta Nocona**. Tally: **12 dead, 3 captured**; loss of ~15,000+ lbs buffalo meat (**69 mule loads**) and **370 horses**.

**Dispute:** Ross/Martinez identification and Cynthia Ann's wailing over the body support death there. Quanah later "forcefully denied that he was even at the Battle of Pease River or that his father had been killed there" — denials Gwynne calls **"untrue,"** aimed at cleansing a disgraceful fiasco on Peta's watch (Comanches saw Pease River as a fiasco and a disgrace). H. B. Rogers: "not a battle at all, but just a killing of squaws. One or two bucks and 16 squaws were killed." Ross's later claim that "the great Comanche confederacy was forever broken" is, per Gwynne, "utter nonsense" — raids of 1864 were among the worst in history; 1871–72 and 1874 still lay ahead.

### Escape of the boys; aftermath for Cynthia Ann

Quanah and Peanuts survived. Goodnight tracked two horseback escapees ~75–100 miles west to a ~1,000-person canyon camp near the foot of the [[places/llano-estacado|Llano Estacado]]; Quanah (per Goodnight later) reported "ten thousand" soldiers, causing winter move Washita–Wichita Mountains and food crisis. Adopted captive boy "Pease" (by Ross) later served as horse tender in 135 Civil War engagements; d. 1883. Cynthia Ann's return to white society became a freak-show "rescue" spectacle (binding, public display); she never reintegrated and died yearning for Comanche life (Prairie Flower also died).

### Sul Ross career note (context)

Ross later became youngest Confederate general, two-term Texas governor, and president of Texas A&M; vainglorious self-account of Pease River as breaking the confederacy is contradicted by Rogers and by later war years.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** 1860 spike in frontier violence under Peta Nocona and ranger offensive policy after Johnson's failure.
- **contributed_to:** Goodnight's tracking of the stolen herd; identification of the famous captive among the taken women.
- **preceded_by:** Decades of mutual raiding after [[events/fort-parker-raid-1836]]; Ford Antelope Hills (1858) and Van Dorn Wichita Village (1858) federal/state offensives.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** Permanent separation of mother and son; Cynthia Ann's tragic second captivity in white society; loss of meat stores and horses for the small camp.
- **contributed_to:** Mythic Texas victory narrative; contested death of Peta Nocona; Quanah's orphan cruelty and white-blood stigma ("more cruelly treated… on account of my white blood").
- **enabled:** Quanah's independent war career in the 1860s–70s and revenge motive for white death of father / theft of mother.
- **not produced:** End of the Comanche war — Pease River was incorrectly remembered as a turning point (Gwynne).

## Historiography

Classic clash between **ranger victory literature** (Ross) and **Comanche/Quanah counter-memory**. Modern and contemporary white participants (Rogers) treat Pease River as closer to a **massacre of a lightly defended packing camp** than a pitched "battle." Gwynne presents both the Texan claim and Quanah's denial, and **adjudicates the denial as untrue** — a rare authorial judgment that should be attributed as Gwynne's, not wiki-voice consensus. Death of Prairie Flower and Cynthia Ann's final years also have variant details across sources. Camp character (women, meat packs, ~15 people at the hit site vs larger autumn village) is essential to scale: do not inflate into a general engagement that "broke the confederacy."
