# Synod of Whitby (664)

The **Synod of Whitby** (664 CE) was a pivotal ecclesiastical council held in the Anglo-Saxon Kingdom of Northumbria. Convoked by King **Oswiu**, the synod resolved the conflict between the Roman Catholic missionary tradition (centered at Canterbury) and the Irish monastic missionary tradition (centered at Iona) over the correct calculation of the date of Easter and the style of the monastic tonsure. King Oswiu’s decision to adopt the Roman calculations aligned Northumbria and the other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms with the liturgical practice of continental Western Europe, integrating the English Church into Roman authority.

## Narrative and Context

Following the arrival of the Roman mission under Augustine of Canterbury (597) in Kent and the Irish mission under Columba at Iona (563), Christianity spread rapidly across the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. However, the two missions practiced different liturgical customs:
- **The Roman Tradition**: Calculated Easter using the Alexandrian tables, which were standard in Rome and continental Europe.
- **The Irish Tradition**: Calculated Easter using an older 84-year cycle that had fallen out of use on the continent.

By the 660s, these differences caused practical problems in Northumbria, where the royal family was divided: King Oswiu, baptized in the Irish tradition, celebrated Easter on a different Sunday than his Kentish queen, Eanflæd, who followed the Roman tradition.

To resolve the impasse, Oswiu summoned a council at the double monastery of Streanæshalch (Whitby Abbey), ruled by Abbess Hilda.
- **The Irish Case**: Presented by **Colman**, Bishop of Lindisfarne, who argued that their custom was inherited from the holy fathers of Iona (including St. Columba) and ultimately from St. John the Apostle.
- **The Roman Case**: Presented by the young Northumbrian priest **Wilfrid of York**, who argued that the Roman custom was universal, practiced throughout the Christian world (Rome, Gaul, Italy, Greece, Egypt, and Africa), and inherited from St. Peter, to whom Christ had given the keys of heaven.

According to Bede, King Oswiu intervened, asking Colman if it was true that Christ had given the keys of heaven to Peter. When Colman admitted it was, Oswiu declared that he would obey Peter, the doorkeeper, lest when he arrived at the gates of heaven, there should be no one to open them.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[christianisation-of-anglo-saxon-england|Roman and Irish Missions]]`: The coexistence of two missionary networks with different origins led to overlapping jurisdictions and liturgical friction within the elite courts.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[christianisation-of-anglo-saxon-england|Roman Liturgical Adoption]]`: Northumbria officially adopted the Roman calculations, and Colman resigned his bishopric, withdrawing to Iona and Ireland with a group of Irish and English monks who refused to conform.
- `contributed_to: [[theodore-of-tarsus|Unification under Canterbury]]`: The decision removed the primary liturgical barrier between the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, enabling Archbishop **Theodore of Tarsus** to organize a unified diocesan system under the primacy of Canterbury, which fostered a sense of shared English identity (*gens Anglorum*).

## Historiography

The primary narrative source for the synod is Bede's *Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum* (completed c. 731), written from a pro-Roman perspective. Modern historians (like Clare Stancliffe) emphasize that while Bede presents the debate as a purely religious issue, the synod also had significant political dimensions, as Oswiu's son Alhfrith supported the Roman faction to challenge his father's authority, and the adoption of Roman customs strengthened Northumbria's diplomatic ties with Kent and continental Europe.

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Differences over Easter (and tonsure/baptism rite) between Irish and continental customs culminated in 664 at Whitby—Bede’s “synod,” a **Northumbrian not national** meeting under King Oswy. Roman party: Ealhfrith of Deira and Wilfrid; Celtic/Iona party: Colman of Lindisfarne and Abbess Hild. Southern Irish and Bangor had already accepted “Roman” Easter—the issue was Iona tradition. Decision by the **king**, preferring Peter’s keys over Columba/John’s tradition; Colman left for Iona. **Broader meaning:** not Rome imposing jurisdiction on a Celtic church (papal jurisdiction then extremely passive) but a **local** issue; long-term, English church organized in Roman fashion with territorial dioceses (Council of Hertford 672; by 737 four northern and 13 southern dioceses); England firmly in Roman orbit; English continental missionaries later worked under papal authority with diocesan organization. Historiography: Bede’s epitaph to Colman and Lindisfarne’s austere Irish regime is generous; cool detached words about Wilfrid leave little doubt whom Bede found more Christ-like.
