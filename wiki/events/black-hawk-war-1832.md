# Black Hawk War (1832)

## Narrative

Short, bloody northern conflict of 1832 between a militant Sauk band under **Black Hawk** and U.S. Army / Illinois militia forces, ending in massacre at the Mississippi River crossing. Michael D. Green, CHNPA I.1 ch. 8, treats it as the armed exception among mostly unarmed northern resistances within [[indian-removal-eastern-dispossession-1780-1880]].

### Background

After southern Removal policy was underway in the early 1830s, Jackson turned north: Ottawas, Potawatomis, Shawnees, Miamis sold last holdings and accepted eastern Kansas lands. Most northern nations resisted without arms. Exception: militant Sauk band under Black Hawk on the Illinois side of the upper Mississippi.

Sauks and Foxes (Mesquakies): patrilineal agriculturalists with mounted summer buffalo hunt—Woodland–Plains transitional. In **1804** a handful of Sauk leaders at St. Louis signed the first Harrison treaty surrendering title but permitting continued use of western/northern Illinois. **1820s lead-mining rush:** government demanded abandonment of the Rock River town; **Keokuk** faction complied; Black Hawk’s hundreds returned each spring to plant corn, arguing the 1804 treaty invalid.

### Course (atrocity-dense)

**Spring 1832:** clash with Illinois militia; Army pursuit; skirmishes along the Illinois–Wisconsin border. Sauk women and children fled to the Mississippi. A **government steamboat armed with a cannon killed many of them while they crossed the river**; **Sioux warriors killed or captured most of the survivors**. Black Hawk and advisers were turned over by Winnebagos to Army imprisonment.

### Aftermath

Sauks remained in Iowa with Foxes for about a dozen years; to Kansas 1845; 1860 to [[indian-territory]] on lands from the [[creek-muscogee|Creeks]]. Foxes never fully cut Iowa ties: **1856** Iowa legislature approved a Fox request to remain; from **1857** land purchase program accumulated about **4,000 acres** on the Iowa River in **Tama County**, where they reside today (Green).

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: massacre of fleeing noncombatants at the Mississippi crossing; destruction of Black Hawk’s band as an independent military force
- produced: serial Sauk/Fox displacements (Iowa → Kansas → Indian Territory); Fox reestablishment in Tama County, Iowa
- contributed_to: clearing Illinois/Wisconsin frontier for settler state consolidation
- contrasts_with: unarmed northern land sales and Kansas temporary removals

## Actors

Black Hawk; Keokuk (accommodationist Sauk leadership); Fox (Mesquakie) kin; Illinois militia; U.S. Army; Sioux (as killers/captors of survivors); Winnebago (surrender of Black Hawk).

## Historiography

Green points to Wallace and Jackson on Black Hawk as standard guides. Atrocity at the river crossing is first-class content—perpetrators: U.S. steamboat gunners and Sioux warriors; victims: Sauk people (including women and children) attempting to cross. Do not soften to a “skirmish” narrative only.
