# Termination Era (House Concurrent Resolution 108)

Legislative and administrative apex (from summer **1953**) of U.S. [[termination-policy]]: HCR 108’s “freedom from federal supervision,” PL 280’s unilateral state jurisdiction, and the cascade of tribe-specific termination statutes through the early 1960s.

## Narrative

In summer **1953** a bipartisan Congress passed two foundational measures:

1. **House Concurrent Resolution 108 (HCR 108)** — stated simply that it was the “sense of Congress” that a group of Indian tribes named in the act should be “freed from federal supervision” at an early date. Marked departure from protecting Native communities as a federal policy goal.
2. **Public Law 280 (PL 280)** — unilaterally turned civil and criminal jurisdiction on most Indian reservations in **California, Minnesota, Nebraska, and Wisconsin** over to the states, abolishing reservation police and courts there. Eisenhower declared PL 280 “still another step” in Indians’ movement toward “complete political equality.”

**1954** termination laws set procedures for **Klamaths (Oregon), Menominees (Wisconsin)**, and tribes in Utah, Oregon, Texas. Southern Paiutes terminated **1957**; Menominees last cut off **1961**. BIA “negotiated” while telling leaders termination was inevitable. Senator **Arthur V. Watkins** of Utah was a leading figure (Washburn).

By President Richard Nixon’s **1970** special message renouncing the policy, over **13,000 tribal members** and **1.3 million acres (445,000 hectares)** of Indian real estate had been “freed” of federal control—tiny fraction of total estate and population, large enough to inject fear into every Native community.

Parallel administrative moves: public-school enrollment of Indians **+>60%** while federal school attendance held steady; Indian hospital transfers from 1952; **1954** health responsibility shift BIA → Public Health Service; BIA Relocation Branch (**1954**) supervising permanent urban migration.

## Causal Analysis

- contributed_to: WWII integration narrative (~25,000 US Indians in forces; perhaps 50,000 wartime off-reservation workers) reshaping non-Indian assumptions about assimilation readiness (Hoxie).
- contributed_to: bipartisan postwar conviction (Eisenhower era identification per Washburn) that reservation/trust status held Indians back economically.
- contrasts_with / reverse of: Collier/IRA community reconstitution and cultural respect agenda.
- preceded_by: Indian Claims Commission Act 1946 framed partly as “final settlement” so Indians could take their place “without handicap or special advantage”—compatible with termination logic even when conceived under New Deal.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: tribe-specific dissolutions (Menominee, Klamath, Southern Paiute, others); state criminal/civil jurisdiction under PL 280 in four states (later extensions elsewhere).
- produced: continental fear climate despite limited acreage share.
- enabled: [[pan-indian-movement]] counter-mobilization—NCAI restructured around recognized tribes; Feb **1954** emergency conference of **43** groups (over one-third of US Indian population) issued “Declaration of Indian Rights” (“Reservations do not imprison us… ancestral homelands”).
- Once NCAI asserted shared separate national heritage and permanent reservation homelands, termination could not be justified as “in the best interests” of Indians (Hoxie).
- Urban centers from relocation became pan-Indian by definition.
- Incomplete repair: Menominee Restoration **1973** (Washburn: “signaled the official end of the policy of termination”); Klamaths a decade later; smaller groups never restored.
- Canadian echo: 1969 White Paper as analogous complete-abolition proposal (withdrawn 1971)—analogous_to this era.

## Actors

- Congress (bipartisan); Eisenhower; BIA; Sen. Watkins.
- NCAI leadership: Mark L. Burns (1944 founding convention); Napoleon B. Johnson; Joseph R. Garry (1953: “nations within a nation”).
- Terminated nations: Menominee, Klamath, Southern Paiute, Utah/Oregon/Texas groups.
- Ada Deer / DRUMS later restoration leadership (Menominee).

## Historiography

Hoxie treats 1953 as the sharp policy departure within the 1880–1960 reservation frame and stresses NCAI’s conceptual defeat of termination. Washburn identifies Eisenhower 1953–61, bipartisan economic-integration ideology, “disastrous” results, Menominee coercion (scholarly consensus), more ambiguous Klamath timber per-capita motives, and 1973 restoration as official end. Contested: whether “equality” rhetoric was sincere reform or land/jurisdiction grab; how far PL 280 remains living termination residue; relation of Claims Commission “final settlement” language to termination. Do not treat 13,000/1.3M figures as small without Hoxie’s fear-injection point.
