# The Wars of the Roses (1455–1487)

The intermittent dynastic civil wars between the Houses of **Lancaster** and **York** (and finally
**Tudor**) for the English crown. Treated by Edward Powell and Rosemary Horrox in
[[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Chs. 21a–b). Both contributors stress that the wars sprang not
from structural weakness in the late-medieval polity but from a specific conjunction of
**royal incapacity, military failure and dynastic rivalry** — and that throughout, men overwhelmingly
preferred effective royal authority to civil strife.

## Narrative

- **Origins (1449–1455).** The catastrophic loss of **[[hundred-years-war-1337|Normandy and
  Gascony]]** (Formigny 1450, Castillon 1453) exposed the misrule of the saintly but incapable
  **Henry VI**, whose government had collapsed into faction (Suffolk, then the Beauforts) and
  bankruptcy. Jack Cade's rebellion (1450) and the return of **Richard, duke of York**, opened a
  struggle for control of the king's person. Henry's mental collapse (1453) made York protector; his
  recovery (1455) triggered the first battle of **St Albans** (1455), where York seized the king and
  killed his rival Somerset — "the collapse of any semblance of normal political discourse."
- **The Yorkist triumph (1459–1461).** After the rout at Ludford (1459) and York's death at
  **Wakefield** (1460), his son seized the throne as **Edward IV** and confirmed it by the bloody
  victory of **Towton** (1461) — the first English deposition decided purely by battle.
- **Warwick's revolt and the Readeption (1469–1471).** Edward's secret **Woodville marriage** (1464)
  and self-assertion alienated **Richard Neville, earl of Warwick** ("the Kingmaker"), who, allied
  with Edward's brother Clarence and then the exiled Lancastrians, briefly restored Henry VI (1470).
  Edward returned to win **Barnet** and **Tewkesbury** (1471), where the Lancastrian heir died;
  Henry VI was murdered.
- **Richard III and Bosworth (1483–1485).** Edward IV's sudden death (1483) let his brother seize the
  throne as **Richard III**, deposing the boy **Edward V** (the "Princes in the Tower"). The
  usurpation "totally destabilised politics"; the Yorkist exile **Henry Tudor** invaded and killed
  Richard at **Bosworth** (1485).
- **The Tudor settlement (1485–1487).** **Henry VII** defeated the last Yorkist rising (the Lambert
  Simnel/Lincoln conspiracy) at **Stoke** (1487) — traditionally the last battle of the wars — and
  saw off the pretender Perkin Warbeck through the 1490s.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the loss of [[hundred-years-war-1337|Lancastrian France]], which "irreparably weakened"
Henry VI; the dynasty's three interwoven problems — **warfare, service and finance** (Powell). The
usurpation of 1399 had "diluted the hereditary principle" and widened the field of claimants.
`contributed_to:` Henry VI's personal unfitness (his household "ruled through the king"), the
crown's effective **bankruptcy** by 1450, and the rivalry of magnate affinities (Neville–Percy,
York–Beaufort) the king could not contain.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the extinction of the male lines of Lancaster and York and the rise of the
**[[tudor-dynasty|Tudor dynasty]]**. Royal authority **emerged enhanced** (as in [[valois-dynasty|
France under Charles VII]]): the repeated **forfeitures and redistribution of estates** undermined
loyalties to regional magnate houses and fostered **direct crown–gentry links** through the royal
household; the chamber-based finance of Edward IV and Henry VII strengthened the prerogative
([[new-monarchy|the English "new monarchy"]]). Horrox insists Henry VII "succeeded because his
leading subjects wanted him to succeed," not by crushing an over-mighty nobility — change was
"circumstantial rather than fundamental." The trauma of civil war lodged deep in the political
culture of Tudor England.

## Historiography

Powell and Horrox write against the older "overmighty subjects vs. the new monarchy" narrative. The
recurring lesson of the wars, Horrox argues, is "the importance attached to effective royal
authority": disorder rallied support *to* the de facto king. Richard III's usurpation was "unique" —
the conjunction of a child king and a man "prepared to step right outside the boundaries of normal
political behaviour" — not evidence of a failing polity. (The wars' wider British dimension — Yorkist
sympathies in [[celtic-lands-and-english-expansion|Anglo-Ireland]] and Wales — is treated under the
Celtic world.)
