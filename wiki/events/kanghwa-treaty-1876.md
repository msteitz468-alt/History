# Kanghwa Island Incident and Treaty of Kanghwa (1875–1876)

The Kanghwa Island incident of September 1875 and the Treaty of Kanghwa (signed February 27, 1876) together constituted Japan's successful "opening" of Korea — a coercive diplomatic process deliberately modeled on the gunboat diplomacy through which Western powers had forced open Japan itself a generation earlier. For Japan, it was the first successful assertion of Meiji-era imperial ambitions on the Korean peninsula; for Korea, it was the beginning of a process that would end in annexation in 1910.

## The Incident

On September 20, 1875, the Japanese warship **Un'yō**, described as on a routine survey mission through the Tsushima Strait (though its operations near Kanghwa Island were almost certainly provocative), anchored off the coast and sent a landing party ashore to seek water. Korean shore batteries opened fire. The Japanese responded with naval gunfire, then the next morning landed and occupied Kanghwa Island, killing thirty-five Koreans and taking sixteen prisoners, at the cost of one Japanese sailor.

The incident was modest in military terms — "no more than a minor clash involving a few dozen men on each side" in Keene's description — but "it was deliberately expanded into a crisis by Japanese officials who used it as an excuse for demanding concessions from the Koreans" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

The incident reopened the dispute blocked at the [[seikanron-1873|seikanron]]. Kido Takayoshi, who had opposed sending Saigō to Korea in 1873 on the grounds that internal development was more urgent, now reversed himself: "although previously the evidence to warrant attacking Korea had been insufficient, firing on Japanese troops constituted an unmistakably hostile act." He proposed himself as envoy.

## The Negotiations

Japan dispatched **Lieutenant General Kuroda Kiyotaka** as envoy in January 1876, with two warships, three transports, and about 800 marines. The fleet was far less impressive than Perry's had been, but the parallel was unmistakable. The Japanese ships anchored off Kanghwa Island, twenty miles from Seoul.

Negotiations lasted several weeks. The Korean commissioners maintained that their shore batteries had fired because Japanese marines in European-style uniforms were mistaken for French or American forces — a claim that avoided both admission of guilt and acknowledgment of Japanese legitimacy. Japanese delegates demanded an apology; the Koreans refused, saying their commandant was not authorized to make one. The parties talked past each other in rehearsed formulas until, under the implicit pressure of Japanese military presence, the treaty was signed on February 27, 1876.

## The Treaty's Terms and Significance

The Treaty of Kanghwa (formally the *Japan–Korea Treaty of Amity*) declared Korea an independent state — a designation Japan used to sever the traditional tributary relationship between Korea and China — and opened three Korean ports to Japanese trade. Japanese subjects in Korea received extraterritorial legal status (immunity from Korean courts), the same privilege that Japan was at that moment strenuously trying to revoke in its own relations with Western powers.

A Western scholar's verdict, quoted by Keene: "As the Western Powers had done with herself, so did she now, without one particle of compunction, induce Korea to sign away her sovereign rights of executive and tariff autonomy, and to confer on Japanese residents within her borders all the extraterritorial privileges which were held to violate equity and justice when exercised by Europeans in Japan" ([[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

The presents given by Japan to Korea at the signing ceremony captured the dynamic: traditional bolts of silk alongside a cannon, a six-shooter, a pocket watch, a barometer, and a compass — the same mixture of ceremony and technological demonstration that characterized Perry's gifts to Japan in 1853.

## Strategic Consequences

The treaty had multiple long-term consequences:

1. **Korea's sovereignty weakened**: The treaty's designation of Korea as an "independent state" undermined the Chinese tributary relationship but replaced it not with genuine independence but with Japanese influence.

2. **Sino-Japanese competition**: China protested but was in a weak position — the 1874 Beijing convention settling the Taiwan expedition had already implicitly recognized Japanese influence in the region. The Kanghwa treaty intensified the competition between China and Japan for influence over Korea that culminated in the Sino-Japanese War.

3. **The seikanron vindicated, sort of**: Japan achieved diplomatic access to Korea without the diplomatic mission/martyrdom scenario Saigō had proposed — but through the same combination of military pressure and unequal terms his opponents had feared would provoke a major war. The difference was timing and method, not direction.

## Historiography

The Treaty of Kanghwa is standardly interpreted as the opening act of Japanese imperialism in Korea — the beginning of a process of erosion of Korean sovereignty that was completed with annexation in 1910. From a Korean perspective, it represents the collapse of the Sinocentric world order that had provided a framework for Korean independence, replaced by direct Japanese power that could not be managed through the tributary system's diplomatic codes. The explicit mirroring of the Perry precedent — Japan doing to Korea what had been done to it — became a recurring theme in later Japanese justifications of expansion.
