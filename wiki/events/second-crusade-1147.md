# Second Crusade (1147–1149)

## Narrative

The **Second Crusade** was the Latin response to **[[siege-of-edessa-1144|Zangi’s capture of Edessa (1144)]]**, preached above all by **Bernard of Clairvaux** and led in the East by **Louis VII of France** and **Conrad III of Germany**. [[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] notes it was by far the largest expedition since the First Crusade, the first led by European kings, and the first to involve invasions on **multiple fronts** — pagan Baltic, Muslim al-Andalus, and the Near East simultaneously — with primary eastern focus via Anatolia to reclaim Edessa (Andalusi activity opportunistic from that angle).

**Anatolian passage.** Saljuqs of Rum under **Masʿud** and the Danishmendids resisted. Ibn al-Qalanisi describes vast Frankish preparations. In **October 1147** Conrad’s Germans were mauled (southern detachment near Alaşehir nearly wiped out; northern force eroded, retreated to Nicaea); Conrad shipped to Constantinople **January 1148**. The French were nearly stopped at **Mt. Cadmus**; Louis and a core reached Antioch by **March**; Conrad’s veterans arrived Acre early **April 1148**.

**Damascus (July 1148).** With Edessa recapture no longer viable after **[[nur-al-din|Nur al-Din]]**’s hold and army losses, newly arrived forces redirected to **Damascus** (Jerusalem’s old strategic concern). The atabeg **Unur** prepared defenses and blocked wells. Three kings (Louis, Conrad, **Baldwin III**) approached via the Ghuta; fighting at the Barada/al-Rabwa; Franks camped near the walls cutting orchards. Unur’s mountain reinforcements plus Nur al-Din and Sayf al-Din at Homs raised the threat of combined resistance. After **four days** the Franks lifted the siege and retreated. Cobb argues the threat of combined **Zangid–Unur** resistance was instrumental in the failure. Subsequent joint Muslim campaigns followed (e.g. **Inab**, June 1149 — Raymond of Poitiers killed).

**Iberian theatre.** Afonso Henriques of Portugal, with English-route crusader auxiliaries, besieged **Lisbon** (began 1 July; starvation and wall breach 20–22 Oct; mosque purified 1 Nov as cathedral). **Almería** fell **17 October 1147** to Genoese, Catalans, and Alfonso VII (massacre and plunder; Almohads retook it 1157). **Tortosa** fell December 1148; Lérida November 1149. Ibn al-Athir mourns Frankish seizure of remaining Muslim holdings amid internal dissension. For Muslim observers, Lisbon crusaders sailing on to Syria and then suffering at Damascus confirmed a near-global Frankish siege “from Spain to Syria.”

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the fall of Edessa and papal/Cistercian mobilization.
`contributed_to:` the ideology of penitential warfare already articulated for the First Crusade.
`preceded_by:` Zangid expansion in the Jazira and northern Syria; death of Zangi (1146) and Nur al-Din’s early consolidation.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` a strategic and moral reverse for Latin Christendom in Syria (Damascus failure).
`enabled:` further Zangid consolidation under Nur al-Din (Damascus 1154; residual Edessan lands cleared by ~1150).
`concurrent_with:` Iberian gains (Lisbon, Almería, Tortosa) that Muslims experienced as part of the same Frankish Mediterranean pressure ([[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]]); short-lived Norman expansion in North Africa in the same years.

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

Cobb’s Muslim-source arc: Ibn al-Qalanisi’s Anatolian slaughter reports; Unur’s well-blocking and call for Zangid aid; the four-day Damascus siege as a botched Frankish intervention in Syrian politics; Iberian losses registered alongside Levantine ones as one Frankish Mediterranean pressure system. Post-crusade Zangid success (Inab 1149; expunging of Edessa’s rump by 1150) made the Zangids appear, for a time, “Islamdom’s only hope” amid concurrent Norman African gains.

## Historiography

Classically treated as a failed sequel to 1099; pluralist historians stress multi-theatre crusading. From the Islamic side ([[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]]), Damascus 1148 appears as a botched Frankish intervention in Syrian politics rather than a simple “holy war” climax, while Iberian losses register in Maghribi and Andalusi sources as continuous with Sicily and the Levant.
