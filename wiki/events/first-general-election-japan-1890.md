# First General Election of Japan (1890)

Held on 1 July 1890, Japan's first general election was the first popular election for a national
legislature in Asian history. It produced a 300-seat House of Representatives under the
[[meiji-constitution-1889]], with opposition parties — notably the Jiyūtō and Kaishintō — winning
a majority of seats. Linked to [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]].

## Narrative

The election was held under the Law of Election of Members of the House of Representatives,
enacted by [[emperor-meiji]] on February 11, 1889 — the same day the constitution was
promulgated. The franchise was severely limited. Women could not vote; men required age
(twenty-five and above), one year's residence in a prefecture, and payment of at least 15 yen in
national taxes. Only 450,365 men were entitled to vote — approximately 1.14 percent of a
population of nearly 40 million. Despite this restriction, about 95 percent of eligible voters
cast ballots, with no penalty for abstention, indicating intense popular interest.

The government made last-minute institutional preparations: on June 28, immediately before the
election, the administrative code was approved; on June 30, the respective spheres of the Privy
Council and the Cabinet were formally defined. The election covered the entire country except
Hokkaidō, Okinawa, and the Ogasawara Islands. A total of 300 seats were contested.

The elections were carried out without significant violence. As R. H. P. Mason noted: "in
complete contrast to what went on at the time of the second general election two years later, the
Government refrained from abusing its executive or judicial powers to secure the defeat of its
opponents. The law was neutral, and so was its enforcement by the police and the higher political
or judicial authorities." The opposition parties, rooted in the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement]],
won a substantial majority in the House of Representatives, immediately creating tension with
the oligarchic cabinet.

## Causal Analysis

The election was the direct product of the [[meiji-constitution-1889]], which mandated a
bicameral Diet with an elected lower house. Behind this lay the decade-long pressure of the
[[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement]], which had forced the government's commitment in 1881
to open a parliament by 1890. The oligarchs — especially [[ito-hirobumi]] — had hoped that by
designing the constitution to limit parliamentary power (cabinet not responsible to Diet; imperial
prerogative sovereign), they could contain opposition. The election result showed that opposition
had organized effectively despite those constraints.

## Consequence Analysis

The election launched parliamentary politics in Japan. The opposition majority in the House of
Representatives immediately challenged the government's budget requests, forcing the cabinet to
seek coalition or use imperial prerogatives to override the Diet. This pattern of executive–
legislative conflict would define the first decade of constitutional government. The election also
enabled the consolidation of [[meiji-political-parties]], which grew stronger with each
subsequent contest.

## Historiography

The 1890 election is celebrated in Japanese historiography as a milestone of political
modernization — the first popular election in Asia — and simultaneously analyzed as a highly
constrained democratic exercise. The severe property franchise excluded the overwhelming majority
of Japanese. The constitutional design insulated the cabinet and military from parliamentary
accountability. Historians debate whether the Meiji constitutional system should be described as
"guided democracy," "oligarchic constitutionalism," or a transitional form toward genuine
parliamentary government that was derailed by militarism in the 1930s.
