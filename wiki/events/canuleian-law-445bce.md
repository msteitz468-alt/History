## Narrative

The Lex Canuleia of 445 BCE, proposed by the tribune of the plebs Gaius Canuleius, repealed the recent Twelve Tables' prohibition (dating from the decemviral legislation of 451–450 BCE) on legal intermarriage (*conubium*) between patricians and plebeians. According to the Roman historiographical tradition (chiefly Livy), the law was passed only after sustained plebeian agitation and was accompanied by unsuccessful plebeian demands for eligibility to the consulship, a demand instead partially addressed around the same period through the creation of military tribunes with consular power, an office open to plebeians.

## Causal Analysis

The law was `caused_by` accumulated plebeian resentment at the codified patrician-plebeian marriage bar written into the Twelve Tables, and `part_of` the broader Conflict of the Orders through which Rome's plebeian population progressively won legal and political parity with the patrician elite over the 5th and 4th centuries BCE.

## Consequence Analysis

The Lex Canuleia `produced` legal intermarriage between the orders, `contributed_to` the gradual erosion of rigid patrician-plebeian legal distinctions, and `enabled` continued plebeian pressure that fed into later reforms, including the eventual full opening of the consulship achieved by the [[licinian-sextian-laws-367bce|Licinian-Sextian Laws]] over seven decades later.

## Historiography

As with much early Republican history, the episode is known only through later annalistic tradition (principally Livy, writing centuries after the events), and modern historians treat the specific mechanics and even the precise scope of the ban's repeal with caution, while accepting the broader trajectory of patrician-plebeian legal convergence across this period as well-attested.
