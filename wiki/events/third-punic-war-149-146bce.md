# The Third Punic War (149–146 BCE)

The deliberate **destruction of Carthage** by Rome — a war decided "long before"
it was declared, ending in the razing of the city and the annexation of its
territory as the province of **Africa**. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]]
Ch. 5 (Harris), whose interpretation makes it a case study in Roman imperialism.

## Narrative

For fifty-two years (201–149) Carthage kept the peace of 201 scrupulously, paid
its indemnity, and repeatedly supplied Rome with grain — while
**[[masinissa|Masinissa]]** of Numidia, exploiting the treaty clause forbidding
Carthage to make war without Roman permission, steadily seized Carthaginian
territory (the Emporia, the Great Plains). Rome's adjudications "constantly"
favoured Masinissa. When Carthage at last armed and fought him (151), it gave Rome
the **technical *iusta causa*** that the majority of the Senate (with Scipio
Nasica) had hitherto thought lacking — even as Cato agitated relentlessly for war.

Rome declared war (149) and sent an exceptionally large force (Appian: 80,000 foot,
4,000 horse — eight legions). Through a sequence of escalating demands — hostages,
then all weapons (200,000 sets of armour, 2,000 catapults surrendered), then the
final order to **abandon the city and move ten miles inland** — the consuls drove
the now-disarmed Carthaginians to desperate resistance. Carthage freed its slaves,
recalled the condemned general **Hasdrubal**, and re-armed.

The siege dragged through 149–148 (the consuls Censorinus and Manilius made little
headway; Manilius extended control of the hinterland). In 147 **[[scipio-aemilianus|
Scipio Aemilianus]]**, elected consul illegally on his reputation, instituted a
thorough **blockade**, took Nepheris, and in spring 146 stormed the city. Six days
of street fighting drove the defenders back to the **Byrsa**; some 50,000 survivors
surrendered into slavery. The city was burned, plundered, levelled, and its site
ritually **cursed**.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: Harris's central thesis — the **habit of profitable, glorious war**
ingrained in the Roman aristocracy, not genuine fear. He rejects both the
"fear of Masinissa" theory and (largely) the "fear of a resurgent Carthage" theory:
Carthage had built **no fleet**, its citizens had not soldiered for fifty years, and
its army was destroyed before war was even voted.
contributed_to: **Masinissa's encroachments**, which both weakened Carthage and
manufactured the technical breach; **Cato's** revival of anti-Punic hatred; the
expectation of **booty, glory, and *ager publicus*** (a commander to rival Scipio
Africanus; the richest city on the empire's fringe). Harris dismisses
commercial-monopoly explanations but allows that ruining Carthaginian sea-trade
and seizing its land promised gains to Roman landowners.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **province of Africa** (annexed by an unusual law creating *decemviri*;
much Carthaginian land became *ager publicus*, a *stipendium* and poll-tax imposed,
Utica rewarded); the **annihilation of a culture** — the high culture of Carthage
(its libraries given to the Numidian princes; Mago's farming manual translated into
Latin by Senate order) destroyed, though Punic language and religion long survived.
concurrent_with: the destruction of **[[corinth|Corinth]]** the same year (146) —
"a hard year for commercial cities."

## Historiography

[[polybius]] (an eyewitness, friend of Scipio Aemilianus) and **Appian** (*Punica*,
dominated by hero-worship of Scipio) are the main sources; the **Epitome of Livy**
is heavily contaminated by Roman self-justification. The decisive interpretive
question — was the war defensive or an expression of Roman *Machtpolitik*? — is
Harris's: he reads Polybius' own buried view (in the four-positions chapter on Greek
reactions) as favouring the **love-of-power** interpretation, and makes the war the
keystone of his case in [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]. The **date of the real
decision** ("long before" 149 — perhaps 153, perhaps 162/1) is debated.
