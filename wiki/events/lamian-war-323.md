## Narrative

The Lamian War (323–322) was the last bid of the old Greek city-states, led by
[[athens|Athens]], to throw off Macedonian hegemony — provoked immediately by the
news of [[alexander-the-great|Alexander's]] death. A complex discontent had been
brewing: a mass of unemployed mercenaries gathered at the great man-market of Cape
Taenarum under the Athenian *condottiere* Leosthenes, and resentment at Alexander's
Exiles Decree, which had forced Athens to give up her cleruchy on Samos. Athens
(funded partly by the remains of Harpalus' treasure), allied with the rising
[[aetolian-league|Aetolian Confederation]] and the Thessalians, won early successes
and bottled up the regent [[antipater]] in the Thessalian town of **Lamia** — whence
the war's name.

The tide turned with bad luck and Macedonian reinforcement. Leosthenes, the one
leader able to hold the coalition together, fell at the siege; Leonnatus crossed from
Asia (and was killed, but his army joined Antipater); and Craterus arrived with
Alexander's veterans. Athens' last great naval effort was annihilated at
**[[battle-of-amorgos|Amorgos]]** — the battle that, for Will, closes the classical
history of Athenian sea-power as Salamis had opened it. The allies were forced to
fight at **Crannon** in Thessaly (322); Antipater and Craterus, refusing to negotiate
collectively, peeled off the allies one by one.

## Consequence Analysis

The settlement ended Athens as an independent power. She paid a heavy indemnity, lost
Oropus, accepted a Macedonian garrison in the Piraeus (Munychia), and — most gravely —
saw her **democracy abolished**, replaced by a property-franchise oligarchy
sympathetic to Macedon. [[demosthenes]] took his own life at Calauria; Hyperides was
executed. For Greece as a whole the war *worsened* the juridical position: the rebels'
own confederation collapsed and was not replaced, leaving the cities bound directly
and in isolation to Macedon (the fiction of the [[league-of-corinth|Corinthian
League]] not even maintained). One contingent legacy: because Antipater and Craterus
were suddenly recalled east, their expedition against Aetolia was abandoned — sparing
the Aetolians and `enabled` the [[aetolian-league|Aetolian League's]] rise to a great
power.

## Historiography

Reconstructed from Diodorus XVIII, Plutarch (*Phocion*, *Demosthenes*), the *Funeral
Speech* of Hyperides, and inscriptions. Will treats the Greek events of 323–322 as
emotionally moving but "fairly unimportant when viewed in relation to the destiny of
the work of Philip and Alexander" — a deliberate corrective to the Athenocentric
sentiment of the literary tradition. See [[hellenistic-historiography]].
