# The Kalmar Union (1397–1523)

The dynastic union of the three Scandinavian kingdoms — **Denmark, Norway and Sweden** — under a
single monarch, founded at **Kalmar in 1397** and lasting (with interruptions) until 1523. Treated by
Herman Schück in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 26). It was the achievement of one of the
ablest rulers of the age, **Margrete (Margaret) of Denmark**, and the dominant framework of Nordic
politics across the fifteenth century.

## Narrative

**Margrete** — regent of Denmark, then of Norway (through her husband Håkon VI), and finally of
Sweden after defeating its German-backed king at Åsle (1389) — gathered all three crowns and had her
great-nephew **Erik of Pomerania** crowned at **Kalmar in June 1397**. The union was a **personal
union of the crowns under the Danish-led monarchy**, each realm keeping its own laws and council; the
proposed Union Charter, which would have bound them more tightly, was never ratified.

Tension between the Danish crown and the Swedish aristocracy and commons recurred throughout the
century. The miners' and peasants' revolt led by **Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson (1434–36)** — provoked
by Erik's taxation and bailiffs — produced the first Swedish national assembly and a tradition of
Swedish resistance; thereafter Sweden oscillated between union kings (e.g. **Christian I** of
Oldenburg) and native regents (the Sture family). The union was finally broken when **Gustav Vasa**
led Sweden out in **1523** (after the "Stockholm Bloodbath" of 1520), while **Denmark and Norway**
remained joined.

## Causal & Consequence Analysis

`caused_by:` Margrete's **dynastic statecraft** and the high aristocracy's interest in a shared
monarchy that preserved their privileges; `contributed_to:` the **Hanseatic** commercial order of the
Baltic and North Seas, whose Lübeck-led merchants were a constant external factor (and, in 1534–6,
made a last bid to shape the Danish succession). `produced:` a century of Danish-dominated union,
chronic **Swedish separatism**, and ultimately the modern division of Scandinavia into a
**Sweden** and a **Denmark–Norway**.

## The Two Kalmar Documents and the Constitutional Question

[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|The Cambridge History of Scandinavia]]
(Schück, Olesen) distinguishes the two 1397 acts. The **Act of Coronation**, sealed
with sixty-seven hanging seals of bishops, counsellors, and nobles from all three
realms, was legally binding and granted the monarch **free disposal of castles and
*len* even after death** — letting the crown anticipate the succession without
aristocratic consent. The **Union Document** was a diplomatically incomplete act of
witness, never fully ratified, setting out common royal election, mutual defence, and
each kingdom retaining its own law. Erik Lönnroth's influential 1934 reading saw the
two as embodying opposing principles — *regimen regale* (hereditary, centralised
monarchy) in the Act of Coronation, *regimen politicum* (elective, aristocratic
constitutionalism) in the Union Document. The current consensus holds the Union
Document went unratified chiefly because it **clashed with [[margaret-i-of-denmark|Margaret's]]
interests**, not (the older "Norwegian explanation") because of Norwegian resistance.

The union's later constitutional arc ran from Act-of-Coronation monarchism, through
the **council constitutionalism** crystallised by the [[engelbrekt-rebellion-1434|Engelbrekt
rebellion]] (1434) and articulated in the Halmstad Recess (1483) with its "paragraph
of resistance," to [[christian-ii-of-denmark|Christian II]]'s absolutism and its
violent end at the [[stockholm-bloodbath-1520|Stockholm Bloodbath]] (1520). The full
three-kingdom union actually functioned only for brief periods after 1450 (1457–63,
1497–1501, 1520–23); otherwise Sweden was governed by the **Sture regents**, and the
turning point was [[battle-of-brunkeberg-1471|Brunkeberg]] (1471). Helle's conclusion
reads the union's failure as structurally determined by the incompatibility of
Denmark's centralising, Denmark-centred monarchy with the constitutional demands of
the Swedish council aristocracy.

## Historiography

The interpretive debate over the Union's founding documents — Erik Lönnroth's 1934 reading of the Act of Coronation and Union Document as embodying rival constitutional principles (*regimen regale* versus *regimen politicum*), discussed above — remains the central historiographical question, and current scholarship (as synthesized in the Cambridge History of Scandinavia) has moved away from the older "Norwegian explanation" toward seeing Margaret's own dynastic interests as the reason the more binding Union Document went unratified. Primary sources are unevenly distributed across the three kingdoms: Danish and to a lesser extent Swedish administrative and charter evidence is relatively rich, while Norwegian source survival for this period is thinner, which has shaped (and arguably skewed) older historiography toward Danish- and Swedish-centered narratives of the union's politics. The degree to which Swedish resistance reflected genuine "national" sentiment versus aristocratic and regional self-interest is also debated, particularly around the Engelbrekt rebellion and the later Sture regencies.
