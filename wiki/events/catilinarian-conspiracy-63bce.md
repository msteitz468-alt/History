# The Catilinarian Conspiracy (63 BCE)

The attempted *coup* of the bankrupt patrician **[[catiline|L. Sergius Catilina]]**,
fusing a desperate **peasants' revolt** in Etruria with a plot to seize Rome — crushed
by the consul **[[cicero]]** in the defining crisis of his career. Treated in
[[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Ch. 9 (Wiseman), who frames it as "the peasants' revolt
and the bankrupts' plot."

## Background: debt and despair

Wiseman roots the conspiracy in the **debt crisis of the 60s** — a convergence of
**dispossessed peasants and Sullan veterans** ruined by bad harvests and rapacious
money-lenders (worst in Etruria), **provincials** crushed by usury, and **ambitious
nobles** who had borrowed hugely for bribery and luxury and could not repay. Catiline,
twice failing of the consulship (64, 63) and facing bankruptcy and prosecution,
"took up the cause of the oppressed" — boasting that only a poor man could defend the
poor — after the failure of **Rullus' agrarian bill** had left rural distress
unrelieved.

## Narrative

After losing the **election for 62** (Sept. 63), Catiline turned to force. An armed
revolt gathered under the ex-centurion **C. Manlius** at **Faesulae** in north
Etruria. Warned by his informants (and anonymous letters delivered to **Crassus**),
Cicero had the Senate pass the **[[senatus-consultum-ultimum|*senatus consultum
ultimum*]]** (21 Oct.). On **8 November** Cicero denounced Catiline to his face in the
First Catilinarian (*"How long, Catiline, will you abuse our patience?"*); that night
Catiline left to join Manlius' army, and was declared a public enemy.

The conspirators left in Rome — the praetor **Lentulus Sura**, **Cethegus**, and
others — blundered by soliciting the envoys of the **Allobroges** (a Gallic tribe,
themselves crushed by debt), who betrayed them. With **written, sealed letters** as
proof, Cicero arrested the ringleaders (3 Dec.). On **5 December**, after a debate in
which **Caesar** argued for imprisonment and **Cato** for death, the Senate voted that
the five had forfeited their citizen rights, and Cicero had them **executed without
trial** in the Tullianum.

Catiline's rebel army — two legions stiffened by Sullan veterans, carrying a silver
eagle of Marius — was cornered at **Pistoria** (early 62) and destroyed; **Catiline
fell fighting**, his troops dying where they stood.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **agrarian and debt crisis** and Catiline's personal **ruin**; Wiseman
stresses the Etrurian rising was probably **spontaneous peasant despair**, not
Catiline's creation, the urban "plot" a separate desperate gamble by bankrupt nobles.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **execution of five citizens under the SCU** — Cicero's great triumph
and his great liability; the deaths of Catiline and Manlius.
enabled: the **polarization** of Roman politics (Cicero hailed as savior by some, as a
"tyrant" who killed citizens without trial by others) — and Cicero's
**vulnerability** to Clodius, who used it to exile him in 58 (Cycle 6).

## Historiography

**Sallust's *Bellum Catilinae*** (a moralizing monograph) and **Cicero's
Catilinarians** and *Pro Murena* are the central, deeply partisan sources; Wiseman
distinguishes the **genuine 63 conspiracy** from the dubious "First Catilinarian
Conspiracy" of 66/65. The degree of Catiline's responsibility for the rural risings,
and the legality of the executions, remain debated.
