# The Battle of the Atlantic (1939–1945)

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]].

## Narrative

The German surface fleet failed serially — *Graf Spee* scuttled (1939), *Bismarck* sunk after killing HMS *Hood* (27 May 1941), *Tirpitz* neutralized by midget submarines and bombing; Raeder was dismissed 30 January 1943 for Dönitz, who saved the surface ships from scrapping but staked everything on the U-boats. Dönitz had wanted 600 boats and began the war with a fraction of that (25 in the Atlantic in summer 1940), sinking 680,000 tons of British shipping by March 1940 but never enough to sever supply. American entry opened the "happy time" off the unlit US coast (485 ships, 2.5+ million tons by August 1942); the Arctic convoy PQ17, scattered on false intelligence about *Tirpitz* (27 June 1942), lost 26 of 39 ships. The climax came January–April 1943 with 120+ boats in the North Atlantic — but Bletchley's re-entry into the U-boat Enigma (December 1942), escort carriers, centimetric radar, and above all Allied shipbuilding outpacing sinkings from February 1943 reversed the exchange. U-boat losses reached one per day in May 1943; on **24 May 1943** Dönitz conceded defeat and withdrew from the North Atlantic.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by` the tonnage-war logic of a continental power against a maritime one; `enabled` (by its failure) the American build-up in Britain and [[d-day-1944|Overlord]]. Evans treats the intelligence seesaw (B-Dienst vs. Bletchley) and industrial arithmetic, not any single battle, as decisive — `analogous_to` the attritional logic of the bombing war ([[strategic-bombing-campaign]]).

## Historiography

Evans writes within the post-Ultra-disclosure consensus: intelligence and industrial output, not tactics, decided the battle; the older "near-run thing" of spring 1943 is qualified by the fact that Allied shipbuilding had already outpaced sinkings from February 1943.
