## Narrative

The colonization of Sahul — the Pleistocene super-continent combining modern
Australia, New Guinea, and Tasmania — was one of the most remarkable
achievements of Paleolithic *Homo sapiens*. Unlike the colonization of
Eurasia, which followed land routes, reaching Sahul required an open-water
crossing of at least 90 kilometres (even at glacial maximum sea levels, when
Sahul and Sunda/Indonesia were closer than today). This demands watercraft
of some kind — the earliest evidence for seafaring navigation in the
archaeological record.

**Sahul's geography**
During the Pleistocene, Sahul was a single continent. Australia and New Guinea
were joined; Tasmania was joined to the Australian mainland. Sea levels were
as much as 125 metres below present at the Last Glacial Maximum. But even at
low sea levels, Sahul was separated from Sunda (the mainland of Southeast Asia
including modern Indonesia) by deep ocean trenches — the Wallace Line. The
crossing was never passable on foot and required intentional navigation.

**The date controversy**
See [[australasian-colonization-timing]] for the full controversy. In brief:

- **Late dispersal model**: humans arrived ~45,000 BP; earlier dates are
  measurement errors; fits a single Out of Africa 2 dispersal ~60,000 BP
  with rapid coastal migration
- **Early dispersal model** (Hiscock et al.): thermoluminescence dating at
  Arnhem Land rock shelters suggests ~67,000 BP; genetic evidence consistent
  with divergence from African populations ~120,000 BP; Denisovan
  introgression patterns imply an earlier wave of modern humans

Firm evidence:
- Rock shelters in Arnhem Land, northern Australia: TL dates to ~67,000 BP
  (contested)
- Lake Mungo, southern Australia: earliest skeletal remains (~43,000 BP);
  mitochondrial DNA consistent with single founding wave
- Broad continental spread: by 50,000 BP humans occupied diverse environments
  from tropical north to semi-arid south to upland regions

**Population dynamics**
Mitochondrial DNA extracted from Lake Mungo remains is similar enough to
modern Australian Aboriginal DNA to indicate no major subsequent colonization
waves. Indigenous Australian genetic diversity is consistent with a small
founding population that spread rapidly across the continent.

The Lake Mungo 3 (WLH3) skeleton — an ochre-sprinkled burial dated to ~40,000–
45,000 BP and one of the world's oldest known ceremonial inhumations — carries
a mitochondrial lineage that survives in living Aboriginal Australians. This is
strong evidence for a **single colonization followed by long isolation with no
population replacement**: the descendants of the first colonists are still
present. (An early claim that WLH3 carried an extinct "non-modern" mtDNA lineage
implying multiple migrations has not been replicated and is now generally
rejected.)

**Skeletal variation: the Kow Swamp question**
The Kow Swamp population (southeastern Australia, ~10,000–20,000 BP) is notably
robust — individuals 10–20% larger than later Aboriginal Australians, with
thick cranial bones and sloping foreheads. Some of this morphology reflects
**artificial cranial deformation** of infants (binding the still-plastic skulls
of young children), a cultural practice that Hiscock argues emerged during the
Last Glacial Maximum as a form of group social signalling and later disappeared.
The robusticity was once read as evidence of a separate, archaic migration
(a "two-wave" model), but is now generally interpreted as in-situ variation and
cultural practice within a single founding lineage.

**Technological innovation**
Northern Australia produced **edge-ground hafted axes by ~35,400 BP** (Jawoyn
country, Arnhem Land) — among the **earliest ground-edge axes anywhere in the
world**, predating ground-stone technology in most of Eurasia by tens of
thousands of years. This contradicts older characterizations of Pleistocene
Australian technology as uniformly simple or "static."

**Environmental impact**
The arrival of humans coincided with the disappearance of most large-bodied
Pleistocene megafauna (*Diprotodon*, giant short-faced kangaroos, the
flightless bird *Genyornis*, giant goannas):
- Most extinctions cluster ~46,000–50,000 BP, broadly coincident with human
  arrival
- The lack of large kill sites (unlike the Americas) weakens the direct
  hunting hypothesis; the one putative association, Cuddie Springs, is
  stratigraphically contested
- The **Lynch's Crater (NE Queensland) *Sporormiella* proxy**: spores of a
  dung fungus that lives on large-herbivore dung collapse ~41,000 BP,
  indicating megaherbivore decline; this is *followed* by a rise in charcoal
  (more fire) and a shift to fire-tolerant vegetation. The sequence suggests
  humans removed the megafauna *first*, and the vegetation/fire changes were a
  *consequence* — not that climate-driven vegetation change starved the
  megafauna. This is a leading line of evidence for a human role, though it
  remains debated. See [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]].
- Removal of large herbivores transformed vegetation, increased fuel loads,
  and increased the likelihood of natural and managed fires
- Australian Aboriginal fire-stick farming (controlled burning) became a key
  land management tool with continent-wide consequences (see
  [[fire-management]])

**Sahul's environmental phases**
- ~45,000–25,000 BP: "great drying" as sea levels dropped; new coastal areas
  exposed; interior became drier; long-distance exchange networks developed
- ~25,000–18,000 BP (Last Glacial Maximum): extreme cold and dry; some upland
  sites abandoned; populations contracted
- ~19,000–10,000 BP: erratic warming; rapid post-glacial sea-level rise
  (>15 m in the single millennium ~19,000–18,000 BP; from ~125 m below present
  to within ~20 m of modern levels by ~10,000 BP). Sahul lost roughly
  **one-third of its landmass**; the brackish Lake Carpentaria and >1,000 km of
  northern continental shelf were drowned, forcing populations inland and
  triggering territorial restructuring. Tasmania was isolated by the flooding
  of the Bassian Plain (Bass Strait) ~14,000 BP; New Guinea separated similarly.
- **Holocene maritime adaptations**: in the Whitsunday region (NE coast),
  rising seas cut a peninsula into islands, and foragers developed open-water
  fishing, capture of dugong and turtle, watercraft, and inter-island transport
  of stone — one of the earliest specialized maritime economies. Tasmania's
  isolation produced documented long-term changes in technology and economy,
  read as a consequence of severed contact with the mainland.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[out-of-africa-2-60kbp]]` — Australasian colonization is one
branch of the global Out of Africa 2 dispersal.

`caused_by: sea-going navigation technology` — the open-water crossing is the
defining requirement; it implies watercraft and navigational knowledge.

`contributed_to: lower sea levels` — glacial maximum reduced but did not
eliminate the required crossing distance.

## Consequence Analysis

This is the founding event of all Australasian human history. The resulting
populations — Australian Aboriginals, Papuans, Melanesians — developed
independent cultural trajectories for ~50,000 years before substantial contact
with other human populations (contact with Austronesian migrants ~3,500 BP
brought dogs to Australia; substantial European contact begins 1788 CE).

The megafaunal extinctions shaped Australian ecology profoundly: the absence
of large ungulates in Australia meant that Aboriginal land management could
not rely on grazing animals and instead centered on fire management.

## Historiography

Australasian prehistory is an unusually active field. Key debates:
- The TL dating at Madjedbebe (Arnhem Land) to ~65,000 BP published in 2017
  is the most recent major intervention — currently contested
- Denisovan DNA in Aboriginal Australians and Papuans suggests a complex
  early dispersal history involving populations who left Africa much earlier
  than mainstream Out of Africa 2 dates
- The megafaunal extinction debate remains unresolved (see
  [[pleistocene-megafaunal-extinction]])

## From CHA Vol. 1 (Ch 1)

*Source: Veth and O'Connor, "The past 50,000 years: an archaeological view," Ch 1 in
[[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|Bashford and Macintyre (eds), Cambridge History of Australia,
Vol. 1 (2013)]]*

CHA Ch 1 provides the most detailed site-by-site dating evidence available in the wiki
for this event. Key additions and refinements:

**Route debate**: Veth and O'Connor present the **southern route** (Lesser Sunda islands:
Flores, Timor → expanded north-west Australian continental shelf) as generally preferred,
as it involves shorter open-water crossings between larger islands. A **northern route**
via Borneo → Sulawesi → New Guinea is also proposed (Morwood and van Oosterzee) but is
"incompatible with the overwhelming evidence for human presence in this region 42,000
years ago" (citing Lene Hara Cave, East Timor, 42,000 BP).

**Specific dated sites** (keyed to Map 1.1 in Ch 1):
- Malakunanja and Nauwalabila 1 (Arnhem Land): 65,000–50,000 BP by OSL — the oldest
  Australian sites currently known
- Parnkupirti (Lake Gregory, Great Sandy Desert): 50,000–45,000 BP by OSL
- Lake Mungo (Willandra Lakes): 50,000–46,000 BP by OSL
- Devil's Lair (south-west WA): c. 47,000 BP by radiocarbon — oldest radiocarbon date
- Mimbi Caves (south-central Kimberley) and Carpenter's Gap 1 (Napier Ranges): c.
  40,000–45,000 cal BP by radiocarbon
- Sites in both northern and southern Australia: 50,000–45,000 cal BP by radiocarbon

**Consensus**: "general consensus…that as sites with ages of between approximately 50,000
and 40,000 BP have been recovered from all regions of Sahul, occupation probably occurred
somewhere on the northern coastline between 60,000 and 50,000 years ago." The original
landing area is now submerged, so "the precise time of human arrival will never be known."

**Early inland spread**: Resource availability in the interior was likely equal to or
better than the Holocene up to 45,000 BP in many regions, promoting early inland movement
rather than long coastal phases. By 39,000 BP the alpine highlands of Tasmania were
settled — globally significant for their extreme southern latitude.

**Route of entry and Homo floresiensis**: The Sulawesi-bypass northern route proposed by
Morwood and van Oosterzee would explain the late persistence of *H. floresiensis* in
Flores; the chapter notes this is nonetheless "incompatible with the overwhelming evidence
for human presence in this region 42,000 years ago."
