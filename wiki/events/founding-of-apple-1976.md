# Founding of Apple (1976)

On 1 April 1976, Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne signed the partnership agreement that created Apple Computer in Jobs’s parents’ garage in Los Altos, California. The immediate product was Wozniak’s Apple I single-board computer, which Jobs envisioned selling as a complete consumer product rather than a kit. Mike Markkula soon provided critical early funding and business discipline. The company incorporated in January 1977. The event marks the origin point for the personal computing industry’s consumer phase and the Jobs-Woz “odd couple” collaboration that produced the Apple II.

**Narrative**: Wozniak, an engineering prodigy at Hewlett-Packard, had designed a computer far more advanced and affordable than existing hobbyist machines. Jobs recognized the commercial and cultural potential, pushed for a finished product with case and keyboard, and handled sales and marketing. Wayne’s brief involvement provided the initial legal paperwork but he sold his stake for $800 shortly afterward.

**Causal Analysis**: The moment was enabled by the specific 1970s Silicon Valley ecosystem (Homebrew Computer Club, accessible chips, counterculture interest in tools for personal power) meeting complementary personalities. Jobs supplied the insistence on user experience and product framing; Woz supplied the engineering that made it real.

**Consequence Analysis**: Directly led to the Apple II (1977), the first mass-market personal computer with color graphics. Established the template of garage-to-IPO entrepreneurship. Set the closed, integrated philosophy that would define Apple’s later dominance.

## Historiography

Isaacson's authorized biography draws on interviews with Jobs,
Wozniak, and other early participants, and is the standard secondary account; Wozniak's
own memoir (*iWoz*) offers a complementary, sometimes divergent, first-person perspective
that emphasizes his own engineering contribution over Jobs's later mythologized role.
Early Apple history is otherwise reconstructed largely from participant recollection
decades after the fact, so some details (exact dates, dialogue, motivations) rest on
retrospective memory rather than contemporary documentation, and popular narratives have
tended toward "great man" framing of Jobs that historians of technology increasingly
qualify with attention to the broader Homebrew Computer Club and Silicon Valley milieu
that made the founding possible.
