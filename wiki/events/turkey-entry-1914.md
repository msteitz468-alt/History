# Turkey’s Entry into the First World War

The Ottoman Empire’s entry into the First World War on the side of the Central Powers (effective late October 1914, formal declarations early November) transformed a European conflict into a global one, opening the Dardanelles, Caucasus, Sinai, and later Mesopotamian and Arab theatres, and providing the platform for Germany’s attempted peripheral strategy against the British and Russian empires.

## Narrative

On 2 August 1914 Turkey and Germany formed a secret alliance. The arrival of the Goeben and Breslau at the Dardanelles on 10 August 1914 (see [[events/goeben-escape-1914]]) obliged the Turks to come off the fence. The subsequent delay until active belligerence reflected Turkey’s lack of war-readiness after the Balkan wars and Bulgarian neutrality, not uncertainty over policy. “With hindsight, Turkey’s entry to the war was ordained from the moment it admitted the Goeben and the Breslau to the Dardanelles.”

The Young Turk triumvirate (Enver as minister of war, Talaat interior, Djemal) held power after coups, the 1913 recovery of Adrianople, and purges. Their objectives were modernization to reverse decline, assertion of sovereignty (abrogation of capitulations 8 September, customs rise 1 October), and consolidation of the Anatolian heartland through Turkish nationalism and Islam. Britain’s impoundment of two Turkish dreadnoughts (29 July) was a propaganda gift; Entente diplomacy offered no long-term guarantees against Russia and failed to end capitulations or provide financial support.

On 8 September Turkey abrogated the capitulations. On 30 September Enver requested a German loan of £T 5 million in gold. At a German embassy meeting 11 October, Enver, Talaat, Djemal, and Halil agreed Souchon could attack the Russians in the Black Sea once £T 2 million was deposited. On 29 October the Turkish fleet (under German officers including Souchon) raided Sevastopol and Odessa and bombarded Theodosia and Novorossisk. Damage was minimal, but “the political purport of the act was unequivocal.”

Russia declared war 2 November; Britain and France followed on 5 November after the Royal Navy bombarded the outer Dardanelles fortifications (3 November). Even after the raid a majority in the Ottoman cabinet initially inclined against full belligerence, but the central committee of the Committee of Union and Progress supported war (17-10); opponents resigned or yielded. “Turkey embraced a war united in its government and in its purpose.”

On 14 November the Sheikh-ul-Islam declared Islamic holy war in the Sultan’s presence, calling all believers to fight Britain, France, Russia, Serbia, and Montenegro and urging the Crimea, Turkestan, India, Afghanistan, Iran, and Africa to make common cause.

## Causal Analysis

Turkey entered through its own devices for its own interests: to secure freedom from great-power control, reverse territorial losses (Balkan wars, Libya), and strengthen the state via centralization and nationalism. The Goeben/Breslau provided both material naval strength and political cover (“substitutes for the forfeited battleships”).

`caused_by:` [[events/goeben-escape-1914]] and secret alliance; `contributed_to:` economic desperation from mobilization (3 Aug moratorium; treasury only £T92k cash); `preceded_by:` Young Turk consolidation post-1908/1913, Balkan defeats, German military mission (Liman von Sanders).

Entente policy contradictions: desire for Turkish neutrality clashed with imperial needs (India route, oil, Arab stability) and Entente commitments (Russia); no concessions offered on capitulations or Russian threat. Germany offered alliance, cash, and technical support despite misgivings about Turkish readiness.

Enver’s pan-Turanianism (Caucasus toward Turan) and pan-Islam (Egypt, Arabs) fused with German global aims but were not identical: “Turkey had no intention of becoming Germany’s proxy.”

## Consequence Analysis

- Military: Dardanelles closed to Entente shipping, strangling Russian imports and exports; new fronts tied down British and Russian forces (Caucasus, Egypt, later Gallipoli/Mesopotamia).
- Strategic: Legitimized and enabled Germany’s “peripheral strategy” (see [[concepts/holy-war-1914]]); “the voyage of the Goeben and the Breslau was an opportunity to widen the war.”
- Political: Russia’s commitment to war hardened; Entente accepted Ottoman partition implicitly while fighting. Ottoman government remained united in purpose despite internal debates.
- Global: Opened the Muslim world as a theatre of propaganda and potential revolt (270 million Muslims targeted); contributed to war’s character as *Weltkrieg*.

`produced:` opening of Ottoman theatres; `contributed_to:` [[events/first-world-war-1914]] global scale; `enabled:` German jihad strategy, Suez attack, Caucasus offensive; `part_of:` World Wars Era transition from European to world conflict.

## Actors

- **Ottoman triumvirate**: Enver (driving force, pan-Turanian/Islamic vision, authorized Black Sea raid); Talaat (shifted to intervention after Entente failures); Djemal (initially more pro-Entente, won over); Said Halim (grand vizier, reluctant).
- **German**: Wangenheim (ambassador), Liman von Sanders (military mission), Souchon (now Turkish-flagged for raid).
- **Entente**: Sir Louis Mallet (British ambassador, initially optimistic on neutrality); Churchill (pushed aggressive line, planned straits attack); Russian concerns over Black Sea fleet and straits.

## Historiography

Grounded exclusively in Hew Strachan, *The First World War, Volume I: To Arms* (2001), Ch. 8. Strachan stresses Ottoman agency (“through its own devices, and in pursuit of its own interests”), rejects simple pawn/victim narratives, and highlights the mismatch between Enver’s grandiose list of possibilities and actual staff work or logistics. The holy war is treated as politically instrumental (grafted Young Turk secular agenda onto Caliphate authority) rather than pure religious revival. Capacity assessment balances Moltke’s dismissive 1914 view against the army’s later defensive achievements and tying-down effect.
