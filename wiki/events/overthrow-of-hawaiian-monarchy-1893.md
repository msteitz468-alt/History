## Narrative

By the late nineteenth century [[hawaii]] had a recognised international standing as a sovereign nation: "As early as the reign of Kamehameha I, Hawai'i was recognised as a sovereign nation. By 1887 Hawai'i had treaties and conventions with a score of nations, at least five treaties or conventions with the United States, and about a hundred diplomatic and consular posts." In 1843 Britain and France jointly recognised Hawaiian independence. By 1892, the kingdom was, in Laenui's description via [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]], "a multi-racial, multi-cultural nation engaged in intellectual and economic commerce with the world. Literacy rates were among the highest of any nation and multi-lingual citizens abounded."

### The Missionary–Planter Cabal

The "'missionary party' — an alliance of missionary descendants and businessmen" had already transformed Hawaiian law, land tenure, immigration policy and trade relations to benefit sugar plantations. The 1875 Reciprocity Treaty opened the US market; the 1887 "Bayonet Constitution" gave the United States exclusive use of Pearl Harbor in exchange for a seven-year extension of the treaty. Under the Bayonet Constitution, the monarchy's cabinet was effectively controlled by the missionary party, stripping the Queen of executive autonomy.

Key conspirators were Lorrin Thurston, "grandson of one early missionary," and Sanford Dole, "son of another." "As early as 1882, Thurston exchanged confidences with American officials on the matter of the takeover of Hawai'i. The Secretary of the Navy assured him that the administration of Chester Arthur would favour annexation. In 1892 Thurston received the same assurance from the administration of Benjamin Harrison."

### The Coup, 16–17 January 1893

Kalakaua died in 1891. His sister Queen [[liliuokalani]] received a petition from "two-thirds of the voters imploring her to do away with the Bayonet Constitution." By 14 January 1893 she had drafted a new constitution.

Thurston and twelve others formed a "Committee of Public Safety" and met with US Minister John L. Stevens, who had "arrived in 1889, convinced that his mission was annexation." Stevens had already written to Washington: "the golden hour is near at hand . . . So long as the islands retain their own independent government there remains the possibility that England or the Canadian Dominion might secure one of the Hawaiian harbors for a coaling station."

On 15 January the conspirators and Stevens agreed that "Marines would land on the pretext of protecting American lives. The revolutionary party would declare themselves the provisional government and turn Hawai'i over to the United States." On 16 January, Marines landed in Honolulu "without warning, and marched along the streets, rifles facing Iolani Palace." On 17 January, "eighteen conspirators, mostly Americans, sneaked into a government building a few yards from the American bivouac" and proclaimed themselves the provisional government, with Sanford Dole as president. Stevens immediately recognised the new government. The Queen yielded under protest:

> "I, Liliuokalani, by the grace of God and under the constitution of the Hawaiian Kingdom, Queen, do hereby solemnly protest against any and all acts done . . . I yield to the superior force of the United States of America, whose minister plenipotentiary, his excellency John L. Stevens, has caused United States troops to be landed at Honolulu . . . Now, to avoid any collision of armed forces and perhaps the loss of life, I do, under this protest, and impelled by said force, yield my authority until such time as the Government of the United States shall, upon the facts being presented to it, undo the action of its representative and reinstate me."

### Cleveland's Repudiation and the Stalemate

President Cleveland, who replaced Harrison before Senate ratification, ordered an investigation. Congressman James Blount's report "denounced the revolution and recommended that the 'legitimate Government' be restored." Cleveland told Congress the overthrow was "an act of war" — "the lawful Government of Hawaii was overthrown without the drawing of a sword or the firing of a shot by a process every step of which . . . is directly traceable to, and dependent for its success upon, the agency of the United States." He refused to forward the treaty.

The Provisional Government, however, refused to comply. The Senate Foreign Relations Committee, chaired by ardent annexationist John Morgan, issued a counter-report "absolving everyone except the Queen." Constitutional stalemate between executive and legislature followed.

### Republic, Annexation, and Incorporation

The conspirators convened a constitutional convention of 37 delegates (19 selected by Dole); candidates and voters were required to renounce Lili'uokalani. "Less than 20 per cent of the qualified voters participated." The "Republic of Hawai'i" was proclaimed on 4 July 1894, with Dole as president. Japanese and Chinese residents who had supported the Queen were disenfranchised.

When McKinley replaced Cleveland, the Republic ceded sovereignty via the **Newlands Resolution** (1898) — a joint congressional resolution rather than a treaty, bypassing the two-thirds Senate threshold. The **Organic Act of 1900** declared all citizens of the Republic US citizens. "The Hawaiian people were given no voice in these changes."

## Causal Analysis

**Primary causes (direct)**:

- caused_by: US strategic interest in Pearl Harbor as a Pacific naval base
- caused_by: 1887 Bayonet Constitution, which subordinated the monarchy to planter control and first granted Pearl Harbor rights to the US

**Contributing causes (enabling)**:
- contributed_to: US Minister Stevens's personal annexationist agenda, authorised (or tolerated) by the Harrison administration
- contributed_to: Post-contact [[pacific-depopulation]] — the Hawaiian indigenous population had "withered" so severely that Hawaiians were demographically overwhelmed, owning "about 14 per cent of the land, by no means the best"
- contributed_to: [[invention-of-the-native]] — the cultural frame denying Hawaiian political competence

**Temporal precedent (not causal)**:
- preceded_by: The Mahele land reforms (1840s–1850s), which privatised land and accelerated Hawaiian dispossession

## Consequence Analysis

**Immediate**:
- produced: Provisional Government → Republic of Hawai'i (1894) → US Territory (1898–1900)
- produced: Haole oligarchy of Thurston, Dole and missionary-descendant families controlling politics and economy "until the 1950s"
- produced: Transfer of Crown lands ("Ceded Lands") — most transferred to State of Hawai'i in 1959; federal government retains "substantial holdings, especially on O'ahu, as military reservations"

**Long-term**:
- enabled: US Pacific imperial expansion (Philippines, Guam) from the same strategic logic
- produced: Hawaiian dispossession of remaining land base — "vast areas — defined as Crown and government lands in the Mahele — were transferred to the control of the United States"
- concurrent_with: The broader pattern of [[pacific-plantation-economy]] restructuring Indigenous polities throughout the Pacific

## Actors

- [[liliuokalani]] — Queen of Hawai'i 1891–1893; yielded under protest; continued advocacy until her death (1917)
- **Lorrin Thurston / Sanford Dole** — leaders of the missionary–planter cabal; Dole became Provisional Government president and first territorial Governor
- **John L. Stevens** — US Minister whose coordination with the Committee of Public Safety was decisive; Cleveland's investigator Blount found the overthrow "directly traceable to" his agency
- **Grover Cleveland** — repudiated the coup in strong terms but left troops in place and deferred to Congress; his moral stance was not matched by action
- **James Blount** — special investigator whose report confirmed US culpability

## Historiography

The Laenui section in [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]] presents the overthrow as a premeditated conspiracy coordinated between the planter–missionary party and US diplomatic and naval agents, documented extensively in Stevens's own letters to Secretary of State Blaine and in Blount's congressional report. This interpretation, now mainstream in Hawaiian historiography, diverges from an older US position emphasising spontaneous popular revolution. The primary documentation — Lili'uokalani's protest (published in Blount's Report, House Executive Documents, 53rd Congress), Cleveland's address, and Stevens's correspondence — makes the coup's US-backed character clear.

The moral and legal dimensions remain contested in US political discourse; the 1993 Apology Resolution (PL 103-150) acknowledged US complicity but did not resolve land-claims questions.
