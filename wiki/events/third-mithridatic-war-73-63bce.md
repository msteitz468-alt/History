# The Third Mithridatic War (73–63 BCE)

The decade-long war that destroyed the kingdom of **[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates VI]]**,
drew Rome across the Euphrates for the first time, and ended with **Pompey's
wholesale reordering of the Roman East**. Fought in two phases — under **Lucullus**
(73–67) and **[[pompey|Pompey]]** (66–63). Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]]
Ch. 8a (Sherwin-White).

## Outbreak

The Peace of Dardanus (85) was never ratified at Rome, and **Murena's** raids on
Pontus (83–81) kept the wound open. The trigger was the **annexation of Bithynia**
(bequeathed to Rome by **Nicomedes IV** at his death, c. 75): Mithridates, allied
with the **Cilician pirates** and advised by officers sent from
**[[sertorian-war-80-72bce|Sertorius in Spain]]**, struck first in spring **73**,
overrunning Bithynia and smashing the fleet of the consul **Cotta** at **Chalcedon**.

## The Lucullan phase (73–67)

The consul **L. Licinius Lucullus** proved a strategist of the first rank. Refusing
pitched battle, he relieved the great siege of **Cyzicus** by cutting Mithridates'
supply ("he stamped on the stomach of Mithridates," Plutarch) and destroyed the
huge Pontic army by hunger and harrying. He then invaded **Pontus**, broke
Mithridates at **Cabira** (71), and overran the kingdom — Mithridates fleeing to his
son-in-law **Tigranes II of Armenia**, who kept him a virtual prisoner.

On the pretext of Tigranes' refusal to surrender the king, Lucullus **crossed the
Euphrates** (69) — without any authority from Rome — and won a stunning victory at
**Tigranocerta** (6 Oct. 69), routing the Armenian cataphracts and razing Tigranes'
new capital. He pushed on toward **Artaxata** and took **Nisibis** (68). But his army
— above all the long-serving **Fimbrian legions** — mutinied, stirred up by the
young **Clodius**; and in his absence **Mithridates returned to Pontus** and
annihilated the legate **Triarius at Zela** (67). Lucullus, undermined at Rome and
stripped of his provinces, was left with his conquests unravelling.

## The Pompeian phase (66–63)

The **[[pompey-pirate-command-67bce|Lex Manilia]]** (66) transferred the command to
**Pompey**. Securing **Parthian** neutrality (Phraates III invaded Armenia), Pompey
broke Mithridates in a night battle in Lesser Armenia and received the **submission
of Tigranes**, who laid his diadem at Pompey's feet and was left his core kingdom as
a "friend and ally" while losing all his conquests. Pompey then campaigned through
the **Caucasus** (Iberia, Albania), pursued the war's loose ends, and turned south
to the **[[pompeian-eastern-settlement-63bce|reorganization of the East]]**.
**Mithridates**, having fled to his Crimean Bosporus, was overthrown by his son
**Pharnaces** and **died in 63** — the news reaching Pompey at the siege of
Jerusalem.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **annexation of Bithynia** and Rome's refusal to settle with
Mithridates; contributed_to by the **pirate alliance** and the
[[sertorian-war-80-72bce|Sertorian]] connection that made it seem one Mediterranean
war.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **end of the Pontic kingdom**, the provinces of **Bithynia-Pontus**
and **Syria**, and Pompey's [[pompeian-eastern-settlement-63bce|client-king
settlement]]; **colossal revenue** (Pompey raised Rome's income by perhaps 70%).
enabled: the first direct **Roman–Parthian** frontier on the Euphrates (Cycle 6's
Carrhae) and the political supremacy [[pompey|Pompey]] brought home in 62.

## Historiography

Appian's *Mithridatica*, Plutarch's *Lucullus* and *Pompey*, Cassius Dio, the local
historian **Memnon** (Heraclea), and **Strabo** (himself of a Pontic administrative
family). Sherwin-White stresses that **Lucullus had no Roman mandate** for the
Armenian invasion, and that the rival traditions on Pompey are shaped by his
court-historians (Theophanes, Posidonius) and his Roman enemies — including the
later-invented tale of Mithridates' planned overland invasion of Italy.
