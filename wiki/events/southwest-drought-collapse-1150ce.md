# Southwest Drought and Collapse (~1150–1300 CE)

Environmental changes after 1150 CE forced new strategies upon all peoples of the Greater Southwest, producing the most widespread population displacement in the prehistory of the region. A severe drought from 1276 to 1299 CE, precisely dated by dendrochronology, delivered the final blow to communities already stressed by a longer period of climatic deterioration. The result was the abandonment of vast areas of the Colorado Plateau, refugee movements on a scale unprecedented in southwestern prehistory, and the formation of new multiethnic communities across the surviving agricultural zones.

## Narrative

The Ancestral Pueblo tradition reached its maximum geographic extent by around 1150 CE, with communities spread across portions of five modern states. Environmental conditions then turned: hotter and drier conditions replaced the cooler, wetter regime that had sustained agricultural expansion. The western margins of Ancestral Pueblo territory contracted first. Simultaneously, Hohokam communities around modern Phoenix began experiencing widespread erosion of their irrigation canals as early as 1020–1160 CE. Some Mogollon communities dispersed as early as 1150 CE.

The catastrophic episode was a severe drought from 1276 to 1299 CE, established by tree-ring evidence to within a year. Outermost villages collapsed first; their populations retreated to more centrally located surviving communities, stressing the available resource base of increasingly large population centers. As resources became scarcer, warfare broke out between communities competing for water and arable land. The combination of agricultural failure, social stress, and conflict produced cascading abandonment.

By around 1300 CE, Ancestral Pueblo communities had completely abandoned the San Juan River drainage. The Mesa Verde cliff dwellings — Cliff Palace and Spruce Tree House — were among the last occupied sites, mainly dating to 1250–1280 CE. Their defensive placement in alcoves high above valley floors suggests a pervasive sense of threat in the final decades of occupation.

## Hohokam and Mogollon Impacts

Hard times for Hohokam communities began as early as 1020–1160 CE, when widespread erosion damaged the elaborate canal systems on which Sonoran Desert farming depended. Larger towns were partially or entirely abandoned. Many Hohokam communities underwent significant demographic disruption. The Hohokam Classic period eventually ended around 1450 CE, but the stress began a century or more earlier.

Mogollon communities showed variable timing. Some dispersed by 1150 CE; others, particularly in Paquimé's sphere, persisted until 1450 CE. Mimbres pottery, which had reached its peak 1000–1130 CE, disappeared by 1150 CE, replaced by other ceramic forms. The spread of Salado pottery styles in the thirteenth century across the Hohokam and Mogollon ranges resulted from the migration of Ancestral Pueblo refugees southward into still-viable farming zones, where they mixed with existing populations, producing the Salado phenomenon — a derived culture of refugee communities amalgamating under stress.

## Consequences: Reorganization

The population movements produced major reorganization of southwestern societies:

- **Rio Grande aggregation**: People from Mesa Verde relocated to the Rio Grande area, augmenting existing village populations in northern New Mexico. Sites like Bandelier, Pecos, and Kuaua have long prehistoric sequences and persisted into the Spanish colonial period.
- **Hopi and Zuni origins**: Chacoan peoples dispersed and founded new villages; some merged with communities where their Hopi descendants still live. Another branch concentrated near modern Zuni.
- **Multiethnic community formation**: Communities formed from refugees of different origins, producing cultural amalgamation and new identities.
- **Kachina Cult expansion**: The Kachina Cult (also spelled Katsina), focused on over 200 supernatural beings important in modern Hopi and Pueblo religion, spread rapidly during the dislocations. It appeared in nearly all Pueblo religious systems after population relocations and may have served to integrate diverse communities joining new villages.
- **Navajo and Apache arrival**: Speakers of Athapaskan languages (Navajos and Apaches), migrating southward from Canada probably along the eastern Rocky Mountains, entered the region after 1500 CE — moving into areas already largely abandoned by farmers rather than displacing them.

## Historiography

The 1276–1299 CE drought is one of the best-documented climatic episodes in North American prehistory, established by tree-ring data from beams in archaeological sites. Dendrochronology — tree-ring dating — allows year-by-year reconstruction of moisture conditions for the period. The broader question of why the abandonment was so complete — why communities did not simply ride out the drought as they had shorter episodes before — has generated extensive debate. Warfare, social breakdown (including the collapse of Chacoan integrative systems), soil exhaustion, and disease have all been proposed as contributing factors alongside climate. The transition also raises questions about the Kachina Cult: whether it arose partly in response to the Chacoan collapse or was an independent religious development that spread opportunistically during population movements. Kohler and colleagues' modeling work at Mesa Verde has been particularly influential in demonstrating the demographic consequences of sequential crop failures.
