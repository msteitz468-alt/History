# King Philip's War / Metacom's War (1675–1676)

## Narrative

War between southern New England Native nations—[[wampanoag]] (Pokanoket) under Metacom (“King Philip”), [[narragansett]], Nipmucks, Pocumtucks—and the English colonies, among the deadliest conflicts relative to population in American history. Salisbury (CHNPA I.1 ch. 7).

### Causes in Salisbury’s frame

Algonquian–Iroquois polarization ended the southern New England fur trade and wampum trade to [[mohawk]], depriving Natives of powerful outsider ties as English demographic/economic growth pressed expansion. Traders claimed land for debts; authorities seized criminal Indians’ lands; missionaries reduced villages; alcohol and market economy eroded the land base.

### Course

Mohegans, [[pequot]], and Massachusetts (ties to outside Indians severed earlier) sided with English. Initial Algonquian surprise-attack advantage. **Turning point January 1676:** New York–armed Mohawks attacked and dispersed a multi-colony Algonquian conclave and pursued Metacom back to New England. “The combination of hunger, disease, the Mohawks, and a revitalized English military effort that made use of friendly Indians as scouts and troops, led to the uprising’s defeat.”

### Concurrent Eastern Abenaki war (1676–78)

Eastern [[abenaki]] had sought neutrality on the Maine coast; by early 1670s resented abusive English traders and settler violence. When King Philip’s War broke out they initially tried neutrality; English restricted ammunition and colonists deliberately drowned an Indian child—all Eastern Abenakis to arms by summer 1676. Unlike southern New England, sides fought to a standoff; peace August 1677, formalized treaty the following year.

### Aftermath

Coastal southern New England Natives suffered severe population losses, reduced to scattered enclaves with cultural autonomy but restricted subsistence and political power; land-sale pressures intensified. Wars enabled New York Governor Edmund Andros’s **Covenant Chain** (1677)—Mohawk/Mahican and New England colonies agreed to deal with each other’s tributary Indians only through formal Albany conferences—broadening Iroquois intercolonial broker role for much of the next century.

## Actors

## Historiography

Colonial captivity narratives and Puritan histories long dominated; modern ethnohistory (Salisbury, Jennings, Cronon et al.) centers Native strategy, inter-Native politics, and land/trade structural causes. Name “King Philip’s War” is colonial; Metacom’s War preferred by many scholars. Mohawk role as turning point is load-bearing in Salisbury’s account and ties New England to Hudson–Iroquoia system.
