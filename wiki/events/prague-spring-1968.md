# Prague Spring
## Narrative
In January 1968, Alexander Dubcek replaced the Stalinist Antonin Novotny as First Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia. Dubcek launched a program of liberalization, seeking to create "socialism with a human face." The reforms included the abolition of political censorship, economic decentralization, and the legal right to travel. The reform movement caused deep anxiety in Moscow and among conservative satellite leaders. Despite face-to-face negotiations, on the night of August 20, 1968, Soviet troops alongside units from Poland, East Germany, Hungary, and Bulgaria invaded and occupied Czechoslovakia. Dubcek instructed the army not to resist, minimizing bloodshed, and he was subsequently replaced by Gustav Husak, initiating the era of "normalization."

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` **fear of the disintegration of the Warsaw Pact** (although Dubcek promised to remain in the alliance); **ideological hatred of the Czech reforms** (especially the abolition of censorship which allowed criticism of the Soviet Union; Line 6741); and the **urgings of Soviet allies** (especially East Germany's Ulbricht and Poland's Gomulka) who feared that liberalization would threaten their own regimes.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the **formulation of the Brezhnev Doctrine**, which asserted the right of the Soviet Union to intervene militarily in any socialist state where socialist rule was deemed to be threatened.
- `produced:` the **forcible termination of Czech reforms** and the ouster of Dubcek, replaced by Husak who restored strict censorship and centralized control.
- `enabled:` the **consolidation of Soviet control over Eastern Europe**, demonstrating that Moscow would use force to prevent any deviation from the established Soviet model.
- `produced:` **damage to the moral standing of the USSR** in the West, prompting protests from Western communist parties (Eurocommunism) and dissident movements within the Soviet Union itself.

## Actors
- **Leonid Brezhnev**, who ordered the invasion; and **Alexander Dubcek**, who led the Czechoslovak reform program. See [[brezhnev-leonid]].

## Historiography
Riasanovsky notes that the invasion was a preemptive move to prevent the contagion of liberalization from spreading to the Soviet Union and other satellite states, demonstrating the limits of national sovereignty in the Warsaw Pact (Line 6741).
