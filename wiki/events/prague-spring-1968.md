# Prague Spring

## Narrative
Czechoslovak party leader Antonin Novotný had become unpopular. Brezhnev visited Prague Dec 1967 and refused to intervene in the factional dispute. Novotný resigned Jan 1968; Alexander Dubček succeeded. "Dubček and his colleagues allowed the emergence of independent pressure groups; they allowed the mass media to criticize the Czechoslovak official authorities, not excluding himself. The trade unions resumed the role of defence of workers' interests, and market reforms of the Hungarian type were treated as a minimum short-term aim. Dubček, hoping to create a 'socialism with a human face', still thought of himself as a Leninist. But by introducing so many checks on the communist party dictatorship, he was unknowingly rejecting the main tenets of Lenin's thought and practice" (lines 2773–2774).

His cardinal error was assuming he could pull the Soviet Politburo along. In Moscow the reforms were seen as threatening one-party rule, centrally-planned economy and Eastern Europe as exclusively communist zone. Brezhnev sent emissaries over summer. Dubček ignored hints. On the night of 20–21 August 1968 the tanks of the Warsaw Pact rolled into Czechoslovakia. Decision taken in Soviet Politburo (Kosygin had wavered earlier; vote unanimous). Dubček taken prisoner, flown to Russia, injected with drugs and threatened with execution unless he complied. He succumbed with heavy reluctance. In spring 1969 Husák installed. "If I hadn't voted in the Politburo for military intervention, I probably wouldn't be sitting here now" (Brezhnev later). Hungarian Kádár had warned Dubček: "Don't you understand what kind of people you are dealing with?" (lines 2775–2779).

After brief period as ambassador to Turkey, Dubček demoted to local forest administrator. Bloodless purge of Prague Spring participants. No Warsaw Pact country permitted slightest derogation from one-party state, Marxism-Leninism and Pact membership. Brezhnev Doctrine imposed. The invasion damaged USSR irreparably inside global communist movement; world communism became definitively polycentric (lines 2779–2785).
## Narrative
In January 1968, Alexander Dubcek replaced the Stalinist Antonin Novotny as First Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia. Dubcek launched a program of liberalization, seeking to create "socialism with a human face." The reforms included the abolition of political censorship, economic decentralization, and the legal right to travel. The reform movement caused deep anxiety in Moscow and among conservative satellite leaders. Despite face-to-face negotiations, on the night of August 20, 1968, Soviet troops alongside units from Poland, East Germany, Hungary, and Bulgaria invaded and occupied Czechoslovakia. Dubcek instructed the army not to resist, minimizing bloodshed, and he was subsequently replaced by Gustav Husak, initiating the era of "normalization."

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` **fear of the disintegration of the Warsaw Pact** (although Dubcek promised to remain in the alliance); **ideological hatred of the Czech reforms** (especially the abolition of censorship which allowed criticism of the Soviet Union; Line 6741); and the **urgings of Soviet allies** (especially East Germany's Ulbricht and Poland's Gomulka) who feared that liberalization would threaten their own regimes.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the **formulation of the Brezhnev Doctrine**, which asserted the right of the Soviet Union to intervene militarily in any socialist state where socialist rule was deemed to be threatened.
- `produced:` the **forcible termination of Czech reforms** and the ouster of Dubcek, replaced by Husak who restored strict censorship and centralized control.
- `enabled:` the **consolidation of Soviet control over Eastern Europe**, demonstrating that Moscow would use force to prevent any deviation from the established Soviet model.
- `produced:` **damage to the moral standing of the USSR** in the West, prompting protests from Western communist parties (Eurocommunism) and dissident movements within the Soviet Union itself.

## Actors
- **Leonid Brezhnev**, who ordered the invasion; and **Alexander Dubcek**, who led the Czechoslovak reform program. See [[brezhnev-leonid]].

## Historiography
Riasanovsky notes that the invasion was a preemptive move to prevent the contagion of liberalization from spreading to the Soviet Union and other satellite states, demonstrating the limits of national sovereignty in the Warsaw Pact (Line 6741).

## From Paxton & Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century*

[[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011|Paxton & Hessler]] corroborate the narrative
above and add specifics: Novotný's fall came January 5, 1968; the April 1968 "Action
Program" explicitly promised "a new model of socialist society, profoundly democratic";
censorship was formally abolished June 25, 1968; the Warsaw Pact invasion of August 21,
1968 involved some **500,000 troops** drawn from the Soviet Union, East Germany, Poland,
Hungary, and Bulgaria — notably **not** Romania, which declined to participate.
Czechoslovak resistance took the form of passive civilian non-cooperation rather than
armed resistance. Dubček's removal came in September 1969, his expulsion from the party
in 1970, with trials of Prague Spring figures continuing through 1972.

Paxton & Hessler stress two consequences beyond the immediate: first, the invasion
hardened the regionwide "normalization"/conservative turn of the 1970s and convinced
dissidents across the bloc that reform could only come from outside, not within, the
ruling Communist parties — a `contributed_to` link to the wider 1970s–80s dissident
movement (see [[dissident-movement-eastern-europe]] if created). Second, and more
specifically, they cite the suppression of the Prague Spring — covered fully in their
later chapter on the 1968–1989 period — as a major cause of the broader Western European
Left's turn against the Soviet Union and toward [[eurocommunism]]:
`contributed_to:` [[eurocommunism]].
