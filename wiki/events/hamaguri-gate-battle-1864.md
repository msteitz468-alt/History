# Battle of the Hamaguri Gate

The **Battle of the Hamaguri Gate** (*Kinmon no hen*, 禁門の変, also called the *Hamaguri Gomon no hen*) took place on August 20, 1864, when Chōshū domain forces attacked the Imperial Palace (Gosho) in Kyōto in an attempt to force the court to recall the nobles expelled in the Coup of the Eighth Month (1863) and compel the [[tokugawa-shogunate]] to carry out [[sonno-joi|jōi]] (expulsion of foreigners). The battle ended in Chōshū's decisive military defeat and left Kyōto devastated, but it marked the apex and turning point of the radical sonnō jōi movement rather than its triumph.

## Background

Following the Coup of the Eighth Month (September 30, 1863), in which the kōbu gattai faction backed by Satsuma and Aizu troops expelled radical sonnō jōi nobles and Chōshū samurai from Kyōto, the Chōshū domain sought rehabilitation. In July 1864, domain rōnin secretly gathered at the Ikeda-ya restaurant to plan their next move. The shogunate's Shinsengumi — an elite swordsman unit — attacked the gathering; "all the rōnin were either killed or captured." The Ikeda-ya incident inflamed Chōshū.

The domain sent a force of more than 1,000 soldiers to Kyōto under Fukuhara Echigo, augmented by sympathetic rōnin, and sent petitions to the court and shogunate requesting pardon. The Court Council offered conditional terms: if Mōri Yoshichika or his son visited the capital and expressed repentance, the court would consider clemency. The Chōshū domain rejected these terms.

## The Battle

Chōshū drew up its own plan: to set fires in Kyōto on a day of strong winds; in the ensuing confusion, kill Matsudaira Katamori (the Kyōto military governor) and Prince Nakagawa; persuade [[emperor-komei]] to accompany the forces to Chōshū; and compel the shogun to carry out jōi. The palace itself was the objective.

By early morning on August 20, rebel soldiers were pressing close to the palace gates. Fukuhara Echigo's troops were repulsed at Fushimi, but other rebels reached the Hamaguri and Nakadachiuri Gates. Keene's chronicle draws on contemporary accounts: "The noise of the fighting was described as resembling 10 million bolts of thunder, and the palace buildings shook as if in an earthquake." The main rebel effort concentrated on the Hamaguri Gate, defended by Aizu domain forces. "A fierce battle ensued, and the rebels had all but broken through the gate when reinforcements from Kuwana and Satsuma arrived and joined the forces to attack and rout the Chōshū forces."

By the time the battle ended, five hours after it began, the rebels were crushed. The fighting ignited fires that spread across the capital: "Some 28,000 buildings in the capital, including temples and shrines, were destroyed by fire during the fighting. The fires were not extinguished for days."

## Inside the Palace

The scene inside the Gosho was one of controlled panic. By imperial command, the twelve-year-old Prince Mutsuhito (the future [[emperor-meiji]]) was moved to the "residential palace" with the empress and Princess Sumiko; boarded palanquins were kept ready for evacuation. [[emperor-komei]] himself "in full court dress, sat impassively, seemingly unruffled by the furious activity around him. Courtiers were dressed for fighting, their sleeves tied back and their feet shod in straw sandals, quite unlike their normal appearance."

That night, a group of intruders appeared in the imperial courtyard — reportedly numbering about 300, allegedly seeking to steal the imperial palanquin. Tokugawa Yoshinobu rushed to the palace. The emperor was moved to the Hall of State Ceremonies; Prince Mutsuhito and the empress accompanied him. Court ladies "were weeping and wailing." The prince, woken in the middle of the night and "suddenly carried off to the Hall of State Ceremonies amid shrieking women," fainted. (Keene corrects later biographers who attributed the fainting to cannon fire: the gun battle at the Hamaguri Gate had ended a day earlier, and what prompted the nocturnal panic was a maidservant accidentally dropping a jar of teeth-blackening liquid, whose breaking sound was mistaken for gunfire.)

## Aftermath

The loyalists executed more than thirty imprisoned sonnō jōi faction men the day after the battle. Keene notes that bodies "were piled up and exposed for three days outside the palace gates, a place that for more than 250 years had not seen warfare."

The First Chōshū Expedition (1864–1865), authorized by the court and executed by a combined shogunate-domain force, followed. Chōshū submitted, court martial proceedings ended with token punishments, and the radical leaders were executed by the domain itself to demonstrate submission. The experience forced Chōshū's pragmatic reformers — including Itō Hirobumi and Yamagata Aritomo — to recognize that frontal military confrontation with the shogunate was unwinnable without better weapons and allies. This led directly to the Satsuma-Chōshū alliance of 1866 and the eventual [[meiji-restoration-1868]].

## Historiography

Keene's narrative draws on Japanese court diaries, domain records, and firsthand accounts of the fighting and the palace evacuation, giving an unusually vivid ground-level record for a single day's fighting. He explicitly corrects an error repeated in earlier biographical accounts (that Prince Mutsuhito fainted from cannon fire), illustrating how secondary retellings of this episode had accumulated inaccuracies before being checked against the primary record. The battle's significance is debated less in its immediate tactical facts than in its longer-run interpretation: whether Chōshū's defeat should be read as the sonnō jōi movement's decisive setback, or as the necessary catalyst that redirected the domain toward the more pragmatic Satsuma-Chōshū alliance that ultimately overthrew the shogunate — Keene favors the latter reading.
