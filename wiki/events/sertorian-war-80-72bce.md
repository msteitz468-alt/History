# The Sertorian War (80–72 BCE)

The long guerrilla war in Spain in which the **Marian fugitive Quintus Sertorius**,
leading the native tribes, held off two Roman armies for most of a decade — the
last embers of [[sullan-civil-war-83-82bce|Sulla's civil war]] and the proving
ground of the young **[[pompey]]**. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]] Chs. 6–7
(Seager).

## Narrative

**Sertorius**, an officer who had escaped the collapse of the anti-Sullan cause in
Italy, was invited back by the **Lusitanians** in 80 and built a formidable
power in the **Ebro valley**, capital at **Osca**, where he educated the sons of
Spanish chieftains in Roman style and convened a **"senate"** of his Roman
followers — a Rome-in-exile, or at least its trappings. Through his lieutenant
**Hirtuleius** he repeatedly held off **Metellus Pius**, governor of Further Spain;
when **Pompey** arrived (76) with a proconsular command, Sertorius bloodied him
badly — the destruction of Lauro (76, Pompey lost 10,000 men and watched the city
burn), and the drawn battles of the **Sucro** and **Segontia** (75), where Pompey
nearly lost his life.

The tide turned from **74**: the deaths of Hirtuleius (Segovia) and the steady
**defection of the Spanish towns** under Pompey's and Metellus' pressure eroded
Sertorius' base, and he turned to harsh reprisals. He concluded a **pact with
[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates]]** (summer 74) — ships and money for military
advisers — which, with the king's eastern war, alarmed Rome into seeing a single
Mediterranean-wide enemy front. In **72** Sertorius was **murdered by his own
officers**, led by the jealous **Perperna**, who was promptly crushed and executed
by Pompey (who burned Sertorius' papers unread "in the interests of concord").

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the survival of the **Marian cause** after the
[[sullan-civil-war-83-82bce|civil war]] (Sertorius and other fugitives carrying on
the struggle in the provinces) **fused with** genuine **native Spanish resistance**
to Roman domination — the backbone of Sertorius' army being tribesmen seeking
liberation, a goal that (Seager argues) Sertorius could never actually deliver.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **pacification of Spain** and its binding into vast
**[[pompey|Pompeian *clientelae*]]** (Pompey's Pyrenean trophy claimed 876 towns
reduced); the relocation of Sertorians and the grant of citizenship to Spaniards
like **Balbus of Gades**.
enabled: the **rise of Pompey** to the consulship of 70; and, through the
**Sertorius–Mithridates pact**, the Roman conviction of a coordinated threat that
shaped the great commands of the 70s–60s (Lucullus, then Pompey, in the East —
Cycle 4).

## Historiography

Plutarch's *Sertorius* (romanticizing) and Sallust's *Histories* (the speeches and
the Spanish letters of Pompey). Seager: the sources are "so tendentious" that
Sertorius' ultimate aims are irrecoverable — Roman renegade, Spanish liberator, or
a man for whom "the war had become an end in itself."
