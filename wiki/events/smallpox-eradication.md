# The Eradication of Smallpox (1959–1980)

## Narrative

The global eradication of **smallpox** (caused by *variola major*, fatal in 30–40%
of typical cases) is the only deliberate extinction of a human disease and, in Erez
Manela's account in [[mcneill-cwh-v7-2015|CWH VII]], a landmark in the rise of
**global health**. Thomas Jefferson had predicted it to Edward Jenner in 1806; it took
171 years. The last naturally occurring case was recorded in Somalia in **1977**, and
the World Health Organization certified eradication in **1980**.

Manela's central argument is that eradication was as much a **political** as a
technical achievement. It required two technologies — **mass-manufactured vaccine**
and, crucially, **freeze-dried vaccine** that could be stored and transported without
refrigeration — but above all it required a new idea: that **disease control is a
global, not a national or local, task**, plus an institution (the WHO) capable of
acting on it.

## From inoculation to vaccination

Techniques to induce immunity by **variolation** (introducing attenuated smallpox
material) were ancient and widespread across Asia and Africa long before Europe
adopted them — via Lady Mary Wortley Montagu (from the Ottoman court, 1717–1721) and,
in Boston, Cotton Mather (who learned of it from **African slaves**, 1707). Jenner's
**vaccination** with cowpox (1796) reversed the direction of flow: a technique
Europeans had once learned from "Asiaticks" became a symbol of Western modernity that
spread outward along the pathways of commerce and empire — carried across oceans, in
the age before refrigeration, through a living **human chain of infection** (often
orphan boys vaccinated in sequence aboard ship).

## The rise of global health

Through the nineteenth century, disease control shifted from a local to a **national**
responsibility (state-building, germ theory, Pasteur and Koch) and then toward a
**global** one. International **sanitary conferences** and quarantine treaties (from
the 1850s) coordinated nations without yet treating disease globally; colonial and
Rockefeller Foundation campaigns and the **League of Nations Health Organization**
built an "epistemic community" of experts. Its successor, the **WHO** (constitution
ratified 1948), was designed to serve "not nations, but humanity itself."

## Eradication as Cold War politics

The WHO's first eradication target, **malaria** (from 1955), was a US-backed, Soviet-
absent, Cold-War-shaped effort that ultimately failed. Smallpox was different. In
1958, reflecting Khrushchev's "**peaceful coexistence**," Soviet deputy health
minister **Viktor Zhdanov** — quoting Jefferson's letter to Jenner — called on the
World Health Assembly to launch a global smallpox campaign; the program was adopted
in 1959 and **intensified from 1967**. It became a rare arena of **superpower
collaboration across the ideological divide**: Soviet vaccine production and US
funding and personnel combined in a worldwide ring-vaccination and surveillance
campaign that drove the disease from its last endemic reservoirs.

## Consequences

- The **first and so far only eradication of a human disease**, certified 1980.
- A **model** for later campaigns (polio, Guinea worm) and a proof of concept for
  internationalist public health and the WHO.
- A demonstration that global cooperation was possible even at the height of the
  [[cold-war|Cold War]] — Manela's larger point about the period's connective as well
  as destructive character.

## Historiography

Manela's revisionism: against heroic technological narratives (Jenner → triumph), he
foregrounds the **politics** — the slow construction of the idea of global health,
the role of decolonization and the Cold War, and the deep non-European roots of
inoculation that the Jenner story obscured. The "Lady Mary" origin myth is itself
contested (variolation was already known among European peasants and reached the
Atlantic world through multiple channels, including enslaved Africans).
