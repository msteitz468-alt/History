# 1905 Miracle Year

**March–June 1905, Bern.** Albert Einstein, a 26-year-old third-class technical expert at the Swiss Patent Office, produced in his spare time four landmark papers (plus a fifth addendum) that fundamentally altered physics. The papers were submitted to *Annalen der Physik* and announced in a famous May 1905 letter to Conrad Habicht: “I promise you four papers in return.”

## Narrative

While employed full-time examining patents in Bern (near the famous clock tower), Einstein worked nights and weekends on theoretical problems. His circle of friends in the Olympia Academy (Maurice Solovine, Conrad Habicht, and frequent sounding-board Michele Besso) provided discussion and intellectual stimulus.

The four papers (as described by Einstein to Habicht):

- **First paper**: “On a Heuristic Point of View Concerning the Production and Transformation of Light” (submitted March 17, 1905). Argued that light consists of discrete energy quanta (later called photons). “According to the assumption to be considered here, when a light ray is propagated from a point, the energy is not continuously distributed over an increasing space but consists of a finite number of energy quanta which are localized at points in space...”

- **Second paper**: Doctoral dissertation, “A New Determination of Molecular Dimensions” (April 30, 1905; submitted to University of Zurich). Used viscosity and diffusion data from sugar solutions in water to calculate molecular sizes and Avogadro’s number (initial result ~2.1–4.15 × 10²³; later corrected versions closer to modern 6.02 × 10²³).

- **Third paper**: “On the Movement of Small Particles Suspended in Stationary Liquids Required by the Molecular-Kinetic Theory of Heat” (May 1905). Explained Brownian motion as the visible result of statistical collisions by invisible molecules. Predicted observable random walks; “bodies of a microscopically visible size suspended in liquids must, as a result of thermal molecular motions, perform motions of such magnitudes that they can be easily observed with a microscope.”

- **Fourth paper**: “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies” (received June 30, 1905). The special theory of relativity. Discarded absolute space and time; showed that simultaneity is relative; derived E=mc² in a short follow-up addendum. “The introduction of a ‘light ether’ will prove to be superfluous...”

A fifth paper followed in September on mass-energy equivalence.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by** or **contributed_to** the patent office isolation (no teaching or academic pressures) enabled focused theoretical work; prior decade of thought experiments (light-beam rider since age 16, magnet-coil asymmetry); deep reading in Mach, Poincaré, Hume, Boltzmann, Föppl; discussions with Besso and Olympia Academy; Planck’s 1900 blackbody work and Lenard’s photoelectric observations.

The patent-office job (secured via Marcel Grossmann) provided financial stability without requiring rote publication, while the clock-synchronization and electromechanical patent applications supplied concrete examples of signal propagation at light speed.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced**: foundations of quantum theory (photoelectric law), proof of atomic reality, special relativity (relativity of space, time, simultaneity; invariance of c), and E=mc². Directly enabled 20th-century physics revolutions, nuclear energy, lasers, GPS corrections, semiconductors, and the atomic age. Einstein’s methods (deductive postulates + thought experiments) became models for theoretical work.

**preceded_by**: failure to secure academic posts after Zurich Polytechnic (1900); marriage and birth of Lieserl (1902); move to Bern (1902).

**followed_by**: slow initial recognition; Planck’s engagement; eventual academic appointments (Zurich 1909 onward); 1915 general relativity.

## Historiography

Isaacson emphasizes the cluster’s astonishing range and the patent-office context as creative enabler rather than drudgery. The papers are deductive in presentation (postulates first, data later) even when grounded in prior experiments. Einstein downplayed the role of Michelson-Morley data, stressing instead his early light-beam thought experiment and desire for a universal formal principle. Later historians (Norton, Miller, Holton) have traced the precise conceptual path; the light-quanta paper was the most radical and initially least accepted (even Planck resisted the physical reality of photons). The thesis on molecular dimensions became one of Einstein’s most-cited practical works (cement, aerosols, dairy).

**Grounding quotes (Isaacson range 1)**: “The first deals with radiation and the energy properties of light and is very revolutionary...” (letter to Habicht). “If a person could run after a light wave with the same speed as light, you would have a wave arrangement which could be completely independent of time” (recalled light-beam experiment). “I am frightfully busy. Every day I spend eight hours at the office... and then, in addition, I do some scientific work.”
