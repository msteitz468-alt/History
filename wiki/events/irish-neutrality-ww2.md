## Narrative

Ireland's neutrality in the [[second-world-war-1939]] — officially called 'the Emergency' — was the defining policy of [[eamon-de-valera]]'s government from September 1939 to May 1945. It represented, as one contemporary observer noted, Ireland's 'first free self-assertion' as a sovereign state, even at the cost of international isolation and domestic economic privation.

**Prerequisites.** Irish neutrality became a practical proposition only in April 1938, when Britain agreed to return the 'Treaty Ports' (Cobh, Berehaven, Lough Swilly) retained under the 1921 Anglo-Irish Treaty. Without these ports, Britain had no legal foothold in Ireland and could not coerce Irish participation without military invasion. British strategic planners had concluded the ports were 'run-down and unsuitable for modern warfare' and that they could not be operated without Irish assistance. However, the historian Eunan O'Halpin notes that 'in reality, however obsolete the installations themselves, in wartime they would have provided a foot in the Irish door' (Jackson, *Oxford Handbook*, ch. 35).

**The policy.** De Valera made Ireland's position crystal-clear to the Dáil before war began: Ireland would stay out of any European conflict unless herself attacked. When war came, this command found broad cross-party support. The government maintained relations with the Axis powers on the same basis as the Allies (diplomatic courtesy). The most notorious expression of the policy came on Hitler's death in April 1945, when de Valera visited the German legation in Dublin to express condolences — a gesture that provoked enormous international criticism.

**Covert cooperation with Britain.** Neutral in public, Ireland was substantially pro-Allied in practice. Irish army intelligence (G2) worked closely with MI5. The Garda Special Branch cooperated with the RUC Special Branch. Allied airmen who landed in Ireland were allowed to cross quietly into Northern Ireland; German airmen were interned for the duration. Joseph Carroll's 1975 study *Ireland in the War Years* first disclosed the 'extensive range of covert Anglo-Irish cooperation in wartime defence and security matters' (ch. 35). Britain's own security service MI5 concluded that 'it is at least arguable that Eire neutral was of more value to the British war effort than Eire belligerent would have been', since a belligerent Ireland would have kept people at home for defence, required armaments from an already-stretched Britain, and likely generated IRA guerrilla warfare as well (ch. 35).

**Suppression of the IRA.** The wartime period saw the most effective suppression of the IRA in Irish history. The IRA's 'S-plan' bombing campaign in Britain (from January 1939) and its contacts with German intelligence (the 'Abwehr link' discovered by police in June 1940) provided legal and political cover for internment. About 500 republicans were interned in June 1940. Eight IRA men were executed for political crimes. By the end of the war, the IRA had been 'crippled' and its 'pretensions to being an army-in-waiting, poised to overthrow de Valera in combination with their German allies, were shown to be a pathetic delusion' (ch. 35). This success contributed directly to the stability of Irish democracy in the post-war period.

**Economy and society.** Neutrality imposed severe economic costs. The 'Emergency' saw chronic shortages of fuel, food distribution problems, and near-paralysis of the railway system. Domestically produced turf replaced coal; livestock transport became difficult; pharmaceutical supplies ran short. Despite apparent food abundance in shops (shocking to British visitors accustomed to rationing), many urban Irish could not afford basic nutrition: 60 per cent of Dublin mothers were unable to breastfeed due to their own malnourishment, according to a 1941 departmental survey. Tuberculosis spread, with food safety regulations suspended. Between 1939 and 1945, approximately one-sixth of the adult working-age population went to work in the UK (as civilians or military service), despite the state's formal neutrality — 'private collusion . . . suited both sides' (Brian Girvin, cited in ch. 35).

**Post-war consequences.** Irish neutrality damaged Ireland's standing with the United States more acutely than with Britain. The 'rancorous course of wartime American-Irish diplomacy left Dublin more fearful and mistrustful of the United States than of Britain', producing an 'extraordinarily hesitant and half-hearted Irish response' to Marshall Plan opportunities (ch. 35). Ireland was excluded from NATO (1949) because it refused to join unless partition ended — a position that Britain condemned and the United States disregarded. Neutrality thus shaped Irish foreign policy for decades: Ireland remained outside NATO, pursued an independent foreign policy emphasizing small-nation rights, and did not join the UN until 1955.

## Scholarly Debate

Brian Girvin's *The Emergency* (2005) advances the most critical assessment: Ireland could have joined the Allies after 1943 with relatively little cost, and a united Ireland might have been achieved. He argues de Valera chose 'scepticism and indifference'; 'every state has the right to be neutral, but has it the right to be indifferent to outcome?' However, this is disputed. Elizabeth Bowen, reporting for the British government in November 1940, observed that Irish neutrality was 'Eire's first free self-assertion: as such alone it would mean a great deal to her.' Clair Wills's *That Neutral Island* (2007) explores the psychological importance of neutrality for the Irish self-image. T. Ryle Dwyer argues the 'phoney neutrality' deserved more gracious Allied acknowledgement because of covert security cooperation.

O'Halpin emphasizes de Valera's calculations: Britain would prevent a German invasion; Britain could be appeased through covert security cooperation; Britain would not risk alienating American opinion by attacking Ireland. These calculations proved correct. The true post-war cost of neutrality was not punishment but neglect: 'she was ignored' (ch. 35).

**Holocaust.** The greatest moral issue of the war — the fate of European Jewry — was 'never adduced as a reason to fight Hitler' (ch. 35). The Irish government's immigration policy towards Jewish refugees before and during the war was 'at best, uncaring and legalistic' (Taoiseach John Bruton, 1995). Anti-Semitic prejudice was more freely expressed by Fine Gael politicians than by Fianna Fáil in the 1930s and 1940s.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` return of treaty ports 1938 — made neutrality physically possible
- `caused_by:` de Valera's sovereignty doctrine — neutrality was the 'ultimate proof of the sovereignty for which Irish nationalists had fought between 1916 and 1921' (ch. 35)
- `caused_by:` partition — de Valera refused to trade neutrality for British promises of Irish unity, rightly calculating Churchill could not deliver
- `contributed_to:` widespread cross-party support — all major Irish political parties accepted neutrality, and British/American 'hectoring' hardened public Irish opinion in its favour

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` post-war US animus — more damaging than British resentment; Marshall Plan opportunities squandered
- `produced:` NATO exclusion — the Republic remained outside the Western defence alliance
- `produced:` IRA defeat — wartime suppression of the IRA was the most effective in the state's history
- `produced:` deepened North-South divergence — Northern Ireland's wartime contribution and the Free State's neutrality widened the political and social gap between the two polities; the 1949 Ireland Act guaranteed Northern Ireland's position in the UK in part as recognition of its war record
