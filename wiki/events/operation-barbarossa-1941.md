# Operation Barbarossa (June–December 1941)

German invasion of the Soviet Union, the largest military operation in history.

## Narrative

See detailed tactical analysis at [[hubs/warfare/world-wars-era/second-world-war/operation-barbarossa-1941]].

Three army groups advanced; initial successes but stalled at Leningrad, Moscow, with Soviet winter counteroffensives.

## Causal Analysis

**Decision mechanics (Kershaw *Nemesis* Range 03, grounded).** July 1940 Berghof: strategic pivot after Sealion deemed impossible. Halder: 'With Russia smashed, England’s last hope would be shattered... Decision: Russia’s destruction must therefore be made a part of this struggle. Spring 1941.' Primarily to compel British terms by removing last continental prop (USSR as 'the factor on which England is relying the most'); ideological secondary but ever-present. 'Working towards': Halder anticipates July 1940 by ordering study; Loßberg on own initiative drafts plans before formal order. Underestimation shared ('child’s play'). 

Directive 21 (18 Dec 1940): 'crush Soviet Russia in a rapid campaign' (code-name Barbarossa chosen by Hitler as crusade allusion). Molotov visit (Nov 1940) confirms clash. March 1941: explicit ideological framing — 'showdown of two different ideologies... The Jewish-Bolshevik intelligentsia... must be eliminated.' 30 March speech to generals: 'This is a war of annihilation... extermination of the Bolshevist commissars... Commissars... are criminals.'

**Complicity and orders.** Army translates without objection (Barbarossa Decree May 1941: suspends law/collective reprisals; Commissar Order June 1941: shoot commissars at once). Generals like Hoepner echo 'repulse of Jewish Bolshevism... with unprecedented severity.' Einsatzgruppen (Heydrich May 1941): 'Jewry was the source of Bolshevism... had to be eradicated... Jews... executed forthwith.' 'By 22 June the genocidal whirlwind was ready.'

Hitler's psychology: time obsession ('Time... was not on Germany’s side'), all-or-nothing ('I shall never survive the defeat of my people'), post-decision relief ('I again feel spiritually free'). 'Hitler had authorized more than initiated.' Broad mandate + 'working towards' produced mechanics.

**Launch (22 June 1941).** Proclamation frames as preventive crusade against 'Jewish-Anglo-Saxon warmongers and the equally Jewish rulers of the Bolshevik headquarters.' Early advances astonishing but 'Soviet calamity was immense – and avoidable' (Stalin ignored intel, exposed armies).

## Consequence Analysis

Opened two-front war Hitler sought to avoid; massive Soviet losses enabled initial gains but exposed overextension. Merged war with full Holocaust (Einsatzgruppen killings from day one; path to death camps by end 1941). 'By the end of 1941... the death camps were commencing operations.' 'The war in the East... was indeed Hitler’s war. But... acceded to, even welcomed... by all sections of the German élite' and large parts of population as 'crusade against Bolshevism.' 'The path to the Holocaust, intertwined with the showdown with Bolshevism, was prefigured.' Turned European war into world war of annihilation; set conditions for Allied victory and Soviet survival as superpower.

## Historiography (Kershaw emphasis)

Kershaw stresses structural 'working towards the Führer' + Hitler's broad licence over sole intentionalism. Army complicity ideological (anti-Bolshevism overlap + Poland precedent) not reluctant imposition. Decision primarily strategic in July 1940 (force Britain), with ideological 'Jewish-Bolshevism' crusade intensifying March 1941. Shared underestimation of 'Russian colossus' not just intel failure but racial contempt. 'The war in the East was indeed Hitler’s war. But it was more than that... acceded to... by all sections of the German élite.' Source: Kershaw *Nemesis* (primary Goebbels diaries, Halder notes, directives, Heydrich addresses). ## Stahel's logistics thesis (via Evans 2015)

From Evans's review of David Stahel, *Kiev 1941* (2012), in
[[evans-third-reich-history-memory-2015]] (ch. 21), endorsed as persuasive
though "not entirely original": the invasion (22 June, 3:15 a.m. — 3
million German + 500,000 allied troops, 3,600 tanks, 600,000 motor
vehicles, 2,700 aircraft) was strategically dead **by early August 1941**:
losses over 200,000 by end-July, supply lines stretched over poor roads
with 600,000+ horses standing in for absent rail capacity, a forced halt
ordered 30 July. Blitzkrieg worked against coast-pinned Western states,
not "in the limitless expanse of the Eastern European steppe," and German
production could never match combined Soviet-British-American output — by
February 1942 Fritz Todt was telling Hitler the war was lost on exactly
these grounds. The Nazi image of the USSR as "dull peasants oppressed by
a small clique of Jewish Communists" was a fantasy that dissolved against
Soviet mobilization (5 million reservists within days of Stalin's 3 July
broadcast). Goebbels privately conceded in mid-September 1941: "The
Blitzkrieg had become a war of resources... we are now fighting with our
backs against the wall." The Kiev diversion (21 August, against the
generals' Moscow-first advice) delivered the war's greatest encirclement
at ruinous cost — see [[battle-of-kiev-1941]] — before the worst autumn
rains since 1874 and an unequipped winter halted the Moscow drive. On the
deliberate starvation architecture accompanying the campaign, see
[[hunger-plan-1941]].

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Directive No. 21 ("Operation Barbarossa," 18 Dec 1940, only nine copies) is quoted at length: "The German Armed Forces must be prepared to crush Soviet Russia in a quick campaign before the end of the war against England," with the objective line running "from the Volga River to Archangel." [SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer disputes postwar generals' claims (especially Halder's) that the Army opposed Barbarossa as an "adventure" from the outset, citing Halder's own Dec 1940–Feb 1941 diary as showing "genuine enthusiasm" — see [[halder-franz]]. The 27 March 1941 decision to crush Yugoslavia after the Belgrade coup forced a roughly four-week postponement (new date 22 June vs. the original 15 May); Shirer calls this "probably the most catastrophic single decision in Hitler's career" (Shirer 1960), citing Paulus's Nuremberg testimony ("about five weeks") and Rundstedt's postwar statement that the delay "was a very costly delay" — see [[balkan-campaign-1941]].

The Commissar Order (early March 1941, Hitler to assembled service chiefs) is quoted verbatim: "This struggle is one of ideologies and racial differences and will have to be conducted with unprecedented, unmerciful and unrelenting harshness... the commissars will be liquidated." Manstein testified at Nuremberg that he personally refused to carry it out, but Shirer notes "the order, of course, was carried out on a large scale." A parallel Keitel directive of 13 May 1941 suspended court-martial jurisdiction over German troops' crimes against Soviet civilians and gave Himmler "special tasks" to act "independently" of the Army — the Barbarossa-era legal architecture of terror. On the starvation directive from the same weeks, see [[hunger-plan-1941]].

U.S. commercial attaché Sam E. Woods relayed an accurate, detailed warning of the Barbarossa plan to the State Department in January 1941; Sumner Welles passed it to Soviet Ambassador Oumansky on 20 March 1941, who "turned very white" but the USSR apparently never acted on it. Stalin publicly embraced Schulenburg at a Moscow railway station on 13 April 1941 ("We must remain friends... through thick and thin!") even as invasion rumors mounted; the 14 June 1941 TASS statement branded such rumors "an obvious absurdity" the same day Ribbentrop secretly cabled Budapest to secure its frontiers. On the first day of the invasion, Halder's diary recorded the Red Army "tactically surprised along the entire front"; by 3 July he prematurely judged the "Feldzug against Russia... won in fourteen days." German soldiers first encountered the T-34 that summer and found it "so heavily armored that the shells from the German antitank guns bounced harmlessly off it" (Guderian/Blumentritt reports) — the onset of what Blumentritt called "tank terror."

Source: [[shirer-rise-fall-third-reich-1960]]

## From Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

**Rationale and planning.** Lebensraum ideology from the early 1920s, restated to army chiefs on 3 February 1933; formal planning from July 1940 ("child's play" compared to France — Hitler; 5 December 1940: "The Russians are inferior . . . The army is leaderless"). Pragmatic drivers included Germany's dependence on Soviet supplies (three-quarters of phosphate imports, over half of manganese, over a third of oil) and the strategic syllogism that smashing the USSR would knock away Britain's last continental hope, forestall US entry, and unleash Japan. Stalin — reading Hitler as a tool of German capital who could be bought with trade — vetoed pre-emptive measures, dismissed accurate warnings (Sorge; the deserter who swam a river on 21 June to warn of the attack was shot for "disinformation"), and had no plans for attack or defense.

**A war of annihilation by design.** Hitler, 30 March 1941, to 200+ officers (Halder's notes): "This is a war of annihilation." The 19 May guidelines demanded "ruthless and energetic action against . . . Jews"; Keitel's Commissar Order (6 June 1941) mandated shooting political officers on capture — almost no generals objected, and Brauchitsch, Hoepner ("the defence against Jewish Bolshevism . . . must be carried out with unprecedented harshness"), Manstein, and Reichenau issued their own ideological orders. The **Hunger Plan** (Backe/Thomas, State Secretaries' agreement of 2 May 1941: "umpteen million people will starve if what is necessary for us is taken out of the country") and the **General Plan for the East** (Meyer, for Himmler: 31–45 million Slavs displaced or left to perish, 80–85% of Poles removed) framed the campaign; Himmler at Wewelsburg (January 1941) spoke of reducing the Slav population by 30 million, a figure Göring repeated to Ciano. The [[einsatzgruppen]] (600–1,000 men each, plus 23 Order Police battalions of screened, ideologically trained volunteers — "neither 'ordinary men' nor 'ordinary Germans'," Evans writes against the Browning framing) followed the armies with a 2 July 1941 killing brief drafted broad enough to license the murder of male Jews wholesale.

**Campaign.** 3 million German and 500,000 allied troops (Finland, Romania, Hungary, Slovakia, Italy's token 60,000, Spain's Blue Division; Boris III of Bulgaria refused: "My army is pro-German, my wife is Italian, my people are pro-Russian. I'm the only pro-Bulgarian in this country"), 3,600 tanks, 2,700 aircraft on a 1,000-mile front from 3:15 a.m., 22 June 1941. 1,200+ Soviet aircraft destroyed on day one; 600,000 prisoners taken by Army Group Centre by mid-July. But Halder's diary already on 11 August: "we have underestimated the Russian colossus . . . when a dozen of them have been destroyed, then the Russians put up another dozen." Logistics (40,000 miles of metalled road in the whole USSR, incompatible rail gauge, 625,000 horses) and 400,000 German casualties by the Kiev operation ate the offensive. Hitler's August diversion south delivered the Kiev encirclement (665,000 more prisoners) at the cost of the Moscow timetable — a decision Evans declines to score with hindsight, noting the generals' own Prussian knockout-blow dogma and equal underestimation of Soviet reserves. Operation Typhoon (October) took 673,000 more prisoners before the rasputitsa, then −34° to −40°C cold against troops in summer uniforms; Sorge's confirmation that Japan would not attack freed 400,000 Siberian troops; Zhukov's 5 December counter-offensive drove the Wehrmacht into a retreat Heinrici called "absolutely Napoleonic in its manner. The losses are similar" (`analogous_to: [[french-invasion-of-russia-1812]]`). Hitler's stand-fast order stabilized the front; the December command purge (Rundstedt, Bock, Brauchitsch — Hitler taking personal command of the army — then Guderian, Hoepner, Leeb) established "complete dominance over the top army commanders." Evans debunks the surviving generals' postwar "amateur Hitler stabbed us in the back" legend: the crisis was equally their own doctrinal rigidity and failure to prepare winter positions. German dead/missing in 1941: 357,000 (vs. 83,000 in all 1940 campaigns).

**Soviet POWs — the first mass crime of the eastern war.** Commissars shot or handed to the SS; columns of prisoners "like skeletons, just shadows of human beings . . . fighting for scraps of apples in the gutter" (Klukowski, October 1941); 1,000 of 5,000 frozen dead on a single open train; camps that were bare fenced fields, guards betting on dog attacks, cannibalism among the starving — 300,000 dead by the end of 1941. Of ~5.7 million Soviet POWs over the war, **3.3 million (58%) died in German captivity**, against ~2% of Western POWs; of ~2 million German POWs in Soviet hands, 18% died — a differential Evans roots squarely in the racial doctrine "shared by the overwhelming majority of the German officer corps." The improvement from late October 1941 was pragmatic (labor shortage), not moral. Hosenfeld: "so repulsive, inhumane and so naively stupid that one can only be deeply ashamed." Repatriated Soviet survivors then faced Stalin's treason order and the Gulag, unrehabilitated until 1994.

Also documented: the Hess flight (10 May 1941 — the Deputy Führer's solo peace mission to Scotland, disowned as madness; Bormann inherited his apparatus), looting and cultural vandalism (Tolstoy's manuscripts burned as stove fuel), rape and reprisals feeding the partisan war, Soviet-side atrocities (NKVD prison massacres ~100,000; deportation of 1.2 million Volga Germans, up to 175,000 dead), and the eastward evacuation of 1,360 Soviet factories on 1.5 million railway wagons. Evans's closing line for the volume's Part 2: "it was in the end on the Eastern Front, more than any other, that the fortunes of war were decided." Source: [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008]]

## From Paxton and Hessler, *Europe in the Twentieth Century* (2011)

**"His fatal mistake."** Hitler's order of 18 December 1940 to prepare the invasion is characterized starkly: "This deliberate choice of a two-front war was his fatal mistake." A Balkan prelude preceded the main invasion — Mussolini's disastrous Greek campaign of October 1940, the Yugoslav coup, the German invasion of Yugoslavia on 6 April 1941, the fall of Crete on 20 May, and the Iraq/Syria sideshow. Paxton and Hessler float a counterfactual: had Germany continued its Mediterranean advance through the summer of 1941 rather than turning east, it "might well have cut the British Empire in two at Suez."

**Launch and early course.** 175 German divisions crossed into the USSR on 22 June 1941. Stalin had scrupulously honored Pact-mandated deliveries to Germany right up to the invasion, ignored repeated warnings, and then fell silent for two weeks, remaining in Moscow (Zhukov later claimed Stalin privately expected defeat). German forces met genuine welcomes in parts of Ukraine — a legacy of collectivization's rebellions a decade earlier and the officer purges four years before — and by November 1941 had advanced deeper than Napoleon's invasion. The recapture of Rostov-on-Don at the end of November was the first conspicuous German ground reversal of the war, followed by Zhukov's Moscow counteroffensive; Hitler responded by sacking a field marshal and 35 corps or divisional commanders who had refused to hold their ground. Paxton and Hessler's summary judgment: "Soviet survival during that first winter was the eastern front's equivalent of the Battle of Britain."

**Winter War background.** Soviet casualties in the 1939–40 Winter War against Finland exceeded the entire strength of the Finnish army and confirmed a Western belief that Stalin's purged Red Army was militarily worthless — a misjudgment the resilience of 1941–42 would overturn. The USSR had also seized the Baltic states in June/August 1940 and Bessarabia and Bukovina from Romania on 26 June 1940. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].
