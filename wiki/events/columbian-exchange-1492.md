# The Columbian Exchange (from 1492)

A **standing transition page** (per the wiki's transition register: "1492 and the
Columbian Exchange"). The Columbian Exchange is the permanent biological and
cultural linking of the **Old World** (Eurasia, Africa) and the **New World**
(the Americas) that followed Columbus's 1492 crossing — the moment when the two
long-isolated halves of the human world were joined and the
[[afro-eurasian-world-system|Afro-Eurasian world system]] became, for the first
time, genuinely planetary. The term was coined and popularized by **Alfred W.
Crosby** (*The Columbian Exchange*, 1972), who made him a founder of modern
ecological and global history; his work was at first ignored or coolly received
by a discipline still dominated by political and institutional history, and only
later recognized as seminal.

## Narrative

Columbus's first voyage (1492–3) was reconnaissance; its biological impact was
limited (possibly syphilis, carried eastward). The decisive turn came with the
**second voyage** (1493), which carried some 1,500 colonists and a documented
cargo of horses, cattle, pigs, goats, sheep, wheat, and fruit trees — the
vanguard of "a literal tsunami of Old World animals" and plants. Within two
years roughly two-thirds of these first colonists were dead, and the Taíno of
Hispaniola, brutally exploited for gold and stripped of their *conucos*
(cultivated plots), faced starvation. The first well-documented **smallpox**
epidemic struck the Caribbean in December 1518 and swept onto the mainland,
contributing directly to the Spanish conquests of the [[aztec-empire|Aztec]] and
[[inca-empire|Inca]] empires.

The exchange ran in both directions but catastrophically unevenly:

- **Disease (Old World → New World).** Smallpox, measles, typhus, influenza,
  plague, malaria, and yellow fever devastated populations with no acquired
  immunity. Measles could kill 30 percent and smallpox 50 percent or more of the
  unexposed. Repeated, compounding epidemics (1545–8, 1576–81 in Mesoamerica;
  1585–91 in the Andes) drove the **American demographic collapse**; populations
  in many regions did not begin to recover until the later seventeenth century.
- **Crops.** New World maize, potatoes, manioc, beans, squash, tomatoes,
  peanuts, cacao, and tobacco spread across the Old World (maize and cassava
  taking hold quickly in Africa; the potato slowly and suspiciously in Europe);
  Old World wheat, rice, sugar, olives, and grapes were planted in temperate and
  tropical America. **Sugar cane** — flagged by Columbus himself on the second
  voyage — transformed Hispaniola and then Brazil, driving deforestation and the
  demand for enslaved labor.
- **Animals.** Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats reshaped American
  ecologies and economies (pigs ravaging Taíno *conucos*; cattle and sheep
  replacing decimated human populations). Only the turkey passed the other way as
  a significant food animal.
- **People.** European settlement and the forced migration of enslaved Africans
  (see [[atlantic-slave-trade]] and [[early-modern-migrations]]) — the human
  dimension Crosby treated least but which was transformational.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` [[late-middle-ages|Iberian oceanic expansion]] — the Reconquista's
crusading, mobile society and Atlantic seafaring experience, plus the search for
a westward route to Asia. `enabled:` the prior **biological isolation** of the
Americas, which left their peoples immunologically defenseless and their
ecologies open to Old World species. `concurrent_with:` the silver economy and
the rise of oceanic empires.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **American demographic collapse** (the central fact of the
encounter; see the disputed numbers at
[[pre-columbian-american-population]]); the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic slave
trade]] (as American Indigenous labor collapsed, especially for sugar); the
worldwide redistribution of staple crops that underwrote later **global
population growth** (maize and the potato above all); and the integration of a
single, planetary [[afro-eurasian-world-system|world system]] — the realization
of what the wiki calls [[proto-globalization]] and the substance of the
[[early-modern|early modern "construction of a global world"]].

## Historiography

Crosby's environmental-biological framing — extended in *Ecological Imperialism*
(1986) — reoriented world history toward ecology, disease, and demography, and
resonated with the Annales School's attention to climate and *longue durée*. The
quantitative study of the catastrophe remains contested: see
[[pre-columbian-american-population]]. Open questions include the origin of
syphilis (Old World, New World, or shared treponemal pathogens), the exact
timing of smallpox's arrival, and how far disease (versus violence and forced
labor) drove the collapse. The Exchange also anchors the broader debate over the
ethics and language of "discovery" versus conquest and imposition (cf.
[[early-modern-migrations]], [[intercultural-relations-early-modern]]).

The disease side of the Exchange — the **[[virgin-soil-epidemics|Great Dying]]** — now has its own page:
the [[virgin-soil-epidemics|virgin-soil epidemics]] that, in Charles Mann's [[mann-1491-2005|*1491*]],
often ran *ahead* of the Europeans and turned a populous, managed hemisphere into the
"[[pristine-myth|wilderness]]" the settlers mistook for its natural state.

## The livestock-and-ecology side (Burkholder & Johnson)

Burkholder and Johnson's [[burkholder-johnson-colonial-latin-america|colonial
textbook]] details the **biological transfer's reshaping of the American
landscape**, often as dramatic as the disease catastrophe. European **livestock**
— horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, chickens reaching Hispaniola in 1493 —
exploded across a hemisphere with few predators and (after the die-off) emptied
land: feral cattle and horses exceeded 50 million on the southern plains by 1700.
The classic case is the **Valley of the Mezquital** north of Mexico City, where
sheep grew from 34,000 (1539) to 4.4 million (1589), displaced the depopulated
native farmers, and turned irrigated cropland into eroded, mesquite-covered desert
within a single century — a feedback loop of depopulation and overgrazing. Old
World **weeds** (the giant thistle "as tall as a mounted horseman" on the pampas)
rode in on disturbed soil; **mining** at [[potosi-and-colonial-silver|Potosí]]
scarred the land and leached mercury; and the draining of the lakes of the Valley
of Mexico (after the 1607 and 1629 floods) destroyed the Mexica's lacustrine
economy. Paradoxically, because the **demographic collapse** withdrew so much human
pressure — abandoned terraces, raised fields, and dams — Latin America's **forests
in 1800 were more extensive than in 1492**, reinforcing the
[[pristine-myth|"pristine myth"]] argument. The transfer also remade indigenous
cultures: the **horse** revolutionized the Apache, Comanche, Mapuche, and Pampas
peoples; Andeans and Mexicans became muleteers, cowboys, and shepherds.
