# Kerch Campaign

## Narrative

The Kerch campaign of spring 1942 (centered on Operation Trappenjagd, 8–16 May) represented one of the Wehrmacht's last classic victories in the style of Bewegungskrieg and Kesselschlacht. It followed Soviet amphibious landings on the Kerch peninsula in late December 1941 and early January 1942.

On 26 December, elements of Soviet 51st Army (General V. N. Lvov) landed at numerous sites on the Kerch peninsula. Two days later a larger landing at Feodosiya deposited the vanguard of 44th Army (General A. N. Pervushin). By 29 December some 41,000 men, 236 guns, and 43 tanks had come ashore. In January the 47th Army under General K. S. Kolganov also arrived. On 28 January Stalin constituted the three armies into the Crimean Front under General D. T. Kozlov.

The landings were confused improvisations conducted in Force 8 and Force 5 gales with temperatures of −20°C. No dedicated landing craft or sophisticated C2 techniques existed; soldiers waded through icy water. Of ten landing sites, local German garrison troops smashed all but four. The Red Army nevertheless reconquered the Kerch peninsula, throwing the German XXXXII Corps (one division, the 46th) into panic. Its commander, General Hans von Sponeck, repeatedly requested permission to evacuate; Manstein refused three times. On his own initiative Sponeck ordered a retreat to the Parpach neck. The 46th Division conducted a brutal forced march in subzero weather and howling winds, suffering thousands of frostbite casualties and abandoning heavy equipment. Halder recorded 29 December as "a very difficult day!" Sponeck was dismissed, tried for disobeying orders, and imprisoned.

The Germans retook Feodosiya on 18 January with a counterattack by XXX Corps (132nd and 170th Infantry Divisions), establishing a shorter defensive line on the Parpach bottleneck. The situation became a stalemate: Germans held the body of the peninsula and access bottlenecks; Soviets held Sevastopol and Kerch.

Both sides prepared offensives, but the Soviets struck first. With reinforcements including T-34 brigades arriving over the frozen strait, Kozlov launched repeated assaults on 27 February, 13 March, 26 March, and 9 April, all failing to break the Parpach line. The front suffered over 40 percent casualties. Soviet troops in open mud suffered systematic artillery strikes without trenches or foxholes, as described by correspondent Konstantin Simonov. German defenders (XXXXII and XXX Corps plus Romanians) held with customary professionalism; Romanians performed credibly despite lacking heavy weapons. Luftwaffe airpower, especially Fliegerführer Süd under Colonel Wolfgang von Wild (Ju-87s, Ju-88s, He-111s, Bf-109s), turned Soviet rear areas into an inferno due to high density of attacking forces. A 22nd Panzer Division counterattack in March was mauled (losing over 30 tanks) and derided as the "Eau de Cologne Division" for evaporating quickly; Manstein sent it to the rear for training.

Manstein now took the offensive. Operation Trappenjagd ("Bustard Hunt," a reference to Crimean game birds; Americans might have called it "Turkey Shoot") opened on 8 May. The position was formidable: a great antitank ditch (1941 vintage, fortified with concrete and disabled tanks), followed five miles back by the Nasyr Line and then the Sultanovka Line (Turkish Wall) at the peninsula's widest point. Kerch lay forty miles east. Soviets outnumbered Germans at least two-to-one in manpower and matériel and had echeloned defenses in depth.

Two German advantages proved decisive. Hitler assigned all of Luftflotte 4 plus Fliegerkorps VIII under General Wolfram von Richthofen (the Luftwaffe's ground-support expert, veteran of 1940 Channel dash and Barbarossa opening) to the Crimea—startling mass for the small peninsula. Second, Kozlov's failed offensives left a northern bulge; aerial and ground reconnaissance confirmed the mass of Soviet forces there, leaving the southern portion of the bottleneck relatively weaker.

Manstein's plan: XXXXII Corps (now under General Franz Mattenklott: 46th Division plus three Romanian) held the northern sector with deception (fake artillery, troop movements, false radio) to fix Soviet attention. The Schwerpunkt fell in the south with oversized XXX Corps under General Maximilian Fretter-Pico (28th Light, 50th, 132nd, 170th Infantry Divisions, and the retrained 22nd Panzer Division). Infantry would cross the ditch with heavy air and artillery support, opening a path for Panzers. The 22nd Panzer would then wheel sharply left (north) to the coast, encircling 51st and 47th Armies in the northern bulge. Infantry would accompany the Panzers every step due to short distances. A small Grodeck Brigade (22nd Reconnaissance Battalion, truck-mounted Brandenburg Regiment company, 560th Antitank Company, plus two Romanian motorized cavalry regiments under Colonel Radu Korne) would peel off after the wheel and drive east at maximum speed to break up reinforcements, shoot up rear installations, and slash toward Kerch—despite its regimental size facing army-sized forces.

A crafty opening: early 9 May, V Battalion of 346th Infantry Regiment landed from assault boats behind the main Soviet antitank ditch. At 0315 an immense artillery barrage opened; ten minutes later ground attack began with waves of aircraft. The 132nd Division (coast) reached the ditch as the battalion landed inside it. The 132nd, 28th Light, and 50th crossed rapidly. After further preparation the 22nd Panzer and Grodeck Brigade advanced. Rain on day one turned terrain to quagmire, halting 22nd Panzer and disrupting C2 (radios inoperable, wires scattered in muck). It stopped by afternoon of day three; 22nd Panzer completed its short epic thrust (seven miles) into the rear. The Kessel became a "seething cauldron of fire and destruction" under thousands of Luftwaffe sorties daily. Thousands surrendered.

The Grodeck Brigade's pursuit was epic out of proportion to size. It blasted through Nasyr (lucky unoccupied sector) and Sultanovka lines, overrunning rear areas. Low on fuel/ammo, own Stukas bombed it (wounding Grodeck fatally); under Korne it got stuck in mud but received airdropped ammunition on 11 May. It did not reach Kerch first but paved the way. The full 11th Army pursued: Mattenklott's corps liquidated the northern pocket; XXX Corps the southern route. Kozlov was paralyzed (Stukas had pounded his HQ early); communications broke in the first minute. Mekhlis ("military illiterate") was "beyond worthless"; one historian called it "Manstein versus Mekhlis," no contest. Stalin demoted Mekhlis.

By the coast the scene was "amazing": remnants of three armies huddled on the shore seeking evacuation. Richthofen's best units had been recalled north for the Izyum emergency, reducing sorties from thousands to hundreds; Richthofen fumed that a rich target might escape. Manstein wheeled artillery to the cliffs and laid barrage after barrage on the fleet and masses. "The slaughter was terrific." Kerch fell to 170th Infantry Division on 16 May; mopping up lasted two days.

Operation Trappenjagd ended. Scattered groups in eastern coast caves were hunted later amid antipartisan cruelties. Soviet losses: ~170,000 men, over 1,100 guns, 250 tanks in ten days. German: 7,588. "This had been one of the most decisive victories in the history of the German army." Firepower was essential, but "the real key... had been the operational maneuver." It showed the Wehrmacht still possessed "an officer corps that could be depended on to think for itself, manpower with a great deal of drive and energy, and a level of aggression that had served it well over the centuries." The pursuit took its place with Blücher's to Paris after Waterloo.

All that remained was Sevastopol. Manstein concentrated "annihilation fire." By early June Richthofen had full airpower back (~600 ground-support aircraft); artillery of every size poured in, including 305/350/420 mm howitzers and the "big three"—two 600 mm Karl and Dora (800 mm, 90-foot barrel, 31.5-inch bore, rail-transported in 25 trainloads, 2,000-man crew, assembled in up to six weeks, fired only 48 rounds in anger here). "Sevastopol may indeed have been the 'strongest fortress in the world' in 1942": three dense lines, underground tunnels, active Black Sea Fleet role under Admiral F. S. Oktyabrsky (supplies, gunfire, marine infantry), 106,000 men of 1st Independent Coastal Army (Petrov).

Operation Störfang opened 2 June with the greatest concentration of fire the Germans had yet achieved (29 guns per mile on 21-mile front). It turned the area into "a sea of flames" and "indescribable" ruin. Individual strongpoints ("Bastion," "Molotov," "Siberia") and the two Maxim Gorky forts (armored 305 mm turrets) were stationary targets. Main attack: LIV Corps north toward Severnaya Bay (22nd ID reached north shore by 13 June, clearing Fort Stalin etc.); XXX Corps secondary thrust southeast, reaching Zapun Heights. Romanian Mountain Corps held center.

Manstein chose a classically German operational maneuver to "unhinge" the Zapun line rather than methodical investment. Just after midnight 28–29 June, elements of 50th Infantry Division (LIV) made a daring amphibious crossing of Severnaya Bay on 100 assault boats, seizing the steep southern bank by surprise (resistance light). By daybreak they lay just outside the town inside the Zapun position, overrunning Inkerman Ridge and old Malakov bastion. XXX Corps assaulted Zapun; 170th Infantry Division achieved the decisive breakthrough, rolling up the line. The innermost ring was fatally compromised. Stalin ordered evacuation late 30 June; promised ships never came amid German air/artillery on the Chersonese peninsula. Over 30,000 went into captivity there plus 60,000 inside Sevastopol. 11th Army entered the city 1 July. That evening Manstein learned of his promotion to field marshal.

German casualties for Sevastopol admitted at the time ~24,000 (4k killed) plus ~8,000 Romanian; independent estimates range 25,000–100,000. Soviet nearly 100 percent (90k prisoners + ~50k battle casualties out of ~150k). A small amphibious crossing had been "the decisive factor," cracking a position that had held an army for a month—classic avoidance of pure Stellungskrieg.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Soviet landings and reconstitution of Crimean Front aiming at destruction of 11th Army and reconquest of Crimea as part of Stalin's 1942 annihilation plan; "deep battle" doctrine with echeloned attacks.
- `contributed_to:` Failure of Soviet Parpach offensives (Feb–Apr) due to lack of professionalism, mud, artillery exposure, poor precautions; over 40% casualties and withdrawal to start line by 15 April.
- `contributed_to:` Manstein's operational eye identifying the northern bulge weakness after Soviet gains there; Hitler's massive air commitment (Luftflotte 4 + Richthofen) and personal interest in clearing Crimea.
- `caused_by:` Classic German planning: front-loaded, concentric, Schwerpunkt in south with deception north; infantry breach for Panzers; short left hook + aggressive pursuit force (Grodeck); amphibious flank landing behind main line.
- Luftwaffe (Richthofen) at operational level (`operativer Luftkrieg`) paralyzed Soviet C2 (Kozlov HQ hit minutes into attack; unsecured comms); "fighting air war on the operational level."
- `enabled:` Short distances allowed infantry to accompany Panzers; dense Soviet packing made air/artillery devastating and favored Kessel formation.
- For Sevastopol: `caused_by:` Need to finish Crimea before Operation Blue; concentration of entire air and artillery park (Dora etc.); Manstein's "unhinging" amphibious maneuver rather than slow shift of weight.
- `part_of:` Broader 1942 Wehrmacht recovery demonstrating continuing vitality of Bewegungskrieg after winter reverses.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` Annihilation of Crimean Front's three armies in ten days; 170,000 men, 1,100+ guns, 250 tanks lost; entire Kerch peninsula and port recaptured.
- `produced:` Fall of Sevastopol 1 July 1942; ~90,000+ prisoners in city + 30,000 on Chersonese; Manstein promoted field marshal.
- `produced:` "Moral tonic" for Wehrmacht; exultation rivaling Poland/France/early Barbarossa. Militär-Wochenblatt called it "great breakthrough battle," "first great victory of the spring," then "first annihilation battle of the spring" with "a force of vastly inferior numbers smashed the mass of three Russian armies." "The German soldier has stood up in all the shining brilliance of his old combat strength."
- `enabled:` Concentration of 11th Army resources and airpower (post-Kharkov emergency) for final Sevastopol reduction.
- `concurrent_with:` Izyum/Kharkov fighting (Timoshenko offensive and German counter) drawing some air assets temporarily.
- `preceded_by:` Winter 1941–42 Soviet landings and failed offensives; German defensive professionalism at Parpach.
- `followed_by:` Shift of focus to summer 1942 operations (Blue); demonstrated that rapid victories in German style still left "loose ends" (cave fighters, antipartisan operations).
- In grand scheme: Not decisive for war but "within the context of its time" dramatic proof the Wehrmacht "could still bring it" and "speak its own 'unique language'" of maneuver. "The war might already have been lost, but it was far from over."

## Actors

- **Erich von Manstein** (11th Army): Master of operational maneuver; planned Trappenjagd with elegant concentric Kesselschlacht and pursuit; executed daring Severnaya Bay crossing to unhinge Sevastopol. Promoted field marshal. (See [[actors/manstein-erich-von]])
- **D. T. Kozlov** (Crimean Front): Paralyzed by breakthrough and Mekhlis presence; "utterly incapable of fighting a battle of maneuver"; communications severed immediately.
- **Lev Mekhlis** (Stavka representative): "Military illiterate"; drove officers to distraction; "beyond worthless"; probably "worth an army corps to the Germans." Demoted by Stalin.
- **Wolfram von Richthofen** (Fliegerkorps VIII): Decisive air support; "one of the richest targets an airman could desire."
- Soviet army commanders (Lvov, Pervushin, Kolganov) and Romanian allies (credited more than Manstein's later memoirs allowed, despite equipment shortages).
- German corps and division commanders (Mattenklott, Fretter-Pico, Grodeck/Korne) demonstrated independence and drive.

## Historiography

Citino frames the Kerch campaign (Trappenjagd and Sevastopol reduction) as the Wehrmacht "reborn," reestablishing contact with its fundamental tradition of Bewegungskrieg after the near-death experience of winter 1941–42. It proved the continuing vitality of rapid, decisive campaigns of movement and maneuver, operational-level Kesselschlacht, and independence of subordinate commanders— "the lightning pursuit to Kerch" placed alongside Blücher's after Waterloo. The Militär-Wochenblatt contemporary coverage (22 and 29 May issues) reflects exultation: "great breakthrough battle," "first annihilation battle of the spring," "a force of vastly inferior numbers smashed the mass of three Russian armies," "classic form" with Schwerpunkt concentration, immediate exploitation, rapid Kessel formation, and tireless pursuit. "With the best staff work and first-rate troops, the attack went off like a precision watch."

Firepower (Richthofen air, massed artillery including Dora) was essential to crack strong positions, but the "real key" was operational maneuver. The Sevastopol phase illustrated that even against the "strongest fortress in the world," a small elegant amphibious stroke could unhinge what static firepower alone could not. Romanian performance was "quite credibly" better than Manstein's later *Lost Victories* caricature. Soviet command was crippled by political oversight (Mekhlis), poor C2 under stress, and inability to fight open maneuver battle once penetrated— "Manstein versus Mekhlis."

Citino notes the "bureaucratized, ideological, and unprofessional" nature of Soviet command as a crucial (but contextually real) factor; the operation takes on new significance "screened through the mentalité of the German officer corps." It was a "bitter lesson" for foes and "good news for Germany" at the start of a decisive year. Warning signs existed (airpower concentration not scalable to Blue; logistics), but German minds steeped in centuries of tradition perceived the moment as validation of the old truths. The campaign supplied the interpretive backbone for 1942 as the last flowering of classic German operational art before its structural "death."
