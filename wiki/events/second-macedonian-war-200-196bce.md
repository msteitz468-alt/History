# The Second Macedonian War (200–196 BCE)

The war in which Rome broke the power of **[[philip-v|Philip V of Macedon]]** and,
through **[[flamininus|T. Quinctius Flamininus]]**, proclaimed the
[[freedom-of-the-greeks|"freedom of the Greeks"]] — the decisive turn that drew
Rome permanently into the Greek world. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]]
Ch. 8 (Errington).

## Narrative

After the [[first-macedonian-war-214-205bce|Peace of Phoenice]] (205), Philip
turned to aggressive expansion in the **Aegean** — seizing cities, fighting two
sea-battles (Chios, Lade, 201), and besieging Abydus — alarming **Rhodes**,
**Pergamon (Attalus)** and Athens, who appealed to Rome. The Senate's "eastern
lobby" (Sulpicius Galba) carried a war-vote (the *comitia* rejected it once, then
relented). Rome issued an ultimatum framed as the protection of the Greeks; Philip
ignored it.

The war was fought with two aims (Errington): the destructive aim of forcing Philip
to obey Rome, and the constructive aim of **winning Greek "hearts and minds"**.
Galba (200–199) and Villius (199) made little headway; **Flamininus** (consul 198)
forced Philip from the Aous gorge, took the war into Thessaly and Phocis, and
detached Philip's allies — above all the **[[achaean-league|Achaean League]]**,
which abandoned its thirty-year Macedonian alliance for Rome. After inconclusive
talks at Nicaea (over Philip's "Fetters of Greece" — Demetrias, Chalcis, the
Acrocorinth), Flamininus secured his own prorogation and crushed Philip at
**Cynoscephalae** (197). Macedon was humbled but, deliberately, *not* destroyed —
Rome wanted no power-vacuum, especially with **[[antiochus-iii-the-great|Antiochus
III]]** advancing in Asia Minor.

At the **Isthmian Games of 196**, Flamininus had a herald proclaim the Greeks
"free, ungarrisoned, tribute-free, living by their own laws." The
[[freedom-of-the-greeks|declaration]] explicitly included the **Greeks of Asia
Minor** — a deliberate extension of Roman concern into Antiochus' sphere.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: Philip's Aegean aggression and the appeals of Rhodes/Pergamon/Athens —
but Errington stresses these were the **pretext**: the root was the unavenged
"stab in the back" of 215 and the senatorial lobby's appetite for the war (see
[[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]).

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the peace of 196 (Philip confined to Macedon, fleet and indemnity
surrendered, the "Fetters" eventually evacuated by 194); the
[[freedom-of-the-greeks|"freedom of the Greeks"]] as a Roman policy instrument.
enabled: the **extension of Roman interest into Asia Minor** and the
[[roman-syrian-war-192-188bce|collision with Antiochus III]]; the precedent of
Rome as arbiter of Greek affairs, which would harden (under Callicrates, 180) into
direct control.

## Historiography

[[polybius]] (via [[livy]]) is the spine. Errington reads the whole war as an
exercise in **propaganda backed by force**: the *senatus consultum* demands were
framed to be rejected, the "freedom" slogan calibrated for Greek opinion. The
Hannibal–Philip treaty and the chronology of the war-vote are debated. The
genuineness of Rome's "philhellenism" vs. its instrumentality is the central
interpretive question — see [[freedom-of-the-greeks]].
