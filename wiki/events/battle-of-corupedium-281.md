## Narrative

Corupedium (the "Plain of the Maidens," near Sardis in Lydia), fought early in 281 BCE,
was the **last battle between two of Alexander's original Successors** and the close of
the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Wars of the Diadochi]]. It pitted the aged
[[lysimachus]] — at the height of his power, ruling Thrace, much of Anatolia, and
Macedon — against the equally aged [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus I]], the only other
surviving marshal of Alexander.

The war grew out of a **dynastic catastrophe** in Lysimachus' house: pressed by his
queen [[arsinoe-ii|Arsinoe II]], Lysimachus had put his able heir **Agathocles** to
death (c. 283), shattering loyalty to his regime. Agathocles' widow Lysandra and her
brother [[ptolemy-ceraunus|Ptolemy Ceraunus]] fled to Seleucus and urged war; the
Anatolian Greek cities, alienated by Lysimachus' fiscal harshness, and the officer
**Philetaerus** of [[pergamon|Pergamum]] (with his treasury) went over to Seleucus.
At Corupedium Lysimachus was defeated and killed, and Seleucus immediately took his
Asian territories.

## Consequence Analysis

Corupedium briefly **reunited almost all of Alexander's empire** — Asia, plus the claim
to Macedon and Europe — in the hands of one man, Seleucus, the last of the Diadochi. It
was the nearest thing to a restoration of the empire since [[battle-of-ipsus-301|Ipsus]].
But the moment evaporated at once: crossing the Straits to claim Macedon (281), Seleucus
was **assassinated by Ptolemy Ceraunus**, his own protégé. The northern world dissolved
into a free-for-all — Ceraunus' usurpation of Macedon, the [[celtic-invasion-of-greece-279|Celtic
invasion]], and finally the accession of [[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Gonatas]] (276). Two
durable legacies: the Seleucid succession passed intact to [[antiochus-i-soter|Antiochus
I]] (whom Seleucus had wisely made co-regent), and Philetaerus' control of Lysimachus'
treasure at Pergamum founded the future Attalid kingdom of [[pergamon|Pergamum]].

## Historiography

Known from Justin, Memnon, Appian, and Pausanias, with the date fixed by Babylonian
cuneiform evidence (the [[seleucid-era|Seleucid king-list]]). Will treats it as the
final act of the age of the Successors — the point after which the unitary idea was
truly dead and the three-dynasty system became permanent. See [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]].
