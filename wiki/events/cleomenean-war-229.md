## Narrative

The Cleomenean War (229–222 BCE) was the struggle between the
**[[achaean-league|Achaean League]]** under **[[aratus-of-sicyon|Aratus]]** and a resurgent
**[[sparta|Sparta]]** under **[[cleomenes-iii|Cleomenes III]]** for the hegemony of the
Peloponnese — a war that began as a border clash and ended by drawing Macedon back into
southern Greece. CAH VII.1 ch. 12 (Walbank) traces its course.

Friction grew from Achaean expansion into **Arcadia** (the accession of Megalopolis, 235,
brought a tradition of anti-Spartan hostility into the League) and the Spartan seizure of
the disputed Arcadian cities (229). After early skirmishing, Cleomenes' military gifts told:
he beat Aratus at **Mt Lycaeum** (227), then carried out his **[[cleomenes-iii|revolution]]**
at Sparta (land-redistribution, the reformed *sarissa* phalanx) that made him far more
dangerous. He routed the Achaeans at the **Hecatombaeum** (226) and, as the **League began
to disintegrate** — torn by faction, Spartan sympathies, and hopes of social revolution in
its own cities — he swept up Argos, Corinth, and much of the Peloponnese.

Faced with collapse (and with [[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemy III]] switching his subsidies from
Achaea to Sparta), Aratus made the decisive **volte-face**: he secretly invited
**[[antigonus-iii-doson|Antigonus III Doson]]** of Macedon, surrendering the **Acrocorinth**
(224) as the price of intervention. The revolt of Argos from Cleomenes turned the tide;
Doson's army drove him back to Laconia, and the war was decided at **[[battle-of-sellasia-222|Sellasia]]**
(222), where Cleomenes was crushed and Sparta taken.

## Consequence Analysis

- **The Achaean League survived only by sacrificing its independence** — inviting back the
  Macedon it had spent a generation expelling, and joining Doson's **Hellenic Symmachy**.
- **Sparta's last bid for greatness ended**: the revolution was cancelled, the ephorate
  restored, and Cleomenes fled to die in Egypt (219).
- **Macedonian hegemony over the Peloponnese was restored** — the springboard for the
  [[social-war-220|Social War]] and, ultimately, the coming of Rome.

## Historiography

The war is unusually well covered — Polybius (drawing on a Megalopolitan source and on
Aratus' apologetic *Memoirs*) and Plutarch (drawing on the Cleomenes-sympathetic
[[hellenistic-historiography|Phylarchus]]) — which lets the **partisan distortions** be seen
from both sides (Aratus' silence about his own role in calling in Macedon; Phylarchus'
romantic colouring of Cleomenes). See [[cleomenes-iii]], [[aratus-of-sicyon]].
