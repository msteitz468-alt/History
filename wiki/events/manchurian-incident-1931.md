# The Manchurian Incident (1931)

The Japanese **Kwantung Army's unauthorized seizure of Manchuria** — begun with a staged
railway bombing near **Mukden on 18 September 1931** — the act of military insubordination
that demonstrated the army now drove national policy, destroyed
[[taisho-period|party government]] in Japan, and opened the fifteen-year road to the
[[pacific-war-1941-1945|Pacific War]]. From the
[[cambridge-history-japan-v6-twentieth-century|Cambridge History of Japan Vol. 6]] (Hata
Ikuhiko, ch. 6).

## Narrative

Staff officers of the **Kwantung Army** (the Japanese force guarding the South Manchurian
Railway zone) — chiefly **Ishiwara Kanji** and **Itagaki Seishirō** — manufactured a pretext
by dynamiting a stretch of railway near **Mukden**, then blamed Chinese troops and launched a
lightning occupation. Mukden fell overnight; the local Chinese commander **Zhang Xueliang**,
on orders, adopted **nonresistance**. To occupy all of Manchuria the army had to move outside
the treaty railway zone — a **capital offense** under its own code to act without imperial
sanction — yet the army commander Honjō was talked into it, and the **Korea Army** crossed the
border to reinforce **without permission from Tokyo**.

The crucial fact, Hata stresses, is that the **civilian government could not control its own
army**: the Wakatsuki cabinet (with [[taisho-period|Shidehara]] still foreign minister)
adopted a "nonexpansion" policy that the Kwantung Army **simply ignored**. When the cabinet
fell in December 1931, the Inukai cabinet **recognized the occupation as an accomplished
fact**. In 1932 Japan proclaimed the puppet state of **Manchukuo** under the last Qing
emperor Puyi.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the army's drive for **continental autonomy** and the conviction (the "Manchuria
  as lifeline" argument Hata examines) that Manchuria was vital to Japanese security against
  Chinese nationalism and the Soviet military buildup.
- `caused_by:` the **structural weakness of [[meiji-constitution-1889|party government]] over
  the independent military command** — the same flaw that fed [[japanese-militarism|militarism]].
- `contributed_to:` the **Depression's** intensification of the sense of crisis and the appeal
  of a resource-rich continental empire.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the conquest of Manchuria and the puppet state of **Manchukuo** — the great
  expansion of [[japanese-imperialism|Japanese imperialism]] beyond the formal colonial empire.
- `produced:` the **collapse of party cabinets** in Japan: the army's success discredited the
  parties and, with the assassination of Inukai (May 1932), ended the
  [[taisho-period|Taishō-democratic]] order.
- `produced:` **Japan's withdrawal from the League of Nations (1933)** — after the
  **Lytton Commission** condemned the occupation and the US **Stimson "nonrecognition
  doctrine"** refused to accept it — isolating Japan diplomatically while no power intervened
  (Britain, the US, and the USSR all held back; Chiang Kai-shek, preoccupied with the
  Communists, appealed to the League rather than fight).
- `contributed_to:` the **road to the [[pacific-war-1941-1945|China War (1937) and the Pacific
  War]]** — the first step of the "fifteen-year war."

## Historiography

Hata's chapter treats the Incident as the product of *gekokujō* ("the lower overawing the
higher") within the army — field officers driving national policy by *fait accompli* — and
examines, without endorsing, the contemporary "Manchuria as lifeline" rationale. The episode
is the classic case study of how Japan's **fragmented command structure** allowed a determined
military faction to commit the whole nation to war.
