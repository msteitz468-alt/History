## Narrative

On the first day of the eighth month of 1590 — a mere fourteen days after the eight Kantō provinces were formally transferred to him on the fourteenth of the seventh month — [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] made his official entry into Edo. Hideyoshi, who learned of this speed, exclaimed: "The Lord Tokugawa's way of doing things is really extraordinary."

### The State of Edo

Edo was, at this moment, close to a village. Beside the castle built by Ōta Dōkan 130 years earlier and taken by the Hōjō 62 years before — "extensive but dilapidated" — there was "but a straggling town that was hardly more than a village." The buildings were roofed with boards, the kitchens thatched, "the former much smoke begrimed." Rain had leaked through the roofs, staining the mats inside. The entrance was three planks from an old ship. The surrounding landscape was reedy swamp to the east (covering what is now Hibiya), the wide flat plain of Musashi to the north, and a handful of temples and shrines serving a population measured in "some hundred houses or so."

Ieyasu's own retainers advised making Odawara the capital instead — it was a prosperous city "a ri long, and well furnished with every kind of shop." Kamakura was the second choice. Ieyasu rejected both.

### Entry and First Decisions

Arriving by the western highway, Ieyasu paused at the gate of the Shingon temple of Kōmyōji at Kaizuka to speak with the priest Son-ō, recognizing him as a disciple of his own family's temple at Daijuji in Mikawa. He drank the abbot's tea, returned the next morning for rice-gruel and soybean soup, and arranged to make Kōmyōji his chantry temple in Edo — provided Son-ō would switch his sect from Shingon to the Tokugawa's Jodo faith. The monk agreed.

Inspection of the castle with Honda Masanobu revealed its dilapidation. Honda suggested at minimum the entrance should be rebuilt for the reception of daimyo. Ieyasu refused: "Now, Sado, it isn't like you to recommend such extravagance. Besides, the stipends of our retainers must be seen to before anything else." All that was done was to fill one moat and renew some floor mats.

Ieyasu immediately began reorganizing urban space, ordering temples and shrines to relocate outside the city center — issuing orders within his first month in Edo. About a dozen Nichiren, Shingon, Tendai, and Zen temples and four Shinto shrines that clustered in the Tsubonezawa district (near what is now the Imperial Park) were moved to new sites at state expense, with grants for the costs of moving. The speed of this decision forced the "leisure-loving ecclesiastics" to "execute the quick change of their lives." This was only the first relocation: as the city grew, many temples had to move again.

### Apportionment of Fiefs

Ieyasu distributed fiefs to his chief retainers: Odawara went to Okubo Tadayo (though Hideyoshi then asked for Hakone castle to be given to him too, to place Ieyasu's most trusted men under obligation to Toyotomi). Hideyoshi also prevailed on Ieyasu to send Honda Shigetsugu into retirement rather than giving him a fief. Ieyasu conceded. For the three great captains — Ii Naomasa, Honda Tadakatsu, and Sakakibara Yasumasa — Ieyasu originally intended 100,000 koku each. Learning that Hideyoshi would match any amount he named with something larger, Ieyasu stated 60,000, causing Hideyoshi to insist on 100,000 — a maneuver that saved Ieyasu 150,000 koku while demonstrating his docility.

Ieyasu ordered that retainers with small stipends receive properties near Edo, never more than a day's journey away, to prevent their salary being consumed by travel costs.

### Strategic Significance

Hideyoshi's purpose in placing Ieyasu in the Kantō was twofold: reward, and containment. By moving Ieyasu behind the Hakone barrier, he prevented a power base from threatening the capital. He then filled the Tōkaidō corridor — Kai, Kiyosu, Hamamatsu, Okazaki, Kakegawa, and Shizuoka — with his own reliable retainers. The Kantō was unfamiliar territory with pockets of residual Hōjō loyalty, and Hideyoshi may have hoped this would keep Ieyasu busy or even weaken him. Instead, Ieyasu spent the next decade building Edo into what would become the largest city in the world.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[odawara-campaign-1590]]` — The defeat of the Hōjō freed the eight Kantō provinces; Hideyoshi's offer transferred them to Ieyasu.
- `caused_by:` Hideyoshi's strategic calculation — he preferred a subordinate Ieyasu managing the Kantō from behind Hakone to a powerful Ieyasu on the Tōkaidō.
- `contributed_to:` Ieyasu's own strategic vision — Sadler suggests he "no doubt saw the possibilities of this now small city."

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[kanto-region]]` — The eight Kantō provinces became the Tokugawa power base.
- `enabled: [[edo-period]]` — Edo's development as the de facto capital of Japan, and subsequently the formal seat of the Tokugawa shogunate from 1603, began with this entry.
- `produced:` Urban reorganization patterns — the displacement of temples to city peripheries, the priority of retainer stipends over architectural display — that would characterize Tokugawa city-building.

## Actors

- [[tokugawa-ieyasu]] — chose Edo over Odawara and Kamakura; began immediate urban reorganization
- Honda Masanobu — surveyed the castle with Ieyasu; advised on renovation (overruled)
- Son-ō, abbot of Kōmyōji — established as Ieyasu's Edo chantry priest

## Historiography

Sadler uses the Kwan-hasshu-kosen-roku, the Mikawa Fudoki, and local Edo temple records to reconstruct the entry. The figure of Ōta Dōkan's original castle and the precise description of Edo's physical state at 1590 is an important reference for urban historians of Edo/Tokyo. The received narrative — that Ieyasu chose the backwater village of Edo over prosperous Odawara and Kamakura because he foresaw its potential — is a foundational story in Edo mythology, and Sadler presents it without skepticism. Modern scholarship tends to emphasize the structural constraints that made the Kantō assignment both a reward and a strategic trap that Ieyasu gradually transformed.
