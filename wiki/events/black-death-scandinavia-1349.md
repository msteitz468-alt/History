# The Black Death in Scandinavia

The pandemic of 1349–50 and its recurrent later waves, the demographic master-event
of the [[late-middle-ages|Scandinavian late Middle Ages]]. Per
[[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003|the Cambridge History]], the plague
reached Scandinavia **by sea, mostly from England and sometimes from Hanseatic
towns**, and spread along the Baltic and North Sea coasts. Bubonic plague killed half
or more of those infected — in the Middle Ages perhaps 80–90% — and the pneumonic
form was nearly always fatal.

## Narrative

Tradition makes **Bergen** the Norwegian entry point (an English ship, late summer
1349), though O. J. Benedictow argues it may have broken out first around **Oslo** in
autumn 1348 or spring 1349. In **Denmark** the main plague year was 1350; in
**Sweden** King Magnus Eriksson's decree of September/October 1349 warned of the
disease approaching from Norway. **Finland, northern Sweden, Iceland, and Greenland
escaped the 1349–50 wave**; Iceland's first outbreaks came only in 1402–4 and 1494–5.
Recurrent later plagues followed for over a century (Norway's "Child Death" of
1359–60, and many waves to c. 1500).

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the plague pathogen carried along maritime trade routes. The role of
**prior stress** is debated: deserted farms appear in Danish sources around 1315 and
Sjælland land prices fell from c. 1330, and a cold interval c. 1330–90 overlapped the
plague — but the "marginal land theory" of pre-existing decline is contested (Norway
shows few pre-1350 crisis signs).

## Consequence Analysis

The demographic collapse drove the [[scandinavian-agrarian-crisis|agrarian crisis]]:
in [[norway|Norway]] c. 56% of named farms and c. 61–62% of holdings were deserted by
c. 1520, land rents fell to 20–25% of pre-plague levels, and tithe revenue to about
one-fifth. Crucially, the **disintegration of the Norwegian service nobility** of the
royal *hirð* left Norway the weakest partner in the [[kalmar-union-1397|Kalmar
Union]] — a structural cause of its later political marginalisation. By contrast
[[finland|Finland]]'s farms roughly tripled, and surviving tenants generally gained
(lower rents, access to deserted land, rising wages).

## Historiography

O. J. Benedictow's epidemiological work is the key modern reference; his claim that
the plague reached Oslo before Bergen is contested (Bjørkvik). Norwegian abandonment
percentages are higher partly because the single-farm settlement pattern makes
desertion easier to detect than in Danish/Swedish villages — a methodological caveat.
See [[helle-cambridge-history-scandinavia-v1-2003]], [[late-middle-ages]].
