# Gloria Vanderbilt Custody Trial (October–November 1934)

Highly publicized New York custody battle over ten-year-old [[actors/vanderbilt-gloria|Gloria Vanderbilt]] between her mother, Gloria Morgan Vanderbilt, and her aunt [[actors/whitney-gertrude-vanderbilt|Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney]]. Cooper & Howe treat it as the moment the dynasty's private pathology became mass entertainment.

## Narrative

Reginald Claypoole Vanderbilt died leaving debts and a child trust (Cooper & Howe: on the order of $5 million) that made little Gloria financially significant. Mother Gloria Morgan, still young, spent roughly eight years largely in Europe with nurse Emma "Dodo" Kieslich; after health scares (including a tonsillectomy episode) and fears shaped by the Lindbergh kidnapping climate, Gertrude took the child in and cut the mother's draw on the trust (allowance figures in the book move from about $48,000 toward $9,000). Trial opened in October 1934 with lurid testimony — household mismanagement, pornography claims, a lesbianism accusation against the mother. On 21 November 1934 the judge granted sole custody to Gertrude; mother received weekend visitation, one summer month, and Christmas Day. All adult parties agreed Dodo must be dismissed — against the child's attachment. Gloria Morgan told the press that Park Avenue mothers (including Gertrude) also used nurses and schools, adding that she had "very little" while Gertrude had "$78 million."

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` legal custody favoring Gertrude Whitney
- `produced:` the "poor little rich girl" media template
- `enabled:` Gloria's later self-fashioning against that childhood spectacle; long silence then complicated reunion with her mother (1960 in Cooper & Howe's account)

## Actors

Child Gloria; mother Gloria Morgan Vanderbilt; aunt Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney; nurse Dodo; the press.

## Historiography

Trial transcripts, tabloids, Barbara Goldsmith's *Little Gloria... Happy at Last*, Gloria's memoirs, and Cooper's descendant narrative are distinct layers — do not collapse them. Cooper & Howe write with family access and explicit emotional stake.
