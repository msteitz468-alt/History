# Cartier Expeditions (1534–1542)

## Narrative

French voyages under Jacques Cartier (1534, 1535–36, and the 1541–42 colonizing attempt linked to Roberval) that mapped the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the river as far as Hochelaga (Montreal area), established early gift-trade and conflict patterns with St. Lawrence Iroquoians, and failed to produce either a passage to Asia or mineral wealth for the French crown. Part of [[sixteenth-century-north-american-contact]].

**Northwest Passage closed (1535).** By 1522 coastal exploration and Balboa’s 1513 Pacific discovery had shown that explorers had not reached Asia and that no sea passage joined Atlantic and Pacific between Florida and South America’s tip. French, Spanish, and English expeditions charted the eastern seaboard 1524–27 without finding a passage. Hopes of finding such a route south of the Arctic Circle were finally extinguished in 1535, when Cartier discovered that the Gulf of St. Lawrence was merely the estuary of a vast river. Later explorers (Verrazzano, Menéndez; then Champlain, Hudson, La Salle) still sought isthmuses or rivers to the Pacific.

**Gift trade (1534–35).** At Gaspé in 1534, initially reluctant St. Lawrence Iroquoians rowed out after realizing the French would give knives, glass beads, and combs; women received a comb and tin bell or ring each. At Hochelaga in 1535 Cartier distributed knives, hatchets, beads, and tin trinkets. Micmacs in Chaleur Bay held skins on sticks for trade; Cartier’s Gulf route suggests crew familiarity with existing fur-trading protocols (Cape Breton/Strait of Belle Isle by at least the 1530s). When Donnacona was carried off, followers gave him three bundles of beaver and seal skins to trade.

**Kidnapping.** In 1534 Cartier seized Donnacona’s two teenage sons/nephews; in 1536 he entrapped and carried off Donnacona and his two sons so that the old man could tell the king and other French officials tales of a land rich in precious metals to the west. Before an expedition to conquer this land could be launched, Donnacona and all but one of the ten Iroquoians Cartier had kidnapped in 1536 were dead. The practice fits a wider pattern of abductions for trophies and interpreters (Cabot, Verrazzano, Ayllón/Chicora, Frobisher, Weymouth). Returned sons in 1535 advised charging the French more for food because exchange goods were worth little in France.

**Kingdom of Saguenay and mineral failure.** After realizing the St. Lawrence was not a route to the Orient, Cartier sought mineral wealth. Donnacona’s interior stories were read as a “Kingdom of the Saguenay” rich in gold and silver with light-skinned, woollen-clad people. Francis I challenged Spanish claims and invested Roberval with vice-regal powers. Cartier 1541 and Roberval 1542 failed to penetrate west of the Lachine Rapids; Cartier’s “gold and diamonds” near Quebec proved valueless — official interest in Canada ceased immediately.

**Cap Rouge siege (1541–42).** After failure to return kidnapped people and unauthorized fortification, the Cap Rouge colony was besieged; 35+ colonists were killed.

**Alliance politics.** Donnacona had sought French ties for iron weapons against Micmacs and better standing versus Hochelagans; relations soured when Cartier visited Hochelaga without alliance and wintered without permission. Hochelagans brought their sick to Cartier in 1535 (supernatural/healer perception); Stadaconans, with more sustained contact, more quickly treated the French as mortal humans.

**Disease note.** During the 1535–36 winter near Stadacona, an assumed European disease killed about 10% of some 500 inhabitants; repeated localized infusions may have played a role in later St. Lawrence Iroquoian disappearance (Trigger & Swagerty; uncertainty preserved — not framed as a continental pandemic).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by`: French monarchy’s search for Asian routes and new wealth; competition with Spanish imperial claims
- `contributed_to` by: prior coastal charting showing no mid-latitude passage; existing coastal fur-trade protocols among fishermen and Native groups
- `part_of`: [[sixteenth-century-north-american-contact]]; `concurrent_with` [[de-soto-expedition-1539]] and other *entradas*
- Gift-kit diplomacy and kidnapping as standard early-contact practices

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced`: European knowledge that the St. Lawrence was a river system, not a strait to Asia
- `produced`: temporary collapse of official French colonizing interest after mineral failure
- `contributed_to`: [[eastern-north-american-fur-trade]] protocols and later Tadoussac-centered professional trade (1580s)
- `contributed_to`: French policy of *douceur* — realistic assessment of Native groups’ capacity to promote or thwart French goals — which became a cornerstone of later New France colonization (Washburn & Trigger)
- `contributed_to`: possible localized disease impact and political disruption among St. Lawrence Iroquoians; distrust from kidnapping that shaped subsequent encounters
- `followed_by`: long gap before Champlain-era permanent French presence; continuity of Basque/Breton fishery and fur contacts

## Actors

- Jacques Cartier; Sieur de Roberval; Francis I
- Donnacona and Stadaconan/Hochelagan St. Lawrence Iroquoians (not identical to later [[haudenosaunee-confederacy]] or [[huron-wendat]])
- Micmacs (Mi’kmaq) and Montagnais (Innu) as regional trade/war neighbors

## Historiography

Cartier’s narratives are classic European first-contact texts, long read more for French discovery history than Native-centered process. Trigger & Swagerty reframe them within fisheries, gift kits, kidnapping, alliance politics, and the limited reach of sixteenth-century biological and technological impact in the Northeast. Washburn & Trigger (CHNPA I.1 ch. 2) emphasize deep French mistrust when unable to dominate, combined with dependence for food and anti-Spanish cooperation in La Floride, crystallizing *douceur*. OCR of CHNPA sometimes renders “Cartier” as “Carrier” — claims use standard spelling. St. Lawrence Iroquoians must not be collapsed into later Haudenosaunee or Huron identities without care.
