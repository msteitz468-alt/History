# The Hussite Revolution and Wars (1419–1436)

The religious and national revolution that convulsed the kingdom of Bohemia after the burning of
[[hus-jan|Jan Hus]] (1415), analysed by John Klassen in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]] (Ch. 18).
For fifteen years the Hussites — at one point poised "to weld national feeling to the notion of
popular self-government" — defeated five papal-imperial crusades, before the nobility crushed the
radicals and brought Bohemia back, on its own terms, into the European order.

## Narrative

The revolution opened in **1419** when, after Wenceslas IV's death, the radicals refused to accept
the heir, **Emperor Sigismund**, who was committed to suppressing Hussitism (and tainted by Hus's
death). Pope Martin V proclaimed a **crusade** (1420). The Hussites split into two camps:

- the **moderate Utraquists** (the "Praguers") — nobles, university masters and burghers seeking
  acceptance within Christendom for the **chalice** and modest reform;
- the **radical Taborites**, who founded a fortified communal town, **Tabor**, in southern Bohemia,
  rejecting all existing social and political forms (some, the Chiliasts, expecting Christ's imminent
  return), and fielding the revolution's formidable peasant field armies.

Their fragile unity was expressed in the **Four Articles of Prague (1421):** communion in both
kinds, free preaching of the word, the limiting of clerical property, and the punishment of public
sin. Under the brilliant blind general **Jan Žižka** — and after him **Prokop the Shaven** — the
Hussite armies, using mobile **wagon-fortresses** (*wagenburg*) and hand-gunpowder weapons,
destroyed crusade after crusade (the rout at **Domažlice/Taus, 1431**, broke the last). They even
raided deep into Saxony, Franconia and Bavaria.

A combination of war-weariness, internal division and the conciliarists' willingness to negotiate
(the **Council of Basle**) let the noble-led moderates take control. In **1434** the conservative
coalition **defeated the radical field armies at Lipany**, ending Taborite military power. The diets
of 1435–6 negotiated the **Compacts** (a watered-down Four Articles), and in **July 1436 Sigismund
was finally accepted as king**.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the [[hus-jan|execution of Jan Hus]] (1415), which turned reform into "a quasi-national
revolution." `contributed_to:` deep grievances over **clerical wealth** (the Church held ~28% of
Bohemian land), **Czech–German ethnic rivalry** (sharpened by German control of Prague's government
and university), and the **refusal of the crusading Sigismund**. The Wycliffite theology of Hus —
that an unworthy clergy need not be obeyed and that the laity should judge their priests — provided
the ideological charge.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` (1) the **Compacts** and a lasting **Utraquist Church** — Bohemia remained a confessional
"island in a sea," its national king **George of Poděbrady** (1458–71) elected over a Catholic
rival; (2) a **massive redistribution of property** — 30–40% of productive land changed hands, the
Church reduced to a minor owner and the **nobility** (Catholic and Hussite alike) the great winners,
which underwrote their drive to dominate the kingdom (the towns excluded from the diet by the
Vladislav constitution of 1500); (3) a **military legacy** — Žižka's disciplined infantry, wagon-forts
and firearms influenced warfare across central Europe (see [[military-revolution]]). Tradition and
the desire for a Europe-recognised king "proved too strong" for the radicals' early experiment in
popular sovereignty.

## Historiography

Klassen frames the revolution as a contest between **popular sovereignty** (secular in Prague,
religious-egalitarian in Tabor — Howard Kaminsky's "leader acting with the people") and the
**three-tiered society** of estates that ultimately reasserted itself. He emphasises the **Czech
vernacular and national consciousness** the movement forged, and Tabor's genuine but incomplete
social radicalism (its leaders, often nobles, "looked after their own interests"). Hussitism is the
great pre-Reformation precedent — a century before [[protestant-reformation-1517|Luther]].

Housley (2002) adds focused analysis of sanctification, scriptural authority, and comparison with later sectarian cases (Münster) and frontier crusading. Taborite "economy of violence" and the limits of total war under divine mandate are highlighted. See [[sources/housley-religious-warfare-europe-1400-1536-2002]].

## From F. Donald Logan, *A History of the Church in the Middle Ages* (2013)

After Hus’s burning, rioting followed in Prague; Hussites became a Bohemian force often associated with Czech national aspirations, though many Czech nationalists were not Hussites and Hussites came to include Germans. Despite Constance burnings of Hus and Jerome of Prague, Cardinal **Cesarini**’s negotiations brought ~**300 Hussites** to **Basel** (Jan 1433) under iron-clad safe-conduct. **Compacta of Prague** (accepted both sides Nov 1433; council ratified **15 Jan 1437**) allowed communion under both kinds without requiring it, free preaching with controls, limited clerical property—Logan calls this Basel’s **greatest achievement**: it ended the Hussite Wars though unpacified dissidents remained, relieving the church of formidable Hussite pressure.
