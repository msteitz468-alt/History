## Narrative

When European sailors entered the western Pacific's tropical waters "they found themselves adrift in a world of bewildering diversity, wealth, and political complexity." The monsoon system (from Malay *musim*, meaning seasonality) structured all trade: northerly winds April–August, southerly December–March. Every trader, Asian or European, adjusted to this rhythm.

The Portuguese had arrived first. Their 1511 capture of Melaka positioned them to dominate Indian Ocean–China Sea trade, but their aggressive approach toward Ming China — sacking a Chinese tributary state, behaving as pirates — earned them exclusion from Canton until 1557 when they gained access via Macao. From Macao the "Great Ship from Amacon," an 800–2,000-ton carrack, sailed annually to Nagasaki carrying Chinese silk, "their only real competition was from Chinese smugglers." The Portuguese triangle trade — Indian textiles for spices, spices for silk in China, silk for silver in Japan — became the template all European competitors would try to copy.

### 1601: Murder at Macao

On 28 September 1601 Dutch Admiral Jacob van Neck anchored at Macao seeking trade "under terms of friendship." Portuguese authorities seized his men without warning, tortured them, and "hastily executed seventeen of them on trumped-up charges of piracy before Chinese officials could talk to them." Their motive: fear that Chinese officials would grant the Dutch the same trading access they had given Portugal. Van Neck, ignorant of who had taken his men, blamed the Chinese for "barbarian customs."

News of the killing reached Southeast Asia in mid-1602. Admiral Jacob van Heemskerck, confirming the killings from captured Portuguese papers and a Chinese crew at Patani, "avenged the Dutchmen's deaths by seizing" the 1,400-ton Portuguese carrack *Santa Catarina*, sold in Amsterdam for 2.25 million guilders as the first "prize" of the newly formed VOC (founded 1602). The murder ensured Dutch–Portuguese hostility in Asian waters for decades.

### VOC Conquest of the Spice Islands

Blocked from China trade, the VOC bypassed Iberian strongholds via the Sunda Straits, making for Maluku (Moluccas). The capture of the Portuguese fort at Ambon in 1605 gave the VOC its first territorial possession. Under VOC Director-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen (1587–1629), whose motto was "Trade without war, and war without trade cannot be maintained," the Dutch subjected the Bandanese to one of the era's bloodiest colonial episodes: "Of the original population of some 15,000 all but 1,500 died, and many were enslaved or deported to Batavia." The conquered town of Jayakarta was renamed Batavia and became the international hub of VOC Asia.

### 1623: The English Close Shop — The Amboyna Massacre

The English East India Company was founded 31 December 1600, directly competing with the Dutch. Critically outgunned — "the VOC sent on average seven ships to Asia for every three sent by the Company" — the English tried to fight in Java and failed. A short-lived 1619 Accord created a joint Anglo-Dutch defence fleet, but Coen sabotaged it at every turn.

The definitive break came in early 1623 when VOC Governor Herman van Speult at Ambon tortured ten English traders and ten Japanese mercenaries into confessing a conspiracy against Fort Victoria. Torture included "burning candles under the men's armpits and feet" and what is today called waterboarding. The Amboyna Massacre — ten Englishmen beheaded — provoked a pamphlet war and coloured Anglo-Dutch relations for decades, cited during three naval wars (1652–4, 1665–7, 1672–4) and immortalized in John Dryden's play *Amboyna: A Tragedy*. The Dutch eventually paid £85,000 in compensation in 1654.

In the same year, 1623, the English also closed their Japan factory — Richard Cocks, the Company factor in Hirado, had been systematically defrauded by the smuggler Li Dan ("China Captain"), whom he had trusted to unlock the China trade. Li Dan "strung them along for years" taking gifts and loans, never delivering. Cocks died disgraced at sea on 27 March 1624. The English redirected attention to the weaker Portuguese position in India.

### 1646: The Rise of Koxinga

The fall of the Ming Dynasty to the Manchu Qing threw the China Seas into turmoil. Zheng Zhilong, sealord of Fujian — who had risen from street hustler at Macao to baptized Catholic ("Nicholas Iquan"), interpreter for the Dutch in Taiwan, pirate/privateer, and finally Ming commander of Fujian Province — was captured by the Manchus on 21 November 1646. "Too late, Zheng cried out to his bodyguard of African musketeers; many were cut down trying to rescue their lord."

His son Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga, 1624–62) — "half-Chinese half-Japanese" (born of a Japanese mother in Hirado) — refused to surrender. Inheriting his father's warfleet and trade networks, he "ruled the South China Seas and monopolized the China–Japan–Southeast Asia trade triangle to finance his personal naval and land forces, which grew from a few hundred to more than 100,000 soldiers at his peak." He launched a massive assault on Nanjing in 1658–9 that was ultimately repulsed.

The Qing response was the *qianjie ling* (Coastal Depopulation Law) of 1661: coastal dwellers along 1,600 kilometres were "ordered to relocate 15–25 kilometres inland, and Qing troops laid waste to what remained." Hundreds of thousands fled to Southeast Asia or Taiwan.

### 1662: The Fall of Dutch Taiwan

Koxinga attacked Taiwan on 30 April 1661 with over 20,000 men, declaring to the Dutch: "I have come to reclaim my land, which my father loaned to the company." He trounced a Dutch musketeer company on the beaches, disproving VOC Governor Frederick Coyett's belief that "twenty-five Chinese together couldn't match one trained Dutch soldier."

The VOC's star-shaped Castle Zeelandia held for nine months. A Dutch relief fleet under Jacob Cauw was dispersed by typhoon before landing a decisive blow. Disease — "scurvy, beriberi, and dysentery ravaged their bodies and their will to fight" — ate at the garrison. The decisive factor was the defection of Hans Radis, "a drunken German mercenary" who, "tired of the expense of liquor" and inadequate food, walked to the Chinese camp and advised Koxinga to build a European-style demi-lune cannon battery to take Redoubt Utrecht. Coyett surrendered on 1 February 1662: "And thus, we had to leave our castle Zeelandia and all of the Island Formosa, to the shame of our nation in these Indian lands."

Koxinga died later in 1662, probably of malaria or syphilis, "staring out to sea."

### 1683: Qing Conquest of Taiwan and the English Position

Koxinga's son Zheng Jing (1641–81) allied with the English Company, which returned to East Asian waters. The English got "their first taste of tea" trading with Zheng Jing on Taiwan 1674–81. The Qing destroyed the Zheng fleet in the Taiwan Strait in 1683; Zheng Jing had died in 1681 and his son surrendered. Taiwan was incorporated into the Qing Empire.

When China officially reopened ports in 1684, "the English were the first responders." Their relationship with the Zheng family, despite its ignoble history, had positioned them uniquely. "The Dutch were stragglers in Canton and they reaped the reward of stragglers." The English China trade — eventually built on "massive cargoes of silver and a new and dangerously addictive item: opium from India" — was the long-term fruit of this era of rivalry.

## Historiography

Ho (Ch29 of [[jones-matsuda-cambridge-pacific-ocean-v1-2022]]) frames the period through five milestone incidents (1601, 1623, 1646, 1662, 1683) to trace "the fortunes of the Portuguese, Dutch, English, and Chinese." He stresses that "Asians and Europeans were equal partners" and that outcomes often "turned on translation errors or political machinations, lucky shots, personality clashes, accidents, or the weather." This is a revisionist counter to teleological accounts of European dominance. Tonio Andrade's *Lost Colony* (2011) provides the most detailed account of the Taiwan siege; Blussé's work on the VOC illuminates the Dutch perspective.
