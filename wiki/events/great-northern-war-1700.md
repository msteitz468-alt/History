# Great Northern War

## Narrative
The **Great Northern War** (1700–1721) was a monumental conflict waged by a coalition of Northern European powers, led by the Russian Tsardom under Peter the Great, against the Swedish Empire under Charles XII for control of the Baltic region (L2862-2866).

The war began in 1700 with a series of quick Swedish victories (L2866). Charles XII forced Denmark to surrender, then routed a much larger Russian army at the **Battle of Narva** in November 1700 (L2866-2870). Underestimating the Russians, Charles XII turned his main attention to Poland to depose King Augustus II, giving Peter I a crucial respite (L2872-2874).

Peter I utilized this time to rebuild his army, cast new cannons, and advance in Livonia and Estonia (L2874). In 1703, he founded St. Petersburg near the Neva River (L2874). After defeating Augustus II and forcing the Treaty of Altranstadt (1706), Charles XII launched a decisive campaign against Russia in 1708, advancing with 50,000 men (L2876-2880). 

Instead of marching on Moscow, Charles XII swerved south into Ukraine to join forces with the defecting Ukrainian Hetman Ivan Mazepa (L2884). However, Peter I intercepted and smashed Swedish reinforcements at the **Battle of Lesnaia** in October 1708, capturing their supply train (L2884-2888). The Swedish army was finally destroyed by Peter I at the **Battle of Poltava** on July 8, 1709, forcing Charles XII to escape to Turkey (L2888-2890).

Although the war was decided in 1709, it dragged on for twelve more years (L2894). Russia captured Viborg, Riga, and Reval (1710), occupied Finland, and won naval victories off Hangö (1714) (L2894-L2900). Following Charles XII's death in 1718, Sweden finally sued for peace (L2900). The war ended with the **Treaty of Nystad** on August 30, 1721 (L2900).

## Causal Analysis
The war was caused by:
* **The Baltic Window:** Peter I sought a direct maritime trade and communication route to Western Europe, bypassing Sweden's monopoly on Baltic ports (L2862, L2908).
* **Anti-Swedish Coalition:** The Baltic nobleman Johann Reinhold Patkul brought Russia, Denmark, and Poland-Saxony together in a coalition, hoping to exploit the accession of the fifteen-year-old Charles XII (L2864-2866).

From Bushkovitch (Cambridge History of Russia Vol. II exclusive range ll. 15003–15033): "The Northern War is another situation entirely, for there are many, if often imperfect, testimonies to Peter’s motives... During the Great Embassy of 1697–8 a number of the Europeans who met Peter and his entourage recorded some discussion about acquiring a Baltic port... The lands at the head of the Gulf of Finland, Ingria and the Kexholm province, had been part of Novgorod and then of Russia since the beginning of recorded history and were lost only in the Time of Troubles. The population remained to a large extent Orthodox... Of course the ethnic structure of the area per se was a matter of indifference in seventeenth-century Europe, but the whole story served as a reminder of the territory’s Russian past." "Peter’s propaganda and diplomacy stressed Christian solidarity against Islam" but "the religious factor" secondary. "In the 1717 tract and elsewhere Peter and his spokesmen also deviated from another norm of earlier Russian justifications for war, the defence of Orthodoxy... Stefan Iavorskii... did mention this issue in some of his early sermons, but it soon disappeared... In a different way, however, Peter retained a religious understanding of his war along with the secular rationale, for he clearly believed that God was on his side." "He celebrated his triumphs with liturgy as well as fireworks." "Many motives made up Peter’s decision to start and continue the war with Sweden. He felt that his cause was just, even according to the latest European thinking. He believed that Russia needed a port to maintain its prosperity and power. He thought Sweden was preventing Russia from acquiring the fruits of European civilisation. ## Consequence Analysis
The Great Northern War produced:
* **Treaty of Nystad:** Russia acquired Livonia, Estonia, Ingermanland, and Karelia, establishing itself firmly on the Baltic Sea (L2902).
* **Rise of the Russian Empire:** In October 1721, the Senate bestowed upon Peter I the titles of "Great" and "Emperor," officially inaugurating the Imperial Period of Russian history (L2902-L2906).
* **Decline of Sweden:** Sweden was reduced to a second-rate power, losing its hegemony in northern Europe (L2908).
* **Reforms and Cost:** The war acted as the primary driver for Peter the Great's internal reforms (conscription, administrative colleges, Governing Senate, and the poll tax), but it exacted a heavy toll in human lives and financial strain, exhausting the country's resources (L2922-2924, L3028-3030, L3040).

## Historiography

Bushkovitch's essay in the Cambridge History of Russia explicitly interrogates Peter's own stated war aims through the testimony of European diplomats present at the Great Embassy and through Peter's later propaganda (notably the 1717 tract), cautioning that such retrospective justifications blend genuine strategic calculation over Baltic access with dynastic-historical claims (Ingria as lost Novgorodian territory) and a religious framing that itself shifted over the war's course. Swedish and other European sources give a different emphasis, generally treating Charles XII's early overconfidence and his 1708 turn south into Ukraine as the war's real hinge rather than any single Russian strategic masterstroke. Source survival for the Petrine war effort — diplomatic correspondence, army records, Peter's own directives — is unusually rich for the period, but standard narratives have been revised in recent decades to treat the war less as an isolated military story and more as the direct engine of Peter's internal administrative and social reforms.
