## Narrative

Beginning in Seville in June 1391 and spreading rapidly across Castile and then Aragon (Valencia, Barcelona, and other cities) over the following months, waves of mob violence attacked Jewish quarters (*juderías*), killing thousands and destroying or forcibly converting entire communities. The violence was preceded by years of inflammatory preaching against Jews by Ferrand Martínez, Archdeacon of Écija, whose sermons blamed Jewish communities for a range of social ills and called for the destruction of synagogues; royal authorities had repeatedly failed to restrain him, and the death of King Juan I of Castile in October 1390 removed a check on escalating violence.

The pogroms devastated some of Iberia's oldest and most prominent Jewish communities. In Barcelona, the community that had produced the philosopher [[hasdai-crescas]] was destroyed by the mob; Crescas's own son was among those killed. Faced with the choice between death and baptism, unprecedented numbers of Jews across the affected cities converted to Christianity, producing a large new class of "New Christians" (conversos) whose sincerity of conversion would be a source of suspicion, social tension, and eventually formal Inquisitorial investigation for generations afterward.

The 1391 pogroms marked a decisive rupture in the position of Jews in Christian Iberia, ending the relatively stable (if always precarious) communal life that had persisted under royal protection in Castile and Aragon and setting in motion a century-long trajectory of shrinking Jewish communities, deepening suspicion of converso loyalty, and eventually the establishment of the Spanish Inquisition (1478) and the final expulsion of practicing Jews from Spain in 1492.

## Causal Analysis

The violence was `caused_by` the immediate agitation of Ferrand Martínez's preaching campaign and `enabled` by the collapse of royal authority after Juan I's death, which removed the crown's traditional (if inconsistent) protection of Jewish communities as royal property and taxpayers. It was `contributed_to` by longer-standing popular resentment of Jewish economic roles and legal status.

## Consequence Analysis

The pogroms `produced` a large converso population whose ambiguous religious status shaped Iberian religious and social politics for the following century, and `contributed_to` the eventual institutional apparatus (the Inquisition) built to police that ambiguity, as well as the final 1492 expulsion of Spain's remaining professing Jewish population.

## Actors

[[hasdai-crescas]], whose Barcelona community was destroyed and whose son was killed, exemplifies the personal and communal devastation; the Castilian and Aragonese crowns are implicated through their prior failure to restrain Martínez and their inability to prevent the violence once it began.

## Historiography

The 1391 pogroms are documented through a combination of Jewish chronicles and laments, Christian municipal and royal records, and later historical reconstruction; historians debate the relative weight of religious agitation, economic resentment, and the specific political vacuum of 1390–1391 in explaining why violence of this scale and coordination occurred when it did, as opposed to the more localized anti-Jewish violence of preceding centuries.
