# Wounded Knee Occupation (1973)

AIM-led takeover and siege of the Oglala Sioux ([[lakota]]) village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, in 1973—the principal radical-activism set-piece in Washburn’s CHNPA I.2 ch. 15. Distinct from the 1890 massacre at the same place ([[wounded-knee-1890]]).

## Narrative

Washburn treats the 1973 siege as the exemplary action of the [[american-indian-movement]]: AIM is “a radical organization of individual Indians who more often than not challenge existing tribal governments, as AIM did in the siege of the Oglala Sioux village of Wounded Knee, South Dakota, in 1973.” He places it among 1970s flashpoints that also drew anthropologists: establishment of Yellow Thunder Camp near a Sioux reservation; Trail of Broken Treaties (1972) and Longest Walk (1978) marches on Washington protesting treaty-rights violations; and Hopi–Navajo relocation/boundary dispute. Outside activists intended to redress Indian–government imbalance but were “not always welcomed by established tribal governments.” Named AIM leaders Dennis Banks and Russell Means appear in Washburn’s aftermath judgment (below), not in a blow-by-blow operational narrative—the chapter supplies no day-count, casualty table, or federal order of battle.

## Consequence Analysis

- **AUTHOR POSITION (Washburn):** Political turmoil of the 1960s–70s challenging tribal-government legitimacy was gradually replaced by conventional debate over particular tribal policies; radicals such as Banks and Means “ceased their attacks on tribal governments as political entities and sought rather to influence the course of those governments”; John Collier was “in effect, vindicated” as tribal governments were restored under English law/American government without losing Indian character; Indian politics turned inward as government-to-government confidence grew.
- Symbolic pairing of the 1973 siege with the 1890 massacre site became a lasting media and historiographical frame (beyond Washburn’s few paragraphs).
- Analogous later confrontation Washburn notes: Oka crisis 1990 (Mohawk armed defence)—analogous_to, not caused_by.

## Historiography

Washburn’s treatment is brief and evaluative, embedded in a larger argument that radicalism yielded to conventional tribal politics under self-determination. It is **not** a tactical or documentary reconstruction of the siege. Collection bias: CHNPA I.2 privileges policy, demography, and cultural “renaissance” over operational activist history; deeper military/policing detail and competing Pine Ridge factional accounts require monographs and primary collections not ingested here.
