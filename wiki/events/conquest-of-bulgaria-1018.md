# The Byzantine Conquest of Bulgaria (1018)

The **conquest of Bulgaria** completed by **[[basil-ii|Basil II]]** in **1018** ended the **[[first-bulgarian-empire|First Bulgarian Empire]]** and restored the Byzantine frontier to the Danube for the first time since the seventh century — the crowning achievement of the [[macedonian-dynasty|Macedonian]] reconquest. [[reuter-ncmh-v3-1999|NCMH III]] (Shepard) treats it as the climax of "Byzantium expanding."

## Narrative

After Symeon's tenth-century apogee, Bulgarian power revived in the west under **Tsar Samuel** (centred on Ohrid), who inflicted an early defeat on Basil at **Trajan's Gate (986)**. Basil, freed from civil war after 989, waged a relentless, decades-long campaign of attrition. The decisive blow fell at the **Battle of Kleidion (1014)**, after which Basil — earning the epithet ***Boulgaroktonos*, "the Bulgar-Slayer"** — is said to have **blinded some 14,000 captives**, leaving every hundredth man one-eyed to guide the rest home; Samuel reportedly died of shock at the sight. Bulgarian resistance collapsed over the next four years, and in **1018** Basil received the submission of the Bulgarian aristocracy and annexed the realm.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the **revival of Bulgarian power under Samuel** and the centuries-old Byzantine–Bulgarian struggle for the Balkans.
- `contributed_to:` Basil II's strategic aim of **restoring the Danube frontier** and his freedom of action after defeating the aristocratic revolts (secured by the [[conversion-of-vladimir-988|Rus′ alliance of 988]]).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **extinction of the First Bulgarian Empire** and its incorporation into Byzantium as the **themes of Bulgaria and Paristrion**, with the autonomous Bulgarian archbishopric of **Ohrid** preserved.
- `produced:` the restored **Danube frontier** and the apogee of Byzantine power in the Balkans.
- Bulgarian statehood would re-emerge only with the **Second Bulgarian Empire** (1185), after the heavy taxation and revolts of the eleventh and twelfth centuries.

## Historiography

The lurid story of the 14,000 blinded prisoners comes from later Byzantine chronicles and is probably exaggerated, but it crystallised Basil's fearsome reputation. NCMH III stresses that the conquest, however total, rested on Basil's personal generalship and left no durable settlement: Bulgarian discontent under direct Byzantine rule fed later revolts.
