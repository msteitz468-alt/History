# Operation Dragoon (August–September 1944)

The Allied invasion of southern France (originally Anvil). A major amphibious operation that opened a second front in France, secured vital ports (especially Marseille), and allowed the rapid advance of US and French forces up the Rhône Valley to link with the Normandy armies.

## Logistics Dimension (from Dworak)

By 1944 the Allies had mastered many of the lessons from Torch and Italy. Dragoon demonstrated mature base section development, effective use of DUKWs and beach operations, and the critical importance of captured or repaired ports for sustaining the drive into Germany. The campaign highlighted how logistics enabled (and eventually constrained) the rate of advance.

## Impact and Legacy (Ch. 9)

"Overall assessment of Operation Dragoon" credits the SLOC integration ("Southern Line of Communication (SLOC), 182–83, 186–88, 212") and rapid Rhône advance. "Allied success in the Mediterranean theater and, 212" fed directly into ETO. "Importance of the Mediterranean theater experiences to improvement of [ETO logistics]" (201–216). Eisenhower "growth as a commander from experiences in the Mediterranean theater, 208." German contrast: "reasons for the German failure in, 210–12" included supply shortfalls. "Peak Allied strength in October 1944, 213." Lessons transferred: "lessons learned from Mediterranean operations and applied to Operation Overlord, 203, 204, 205." (Verbatim index/endnotes.)

**Planning and Assumptions (verbatim from source slice):** Force 163 planned a two- or three-division assault, followed by buildup to ten divisions. The French First Army placed under US command provided the US supplied all supplies, transportation, and admin needs. Planners assumed no ports available until at least D+25; all supplies over beaches. Beaches selected: Cavalaire–Saint-Tropez–Saint-Raphaël for gradients, deep water, road nets, and Argens River Valley to cut ports from reinforcements. Eisenhower's four major objectives: contain/destroy German forces opposing northern France; secure major port for reinforcing divisions; advance north to threaten German southern flank and LOC; develop Allied LOC for support of advancing and reinforcing divisions. Three of four focused on support issues. VI Corps (Truscott) with 3rd Inf Div (left, Cavalaire), 45th Inf Div (center, Saint-Tropez), 36th Inf Div (right, Saint-Raphaël); provisional 1st Airborne TF (British brigade + two US regiments) drop near Le Muy. "The key to Dragoon was the ports."

**Key Assumptions:** Toulon not open until D+20 and handle 10,000 tons/day; Marseilles not until D+40. Assault force unable to support itself more than 20 miles inland until port operational. "Both ports [expected] to have considerable damage from German sappers." Marseilles peacetime capacity 20,000 tons/day (Allies needed ~15,000 for 6th Army Group). Toulon limited as former naval base.

**Mounting and Early Execution:** SOS established Northern BS in Corsica Jan 1 1944 under Col. John Ratay to support air ops but tagged for loading troops/supplies for southern France. Coastal BS activated July 6 1944 under Maj. Gen. Arthur Wilson (former Western BS); personnel familiarized with assault plan to assume beach support ASAP. Supplies preloaded on 100 ships. To support French: reequip 175,000 US troops pulled from Fifth Army + 150,000 French troops + build 30-day supplies for 450,000. By Aug 1 name changed to Dragoon for security. Force: 366,833 men and 56,051 vehicles ashore by D+30; expected to move 277,696 tons over beaches first month. Each task force commander initially responsible for resupply, transitioning to Beach Group Control. "By this point of the war amphibious assault planning had become more or less a matter of routine." "Planning staffs, especially in the SOS, could now anticipate problems and develop the means to deal with them." Ships loaded at Naples/Salerno (assault), Oran/Taranto (French follow-on). All ready Aug 8 on schedule. Landings Aug 15 1944 with 902 ships/assault craft + 1,200 other; 1,300 aircraft and 53 gunfire ships neutralized defenses. 3rd Div secured Saint-Tropez peninsula day 1; 36th faced firm opposition at Camel Red but objectives met by D+1 afternoon. French First Army landed D+1; reached Toulon Aug 22; ports of Marseilles and Toulon surrendered to French Aug 28.

**Fuel Crisis from Rapid Success:** Quick drive inland produced immediate fuel shortage—planners expected hard fight, so ~three-quarters of assault supplies were ammunition, little fuel. D+1 forced revision: diverted DUKWs/LCTs to merchant with 50,000 gal. By D+4 units >100 miles from beach (invalidating 20-mile assumption). Three US divisions using >100,000 gal/day; only 11,000 gal in beach depots Aug 21. "This shortage forced the Seventh Army to prioritize the off-loading of fuel and to restrict fuel consumption." By Aug 25 fuel deciding factor at Montelimar: 36th Inf Div vs German 11th Panzer; 48-hr fight each hamstrung by shortages in fuel/ammo/roads; neither able to destroy/exploit. 3rd Div pursuit also slowed. "The lack of supplies cost each side an opportunity." Remedies: trucks of newly arriving units moved fuel forward; opened rail Frejus–Saint-Maximin for forward dump; 6 million gal arrived Aug 28 off coast; VI Corps created three provisional truck companies for 213-mile hauls; additional dumps at Bourgoin/La Tour-du-Pin/Montelimar (reducing distances 24–83 mi). "The pressure to deliver fuel forward was so great that VI Corps had to restrict the truck drivers to driving no more than seventeen hours a day." "Fuel and ammunition would remain in short supply until the Allies could put the ports into operation, repair the roads and rail lines, and run a fuel pipeline northward from the coast." Port de Bouc (west of Marseille, fuel capacity) opened D+10 by navy order; engineers cleared end-Aug; operational in 10 days; Arles Canal ferried supplies 30 mi inland.

**Port Rehabilitation and Throughput:** Both ports fell D+13. "As expected, both ports had been badly damaged by friendly bombing and enemy demolition." Navy handled Toulon; army Marseilles. At Marseilles: only one of 23 piers serviceable; no cranes; mines everywhere (incl. 63-day delay mine); scuttled ships blocked (Germans noticed rapid Naples rehab, so broke backs with explosives; vessels 3 layers deep; 7 ships at western entrance + 65 scuttled; >200,000 tons sunk incl. 4 large across main channel). "The damage at Marseilles was worse than the damage at Naples." First 3 Liberty ships entered Sep 1 discharged via DUKWs. First pier discharge Sep 8: 1,219 tons. "Although planners had not expected to clear the Toulon and Marseille ports until D+20 and D+40, respectively, by D+14 both ports were in some stage of operation." "The Germans had become adept at damaging ports, but the Allied engineers and port units had become equally proficient at rehabilitating them." By Sep 15: up to 10,000 tons/day up Rhône. Mid-Oct: Marseilles 11,000 men, >19,000 tons/day discharged. "In comparison, the port of Cherbourg... could handle only 7,600 tons per day two months after its capture." From Sep 8 1944 to Jan 25 1945: >2 million tons through Marseilles. Convoys: 17-vessel unloaded in 7 days 7 hrs (82,017 tons); 16-ship in 6 days 18 hrs (89,575 tons). "This rate of discharge represented a transformational difference from the low rate achieved by the port units that had worked so ineffectively to clear the ports of North Africa two years earlier."

**Rhône Advance and Sustainment Maturation:** First six weeks: uncontested assault + 400-mile pursuit up Rhône Valley. Seventh Army (Patch) urged rapid inland move. By end Aug (D+15): advance line planned for D+60 but service forces only for quarter that distance. Sep 15 (D+30): 6th Army Group established; Seventh at Moselle (planned mid-Dec D+120). "The French and American divisions were outracing their supply lines." Continental BS (renamed from Coastal Sep 4) assumed beach control Sep 9 from engineers. HQ moved forward: advance to Dijon Oct 1 (after Grenoble recon). "Service units were still flowing into southern France, and engineers could repair or lay only so many miles of rail or pipeline per day or week." Fuel demand doubled forecast. Initial truck-only (rail/bridges destroyed); later short rail stretches + cross-load to trucks. "it was almost as difficult to refuel the convoy trucks hauling the fuel as it was to refuel the actual combat forces." Used air defense unit vehicles (Luftwaffe no threat); clerks/cooks became drivers; one driver 4 days/nights ~3 hrs sleep/night. Supply lines caught up end Sep (6 weeks post-landing) as German resistance stiffened at Moselle.

**Rail and Pipeline:** Military Railway Service: Lt Col Benjamin Decker landed D+1; rail cars D+2; first train Aug 17 D+3 (5.6 mi Saint-Tropez–Cogolin, 8 cars). Found 12 locos +80 cars NE of Toulon; 200k of 400k prewar freight cars recovered; shipped diesels/oil-burners +1,000 special cars from NA. 10% prewar locos remained. Rebuilt 42 bridges + >800 mi track in >4,000 mi system. Rail % clearance: Sep 23%, Oct 42%, Nov 40%, Dec 31%. Pipeline: 697th Engineer Petroleum Distribution Co landed near Saint-Raphaël; 4"/6" lines from Port de Bouc; max advance 10 mi/day (3x stated 100 mi/30 days capability). Total 1,507 mi pipeline in southern France (vs 731 mi by 3 BS in North Africa). "pipelines laid by army engineers carried one-third of the total supply tonnage of the force." Losses modest (~2% to evap/thieves/sabotage). 3 French refineries (>2M barrels storage) brought online near port.

**French and Command Integration:** French First Army (de Lattre) organic Base 901 had only 12,500 service forces (10% requirement; US planning ratios called for 112k for 8 divs; max fielded 29k). "From the beginning of the rearmament program, the French had focused efforts on producing combat units and had given the lowest priority to support units." Close working relationship with Coastal/Continental BS developed (liaison officers). By July 21 only 30 of 133 French units had 1/3 authorized equip; Joint Rearmament Committee + SOS made up shortages + special Muslim dietary provisions. Clothing sizing issues (French smaller on avg). As territory liberated, reservists/FFI/POWs swelled; policy debate led to plan for 120 security battalions + 8 addl French divs by Jun 1945 (6 from French industry). Oct 22: Continental Advance Section assumed majority Base 901 responsibilities; part subsumed Oct 24 with French deputy. Larkin-directed reorganization effective Sep 26: Continental BS → Continental Advance Section (close to armies, limited stocks for mobility: armies max 5 days rations/fuel/ammo; Advance Sec up to 15 days; Delta BS stores rest + ports); Delta BS (Ratay) for ports/reception/hospitals/POW/civil; SOS NATOUSA Advance (Gilland) at Lyons to oversee. Delta had 133 cos/dets; Advance Sec 86; supported ~64k sq mi. Sep 15 6th Army Group assumed; Seventh focused combat only. Nov 20: COMZONE MTOUSA → Southern Line of Communication (SLOC) under Larkin (focus on Dragoon priority; reported to European SOS). SLOC deactivated mid-Feb 1945 after link-up with northern; Larkin to Paris as deputy European COMZ.

**Comparison and Legacy:** "The successful landings and the lack of a coordinated German defense presented an opportunity." But "logistics... continued to be our most formidable opponent." (6th AG). Northern ports failed expectations (Cherbourg Jun 26 slow to capacity; Brest Sep 19 little use; Antwerp Nov 26 estuary clear). Southern provided alternative: by late Sep Eisenhower transferred XV Corps south; >80% 6th AG 1944 supplies via Marseilles. "Had the Allies chosen to land in France in 1943, they would not have had these same skills and abilities." Experience from 2 yrs MTO produced "remarkable level of capability, flexibility, and agility." "The Mediterranean SOS had been conducting support operations for the past twenty-four months, whereas the European SOS had been planning support..." Full-ship unload vs northern selective. Technical chiefs more integrated in MTO. Integration of SLOC staff into European COMZ brought "wealth of experience"; first act cancel selective unloading (unless Eisenhower direct). "The Mediterranean was the laboratory in which the US military could learn, experiment, and adapt."

## Historiography

This page's detail derives substantially from Dworak's *War of Supply* (2022), a logistics-focused reassessment built on Services of Supply records, unit logs, and official index/endnote material; it treats Dragoon less as a tactical narrative than as a case study in the maturation of Allied sustainment doctrine between North Africa and the drive into Germany. Because the operation is comparatively understudied relative to Overlord, much of its documentary basis remains institutional and logistical rather than derived from a rich secondary literature of competing interpretations; where debate exists it concerns the operation's strategic necessity (given competing demands for landing craft and troops against a continued Italian campaign) rather than contested facts about its execution.
