# Fatimid Conquest of Egypt (969)

The **Fatimid conquest of Egypt** in 969, carried out by the general [[jawhar-al-siqilli|Jawhar al-Siqilli]]
for the Imam-caliph [[al-muizz-li-din-allah|al-Mu'izz]], transformed the [[fatimid-caliphate|Fatimids]] from a
North African into a **Near Eastern and Mediterranean empire**. It was secured under a famous ***aman***
(guarantee of safety), and immediately produced the new capital **[[cairo|Cairo]]** and the mosque-university
of **al-Azhar**.

## Narrative

By the late 960s Egypt — governed by the **Ikhshidids** on behalf of a crumbling [[abbasid-caliphate|Abbasid]]
caliphate — was in "a cycle of disaster": famine, low Niles, and factional military strife, while the local
Fatimid da'wa (led by **Abu Ja'far Ahmad b. Nasr**) grew among the notables of **Fustat**, sending letters
inviting al-Mu'izz to intervene. Al-Mu'izz spent over **20 million dinars** over two years on preparations,
building wells and forts along the coast road. On **6 February 968** Jawhar set out at the head of a reported
**100,000 troops**; in **May 969** the army entered Egypt and camped in the Delta.

Envoys from Fustat — the chief judge and the Fatimid da'i, led by the **sharif Abu Ja'far Muslim al-Husayni** —
negotiated terms. Jawhar signed an ***aman*** guaranteeing "God's complete and universal safety" for the
Egyptians' lives, property, and families; the right of the Sunni majority to continue in their own *madhhab*
and rites; protection for **Christians and Jews** "according to previous custom"; secure pilgrimage routes,
coinage reform, abolition of unlawful taxes, and repair of mosques. After brief skirmishes with old military
factions, "the conquest was complete." Jawhar led the first Friday prayer in Fustat in al-Mu'izz's name,
founded Cairo and al-Azhar, and governed Egypt for four years until al-Mu'izz migrated the court east in
**972**, handing the Maghrib to the **[[maghrib-successor-states|Zirid]]** viceroy Buluggin b. Ziri.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** the **Ikhshidid collapse** — famine and factional fighting left Egypt undefended.
- **enabled:** the terminal weakness of **Abbasid** authority over its richest province.
- **contributed_to:** the growth of the **Fatimid da'wa** in Fustat, which supplied both intelligence and an
  invitation.
- **caused_by:** **al-Mu'izz's** long-prepared strategic ambition, signalled to a Byzantine envoy in 967 when
  he said future negotiations would be held "in Egypt."

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** the founding of **[[cairo|Cairo]]** (969) and **al-Azhar**, and the **transfer of the
  caliphate** from Ifriqiya to the Nile (972).
- **produced:** the ***aman*** as a durable **precedent of religious toleration**, setting the pluralist tone of
  Fatimid Egypt (administration staffed by Muslims, Christians, and Jews).
- **enabled:** two centuries of Fatimid rule on the Nile and the later contest with the Crusaders and Saladin.
- **produced:** the handover of Ifriqiya to the **Zirids**, whose later defection would reshape the Maghrib.

## Historiography

Uniquely for the Fatimid rise, this event is **richly documented on both sides**: the full text of the *aman*
is preserved by both **Egyptian (Sunni)** historians such as al-Maqrizi and **Ismaili** sources, and
[[qadi-al-numan|Qadi al-Nu'man]] and Ibn Hawqal give near-contemporary accounts. Jiwa stresses the *aman*'s
programmatic toleration and its comparison to the "Ahd of Ali." A minor chronological point — whether al-Mu'izz
renamed Cairo on his departure (972) or his arrival/residence (973) — reflects the departure-versus-arrival
framing in the sources; the conquest itself (969) and the migration (972) are secure.
