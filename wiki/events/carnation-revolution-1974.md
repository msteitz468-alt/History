## Narrative

On April 25, 1974, a Supreme Revolutionary Council composed of generals and colonels — many radicalized by Portugal's faltering colonial wars in Angola and Mozambique — overthrew Marcello Caetano, the moderate law professor who had succeeded the incapacitated dictator Salazar (comatose from 1968, died 1970). The coup, remembered as the Carnation Revolution for the flowers Lisbon civilians placed in soldiers' rifle barrels, opened eighteen months of the most radical political transformation in postwar Western Europe short of outright revolution.

Through 1974–1975 the ruling officers ended the colonial wars and granted independence to Angola and Mozambique, collectivized the great estates of southern Portugal into cooperatives, and delegated extensive power to elected factory councils. Procommunist Colonel Vasco Gonçalves became prime minister in June 1974, backed by the populist Major Otelo de Carvalho's new command structure, COPCON. Communist leader Álvaro Cunhal, unlike Italy's Eurocommunists, saw in the radical officers a genuine parallel revolutionary force and refused to moderate. But elections in April 1975 revealed that a large majority of Portuguese voters — family farmers of the north, the urban middle class — backed Mário Soares's moderate Socialists (40%) or the Democrats (27%), not the Communists (12.5%). Radicals overplayed their hand by occupying a Catholic radio station and a moderate newspaper in May 1975, triggering internal unrest and international pressure (Washington and the European Community both signaled opposition). In August 1975 President Francisco da Costa Gomes replaced Gonçalves with a moderate prime minister; a November 1975 coup attempt by Otelo de Carvalho and other radical officers was crushed at the cost of five dead, breaking the revolutionary momentum. A new constitution in April 1976 formally committed Portugal to a "socialist society" but sharply curtailed the Supreme Revolutionary Council's power (it was abolished in 1982).

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by` the exhaustion and radicalization produced by Portugal's colonial wars in Angola and Mozambique, which convinced a critical mass of career officers that only a fundamental transformation of the Portuguese state could end the fighting; `contributed_to` by the rigidity of Salazar's "Estado Novo" and its lack of a mechanism for peaceful reform after his 1968 incapacitation. `preceded_by`, not directly caused by, [[francoist-spain]]'s contemporaneous but much more tranquil royal succession — the two Iberian transitions occurred nearly simultaneously but through very different mechanisms (Portugal: military coup and mass mobilization; Spain: managed succession under King Juan Carlos, see [[spanish-transition-to-democracy]]).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced` the end of the Portuguese colonial empire (independence for Angola and Mozambique, 1975) and, by 1976, a functioning if fragile multiparty democracy under a new constitution. `enabled` a decade of unstable coalition government (nine ministries 1976–1984, no party achieving a parliamentary majority in four successive elections) before Soares's moderate Socialists consolidated power; `contributed_to` Portugal's 1986 accession to the European Community, which provided the external anchor for democratic and market consolidation IMF-mandated austerity could not achieve alone. Rural cooperatives in southern Portugal, created by the 1974–75 collectivizations, were substantially rolled back after 1977 as land was returned to former owners.

## Actors

President Francisco da Costa Gomes ("The Cork," for his survival skills) steered the transition to moderation; Mário Soares's Socialists provided the electoral anchor for democracy; Álvaro Cunhal's intransigent Communist Party, isolated after November 1975, represents the road not taken; General Otelo de Carvalho embodies the radical-officer faction. ## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler frame the Carnation Revolution as "the closest any European state had come since 1948 to a revolutionary seizure of power" and contrast its troubled, near-civil-war trajectory explicitly with the "gratifyingly tranquil" Spanish transition occurring at the same moment — a comparison worth its own controversy/comparison page given the shared Iberian authoritarian starting point and divergent paths. Key secondary works cited: Kenneth Maxwell, *The Making of Portuguese Democracy* (1995); Lawrence Graham and Douglas Wheeler, eds., *In Search of Modern Portugal* (1983).
