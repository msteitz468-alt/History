# Ansei Purge

The **Ansei Purge** (*Ansei no taigoku*, 安政の大獄) was a wave of arrests, forced retirements, and executions carried out by tairō Ii Naosuke from late 1858 through early 1860, targeting opponents of the [[tokugawa-shogunate]]'s treaty policy and its choice of shogunal successor. Named for the Ansei era (1854–1860), it was "known as the Great Purge of the Ansei era" and became a defining moment of the [[bakumatsu]] crisis, simultaneously demonstrating the shogunate's power and fatally discrediting it.

## Background

The purge arose from two converging crises. First, tairō Ii Naosuke had signed the Ansei commercial treaties — beginning with the Harris Treaty with the United States on July 29, 1858 — without obtaining imperial sanction. This enraged [[emperor-komei]], who threatened abdication, and galvanized court nobles and domain activists in the [[sonno-joi]] movement. Second, a dispute over the succession to the ailing shogun Tokugawa Iesada had split the bakufu between supporters of Tokugawa Yoshinobu (Hitotsubashi faction, associated with reform-minded domains and sonnō jōi sympathizers) and Tokugawa Iemochi (backed by Ii and the fudai daimyo establishment). Ii prevailed on both questions — Iemochi was appointed, and the treaties were signed — but doing so required crushing the opposition.

## Events

In October 1858, senior councillor Manabe Akikatsu arrived in Kyōto, ostensibly to explain the treaty situation to the court but in practice to implement the purge. He restored the shogunate's preferred chancellor Kujō Hisatada (replacing Kōmei's choice, Konoe Tadahiro), then systematically moved against the opposition.

Eight *sonnō jōi* samurai leaders were executed, including:
- **Yoshida Shōin** (1830–1859), the influential Chōshū educator and activist, executed for plotting to assassinate Manabe on his journey to Kyōto
- **Hashimoto Sanai** (1834–1859), Fukui domain's reform strategist
- **Rai Mikisaburō** (1826–1859), son of the historian Rai San'yō

The Keene text notes that "the 'crimes' of the others were by no means so clear" as Yoshida Shōin's. Beyond executions, "members of the aristocracy (even those of the most exalted families) who were suspected of sonnō jōi leanings were also questioned and forced to resign their offices. Others, judged to be more deeply involved, were confined to their quarters or ordered to shave their heads and become Buddhist priests."

The court nobles forced to retire or enter Buddhist orders included Kōmei's personal intimates: Konoe Tadahiro (the emperor's calligraphy tutor and former chancellor), Takatsukasa Masamichi (former chancellor), Takatsukasa Sukehiro, and Sanjō Sanetsumu. Kōmei repeatedly interceded for them and was repeatedly rebuffed by Manabe and the kyōto shoshidai Sakai Tadaaki, who demanded the men shave their heads and refused even a stay of punishment.

Their crime, as Manabe explained, was having violated the shogunate's prohibition against the court communicating directly with the domains — specifically, transmitting Kōmei's anti-treaty letter to Tokugawa Nariaki of Mito and inciting Mito and Fukui samurai to subversive activity.

## Kōmei's Response

The purge was a source of deep personal humiliation for [[emperor-komei]]. He described the accused men as having "faithfully served Kōmei and his father before him," noting that Takatsukasa had guided him through his accession and Konoe had been his tutor. In a private letter he wrote: "When the foreigners came to Japan, all four men had done everything humanly possible to accord with his wishes." His intercessions were entirely ineffective. As Keene observes, "Rarely has the contradiction between the reality and the actuality of imperial authority been so clear."

Kōmei declined to give Manabe an audience during his visit, and his final letter to the departing official condemned the treaties in unambiguous terms while accepting their fait accompli: "Friendship and commerce with the foreign barbarians constitutes a fatal flaw in the Imperial Land, a pollution of the Divine Land." He called for a return to *sakoku* (the closed country) as the ultimate goal, while pragmatically accepting the immediate situation.

## Consequences and Assessment

Ii Naosuke was assassinated by Mito rōnin at the Sakuradamon Gate in Edo on March 3, 1860 — a demonstration that the shogunate could not protect its most powerful official. His killers had been radicalized partly by the purge's execution of their domain's intellectual leaders.

Keene's assessment is blunt: "The purge proved to be a total failure and ultimately contributed to the shogunate's collapse, but the two years during which arrests and imprisonments were carried out would be remembered as a reign of terror." By making martyrs of Yoshida Shōin and Hashimoto Sanai — both of whom became iconic figures of patriotic self-sacrifice in the Meiji nationalist historiography — the purge accelerated the very radicalization it sought to prevent. The [[sonno-joi]] movement's subsequent political violence in Kyōto (ca. 1861–1864) was fueled by men who drew direct inspiration from the purge's victims.

## Historiography

Keene's account rests on a rich Japanese documentary record — the shogunate's own investigative and sentencing records, the correspondence of Emperor Kōmei (whose private letters are unusually well preserved), and the writings and last testaments of purge victims such as Yoshida Shōin, later canonized by Meiji nationalist historiography as martyrs of the Restoration. This martyrology creates a source-reliability problem: accounts of Yoshida Shōin and Hashimoto Sanai were shaped retrospectively by the political needs of the Meiji state that came to power partly in their name, and later biographical dictionaries tend to inflate their contemporary significance. Scholarly debate concerns how much the purge itself, rather than the treaty crisis it targeted, radicalized opposition to the shogunate — Keene reads it as a decisive catalyst, though the relative weight of the purge versus the treaties themselves versus longer-term economic dislocation remains open. Court records and Kōmei's letters give unusual insight into the emperor's private views, but domain perspectives beyond Chōshū and Mito are thinner in the collection.
