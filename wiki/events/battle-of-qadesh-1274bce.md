# The Battle of Qadesh

The great chariot battle between **Egypt and the Hittites** at **Qadesh on the
Orontes** — the best-documented battle of the ancient world (Ramesses II plastered
it across his temple walls), and the high point of the Late Bronze contest for
**Syria**. A **tactical draw** that Ramesses trumpeted as a victory, but a
**strategic Hittite success**. Treated from both sides in
[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II, Part 2]] (Ch. XXIII, Faulkner — Egyptian;
Ch. XXIV, Goetze — Hittite). Year 5 of Ramesses II — **1274 BCE** by modern
reckoning (the volume's high chronology dates it 1300).

## Narrative

The issue was control of **Qadesh (Kinza) and Amurru**, the Hittites'
southernmost holdings, which the Nineteenth Dynasty was determined to recover (see
[[sethos-i|Sethos I]] and [[ramesses-ii|Ramesses II]]). [[muwatalli-ii|Muwatalli
II]] assembled "all countries from the ends of the sea to the land of Khatti" —
including [[mitanni|Mitanni]], Arzawa, [[ugarit|Ugarit]] and the Anatolian
vassals — and waited, concealing his army **behind the city**.

Marching north in four divisions (**Amun, Pre, Ptah, Sutekh**) strung out over
miles, Ramesses was deceived by two captured Hittite "spies" into believing the
enemy was far away at Aleppo. He pressed ahead and pitched camp west of Qadesh —
whereupon Muwatalli's **chariotry forded the Orontes and smashed the division of
Pre** in the flank, then broke into the camp of Amun. The Egyptian army panicked;
Ramesses was surrounded with only his guard. He was saved by the chance arrival of a
flanking **task force from Amurru** (the *Ne'arin*) and then the division of Ptah,
which drove the Hittite chariots back into the river. Muwatalli kept his **large
infantry uncommitted** on the east bank; night ended the fighting.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the structural **Egyptian–Hittite rivalry over Syria** opened by
[[shuppiluliuma-i|Šuppiluliuma I]]'s conquests; contributed_to: the **defection of
Amurru** (Benteshina) to Egypt after [[sethos-i|Sethos I's]] campaigns, which forced
Muwatalli to fight; and Ramesses' **intelligence failure** (the planted spies) and
over-extended line of march, the immediate tactical cause of the near-disaster.

## Consequence Analysis

- The battle itself was a **draw**, but **strategically a Hittite win**: the
  Hittites held the field, pursued the Egyptians as far as Damascus, kept Qadesh,
  and recovered **Amurru** (deposing Benteshina). "Not even Ramesses II can have had
  enduring military successes... in Syria after the battle of Qadesh."
- The spectacle of an **unsuccessful Egyptian army marching home** triggered
  **revolts** across Palestine, which Ramesses spent years (storming Ascalon, taking
  Dapur) re-subduing.
- Sixteen more years of intermittent war convinced both powers that **neither could
  overcome the other**, leading directly to the [[egyptian-hittite-treaty-1259bce|
  peace treaty of Year 21 (1259 BCE)]].

## Historiography

Uniquely documented — the Egyptian **"Bulletin" and "Poem"** and the great battle
reliefs (Abydos, Luxor, the Ramesseum, Abu Simbel), to be "used with caution" as
royal propaganda, against the Hittite treaty and annal material. The contrast
between Egypt's victory-claims and the actual strategic outcome is a classic lesson
in reading ancient royal sources. Absolute dates depend on the
[[mesopotamian-chronology|Egyptian–Hittite synchronisms]].
