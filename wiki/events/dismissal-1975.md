## Narrative

On 11 November 1975, Governor-General Sir John Kerr terminated Prime Minister Gough Whitlam's commission and installed Liberal leader Malcolm Fraser as caretaker prime minister. "The dismissal joined Remembrance Day and Ned Kelly's hanging in a trio of momentous events on this date on the Australian calendar." Fraser won the subsequent election in a landslide.

## Background: The Supply Crisis

The immediate catalyst was the "loans affair." At the end of 1974, Whitlam and senior ministers had issued Minister for Minerals and Energy Rex Connor with authority to raise a large overseas loan — focused on the Middle East — to fund increased Australian ownership of minerals and energy resources. The first casualty was deputy prime minister Jim Cairns, dismissed after it emerged he had "cavalierly explored alternative options for a loan." Connor was then sacked when "evidence emerged that Connor had continued negotiations for a loan following the revocation of his authority."

Upon his election as Liberal Party leader in March 1975, Malcolm Fraser had warned that should the government become "so reprehensible" the Opposition might have to resort to using "whatever power is available to it." In October 1975 the Coalition used its Senate numbers to defer a vote on appropriation bills, vowing to do so until Whitlam called an election.

## The Constitutional Deadlock (27 days)

"Australia was in constitutional deadlock for 27 days." The Coalition used Senate numbers to defer supply; Whitlam was "equally adamant that the Opposition's actions were an affront to the principles of responsible government and hallowed convention that ministries are made and unmade in the lower house of parliament."

Whitlam "had not accounted for the vice-regal adventurism of Sir John Kerr, whom he had appointed governor-general in 1974." Kerr, "an unsteady mix of grandiosity and insecurity," consulted High Court justice Sir Anthony Mason clandestinely "to fortify myself for the action I was to take," and — defying the prime minister's express instruction — solicited advice from Chief Justice Sir Garfield Barwick, a former Liberal Party minister. On 11 November, against the background of (inflated) reports of wavering in Liberal ranks and Whitlam having decided to request a half-Senate election, Kerr acted.

## The Dismissal and Election

Kerr terminated Whitlam's commission and installed Fraser as caretaker prime minister. Whitlam endeavoured to transform the election into a plebiscite on Australian democracy, "charging the Coalition parties and Kerr with constitutional infamy." His rallies were "more messianic than those of 1972." But "Fraser was the ideal foil. A towering man of aloof bearing, whose ruthlessness during the constitutional crisis made him appear even steelier, he looked every inch the strong man who would put a stop to the political and economic rot of the past three years. Fraser won in a landslide."

## Consequence Analysis

Fraser won three consecutive elections (1975, 1977, 1980). His government enacted the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976, resettled Indochinese refugees, established SBS (ethnic broadcasting), and established the Australian Institute of Sport. However, "entrenched unemployment was the Fraser government's chief economic legacy." The Coalition lost office in March 1983 to Bob Hawke.

For Australian political culture, the dismissal created lasting divisions. "For Labor partisans and their fellow travellers in the middle-class intelligentsia, his governments always carried a stain of illegitimacy." The constitutional question of whether the governor-general had the right to dismiss a prime minister who held the confidence of the lower house remains contested.

## Historiography

The principal scholarly accounts: Jenny Hocking, *Gough Whitlam: His Time* (2012), chs 8–10 — argues Kerr's action was constitutionally illegitimate; Paul Kelly, *November 1975: The Inside Story of Australia's Greatest Political Crisis* (Allen & Unwin, 1995) — more sympathetic to Kerr's reserve powers reading. The constitutional legitimacy question is genuinely unresolved: the governor-general possesses reserve powers to dismiss a prime minister; whether those powers were appropriate here is disputed. More recently, declassified documents about the role of Australian intelligence (ASIO) and possible US involvement through the CIA have added further controversy, though the source does not address these dimensions. The dismissal is the centrepiece of Jenny Hocking's two-volume Whitlam biography and Michael Sexton, *The Great Crash* (2005).
