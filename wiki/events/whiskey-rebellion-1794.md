# Whiskey Rebellion (1791–1794)

The Whiskey Rebellion was the first major test of the new federal government's power to impose and collect an internal tax on American citizens. The 1791 excise on distilled spirits, part of Alexander Hamilton's financial program, fell especially hard on western Pennsylvania farmers who converted grain into whiskey for easier transport and used it as a medium of exchange in a cash-poor economy.

## Narrative

Congress passed the excise in 1791. Resistance in the backcountry included tarring and feathering revenue officers, closing courts, and armed gatherings. In 1794, after a federal marshal was fired upon, President Washington called out the militia from several states (personally leading the force part of the way) to suppress the uprising. The rebellion collapsed with minimal bloodshed; a few leaders were tried for treason (most acquitted or pardoned).

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Hamilton's deliberate choice of an internal excise to prove that the federal government could tax citizens directly, not merely through tariffs at ports.

**contributed_to:** Frontier perception that the new government favored eastern commercial interests and was repeating the unrepresentative taxation that had helped spark the Revolution.

**preceded_by:** The Constitution's grant of broad taxing powers to Congress and the assumption of state debts.

## Consequence Analysis

The suppression demonstrated that the federal government possessed both the will and the capacity to enforce internal taxes. For Hamilton and the Federalists it was a success; for many westerners and later Jeffersonians it confirmed that the new system could be turned against small producers. The event helped legitimate federal internal revenue as a constitutional reality.

## Actors

- Alexander Hamilton (architect of the tax and enforcement policy)
- George Washington (commanded the militia response)
- Western Pennsylvania distillers and farmers (the taxed population)

## Historiography

Standard military and political histories treat the Whiskey Rebellion as a successful early test of federal authority. Williamson (2025) emphasizes its fiscal meaning: it was the first time the new government collected a direct internal tax from ordinary citizens and used military force to do so. The contrast with the Revolution's "taxation with representation" rhetoric is explicit in the source. The tax itself was later reduced and the enforcement lesson remained.
