# The Greek Settlement of Asia Minor

The colonization of the west coast of Anatolia by Greek migrants in the
[[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]] — the **Aeolians** in Lesbos and the northern coast, the
**Ionians** in the central coast (Ionia), and the **Dorians** in the southeast (Cos,
Rhodes, Cnidus, Halicarnassus) — driven across the Aegean by the
[[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian invasion]] of the mainland. Out of these settlements
grew the **Hellenic polis**, the **Ionic dialect**, and the cultural world that
produced [[homer|Homer]] and the first Greek philosophy and science.
[[edwards-cah-v2p2-1975|CAH Vol. II.2]] treats it in two chapters: the migration
**legends** (Hammond, Ch. XXXVI) and the **settlement** itself (Cook, Ch. XXXVIII).

## The three streams

The whole coast "crumbled or fell" with the Hittite empire; the western Anatolian
principalities (Arzawa, Assuwa) vanished, leaving an underpopulated, politically
disorganized shore. Into it three Greek streams moved:

- **Aeolic (Lesbos and the southern Aeolis).** Led by descendants of **Orestes**
  (Penthilus, then Gras), settlers from Boeotia and Thessaly — whose dialect Lesbian
  closely resembles — took **Lesbos** (Mytilene becoming mistress of the island) and
  the mainland strip from the Caicus to the Gulf of Smyrna (a dodecapolis headed by
  **Cyme**, the home of Hesiod's father). Hammond dates the Aeolic stream earliest,
  c. 1130–1050 BCE. It "lacked depth" and made little later history.
- **Ionic (the centre).** The great migration. Tradition (Strabo, Pausanias) makes it
  a single organized act issuing from **Athens** under the sons of king **Codrus** —
  **Neleus** founding Miletus, **Androclus** founding Ephesus. Cook accepts Athens as
  the genuine metropolis (the shared Attic-Ionic calendar, the **Apaturia** festival,
  the **four Ionic tribes**, Eleusinian Demeter, and the Athenian-influenced
  Protogeometric pottery all point there) while showing the "single act" to be a
  schematizing fiction: the real settlement was a **plurality of small foundations**,
  many no bigger than villages, of which the successful ten later monopolized the
  coast.
- **Dorian (the southeast).** Lacedaemonian and Argive Dorians settled **Cos, Rhodes
  (Lindus, Ialysus, Camirus), Cnidus, and Halicarnassus** — the cities of the later
  **Triopian Hexapolis** that celebrated the games of Triopian Apollo. (Halicarnassus,
  though counted Dorian, spoke Ionic and was half-Carian — Herodotus' own city.)

## The Ionian cities

The twelve cities of the **Panionic league** — the island-states **Samos** and
**Chios**, and on the mainland **Miletus, Myus, Priene, Ephesus, Colophon, Lebedos,
Teos, Clazomenae, Phocaea**, with **Erythrae** — were planted mostly on **peninsulas
and headlands**: sheltered, defensible against the natives, with anchorage for a
seaborne people. **Miletus** (on the site of a Mycenaean/Cretan settlement, the
**Millawanda** of the Hittite texts) and **Smyrna** show the earliest Protogeometric
pottery, c. 1000 BCE, fixing Ionic settlement at least that early.

The migrants did **not** displace the natives wholesale. The pre-Greek population —
**Carians** in the south, Lelegians in the north — was absorbed: at Miletus the
Ionians "killed the male Carians and took the women as their wives" (the women, it
was said, refusing ever to eat with their husbands). It was by **exploiting native
labour on the land**, Cook argues, that the Ionians "gained the leisure required for
cultural pursuits" — the explanation for how so small a population (Smyrna perhaps
~2,000 free Greeks) contributed so much to early Greek civilization.

## The birth of the polis

Excavation at **Old Smyrna** traces the development from a scatter of isolated
oval mud-brick huts (~end of the tenth century) to a **fortified, densely built town
with an axial street plan** by the early seventh century — "the first certain and
unambiguous apparition of the organized Hellenic *polis*." The Ionic cities passed
from early **kingship** (the *basileis*, surviving as the clan of Basilidae) toward
aristocracy and oligarchy. Their late-eighth-century **Panionic league**, centred on
the cult of Poseidon Heliconius at Mount Mycale, was a religious union of autonomous
cities, formed (Cook argues) out of the **Meliac War** in which the twelve cities
destroyed and partitioned the thirteenth, Melie.

## The Anatolian interior

The coastal Greeks met little inland opposition until the seventh century. Behind
them lay **Lydia** (Sardis, two or three days' march away, whose Mermnad kings would
later dominate the cities) and, further off on the plateau, the **[[phrygians|
Phrygians]]** at Gordium under **Midas** — in touch with Assyria and Urartu, possibly
the channel by which the alphabet reached them, but (the Gordium excavations suggest)
not yet trading goods or art with the Greeks. The **Cimmerian** raids c. 700 BCE
shook both Phrygia and the Greek cities.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the [[dorian-invasion-1120bce|Dorian invasion]] of the mainland, which
  drove the Mycenaean royal houses (the **Neleidae** of Pylos, the Ionians of Achaea)
  into Attica and then overcrowded **refugee Athens**, launching the eastward
  migration (Thucydides I.2, I.12).
- `contributed_to:` the political vacuum on the Anatolian coast left by the fall of
  the [[hittites|Hittite Empire]] and the western principalities in the
  [[bronze-age-collapse-1200bce|collapse]].

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **Greek cities of Asia Minor** and the **Ionic dialect** — and,
  through them, the *polis*, the **Homeric** epic tradition (Smyrna and Chios claimed
  [[homer|Homer]]), and the Ionian "humanism" and natural philosophy that matured at
  Miletus.
- `produced:` a lasting **Greek–Carian–Lydian** frontier and the cultural mixing of
  the Anatolian coast.
- `concurrent_with:` the Protogeometric recovery of the [[greek-dark-age|Dark Age]]
  Aegean and the consolidation of the Dorian Peloponnese.

## Historiography

Cook's chapter is a careful sifting of legend against the (then) early excavations of
Smyrna, Miletus, and Old Smyrna. He accepts the core traditions — **Athenian
leadership**, the **diversity of origins** (Pylians, Boeotians, Euboeans within the
Ionic body), the broad dating to the eleventh–tenth centuries — while dismantling the
schematic "single organized colonization" and the supposed "hard core" of pure
Ionians. The chief instruments are **Protogeometric pottery** (the archaeological
recognition-symbol of original settlement) and the **dialect/cult/tribe** evidence;
the chief limit is how little of the relevant sites had been dug. The relation of
**Mycenaean** finds at Miletus, Ephesus, and the southeast islands to the Dark Age
settlements — whether they prove earlier Greek occupation — is left open (and connects
to [[coming-of-the-greeks-1900bce|the language question]] and the
[[ahhiyawa-question|Aḫḫiyawa debate]]).
