# Battle of Megiddo (1457 BCE)

The first and most celebrated campaign of [[thutmose-iii|Thutmose III]] (his regnal year 23),
fought at **Megiddo** in the Jezreel Valley against a Levantine coalition led by the **ruler
of Kadesh** and backed by states as far as **Nahrin (Mitanni)**. It is the earliest battle in
history for which a reasonably detailed account survives, recorded in the king's **Annals** at
Karnak. (Distinct from the [[events/battle-of-megiddo-609bce|later Battle of Megiddo of 609
BCE]].)

## Narrative

Marching from the border fortress of Tjaru via Gaza to **Yehem**, Thutmose III made the
audacious decision to advance on Megiddo through the **narrow Aruna pass** — against his
officers' caution — catching the coalition off guard. The Egyptians won the field, but the
enemy fled into the fortified town and the campaign became a **seven-month siege**, ending in
the surrender and peace-offerings of the Levantine chiefs.

## Booty and aftermath

The Annals enumerate the spoils: "894 chariots, including two covered with gold, 200 suits of
armour and two of bronze belonging to the chiefs of Megiddo and Kadesh, as well as over 2,000
horses, and 25,000 animals." Thutmose **replaced the defeated local chiefs** and pushed north
toward the Litani. Megiddo opened seventeen years of near-annual campaigning that established
**Egyptian dominance over Palestine and southern Syria**.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the [[processes/egyptian-imperialism|Egyptian empire in the Levant]] and the
  **vassal system** — the children of city-rulers taken to Egypt "to be Egyptianized," and
  "whoever died from among these chiefs, his Majesty caused that his son stand in his place."
- `produced:` regular **tribute** (*inu*) from Retenu and Djahy, distinguished in the Annals
  from the one-time booty of campaigns against Nahrin.
- `enabled:` the influx of Syrian luxury goods and craft (glass, metalwork) that reshaped
  Egyptian elite material culture.

## Historiography

The battle is known almost entirely from Thutmose III's **Annals**, inscribed at Karnak in his
year 42 — a royal record that magnifies the king's role and compresses the siege. Eleven
Theban tombs of his officials supplement it. As royal propaganda framed by
[[concepts/divine-kingship|divine kingship]], the numbers and the tidy decisiveness should be
read critically, but the campaign's strategic outcome is not in doubt.
