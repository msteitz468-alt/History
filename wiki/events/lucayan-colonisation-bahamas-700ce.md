## Narrative

The Bahama Archipelago — a chain of limestone islands stretching over 1,000 km from near West Palm Beach, Florida, to within 120 km of Haiti and Cuba — was the last major Caribbean region to be settled. All evidence indicates that the archipelago was unoccupied before c. AD 700, and no Archaic Age sites have been found anywhere in the Bahamas. The culture history falls entirely within the Ceramic Age.

Settlement unfolded in two overlapping streams across roughly eight centuries:

**Stream 1 — From Cuba (central and northern Bahamas, c. AD 700)**: The current evidence suggests that communities from northern Cuba initiated the first permanent settlements in the central Bahamas. These were Archaic Age communities in the protoagrícola stage who had recently adopted greater pottery use. The earliest dated Lucayan site — Preacher's Cave on northern Eleuthera (cal. AD 560–720; burial AD 810–1010) — and the Three Dog site on San Salvador (8th–early 9th century) have imported pottery with affinities to the Arroyo del Palo style of northern Cuba. Six lines of evidence support Cuban origins for central Bahamian colonisation: (1) simultaneous expansion of ceramic use in Cuba and earliest Bahamas sites; (2) pottery similarities between Arroyo del Palo and Three Dog sites; (3) locally invented Palmetto ware (using pond/mangrove clays and calcined conch-shell temper) spreading from a single invention point; (4) rapid adoption of shell tools (a long Cuban tradition) where stone was unavailable; (5) high residential mobility characteristic of Cuban protoagrícola settlements; (6) continuing emphasis on marine-resource extraction. Small sites on the Jumentos Cays (100 km west of the central Bahamas, 150 km from north Cuba) served as way-stations and water sources for long-distance voyages.

**Stream 2 — From Hispaniola (southern Bahamas / Turks & Caicos, c. AD 700–1300)**: Hispaniolan communities knew of and exploited the Turks & Caicos Islands by AD 700. The Coralie site on Grand Turk (GT-3), excavated by Keegan and Carlson 1995–1997, is the oldest site in the southern Bahamas (cal. AD 705). All its pottery is Ostionoid with quartz-sand tempers petrographically sourced to Hispaniola. This and the Governors Beach site (GT-2) represent not permanent colonisation but seasonal resource-extraction and specialist-craft expeditions from Hispaniola communities; Meillacoid visitors also appear in the Turks & Caicos from c. AD 1100–1300. From the 13th century AD, Meillacoid and later Chicoid influences arrived from Hispaniola in the southern islands (Sinelli 2013).

The indigenous communities that became permanently established throughout the archipelago produced a locally distinctive pottery called **Palmetto ware** — coiled, low-fired earthenware from pond and mangrove clays, tempered with calcined conch shell, fired in an oxidising environment, mostly unslipped, with vessels that are hemispherical, carinated, or boat-shaped. Palmetto ware constitutes ≥90% of most Bahamian pottery assemblages. Plastic decoration (less than 10%: incised parallels, crosshatch, sigmoid appliqué) shows Meillacoid affiliations. The most distinctive feature is basket impressions on griddle bases, including intricate weaves of deliberate design whose meaning is under study (Hutcheson 2015).

Settlement was strongly coastal — no interior Bahamian sites have been found despite repeated surveys — and settlement patterns show high residential mobility, consistent with hurricane-prone environments that discourage heavy permanent infrastructure. More than 90% of "village" sites occur in paired settlements within each other's 5-km terrestrial catchment, a pattern also observed in Cuba and Hispaniola interpreted as reflecting matrilineal/avunculocal social organisation (Keegan and Maclachlan 1989).

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: [[post-saladoid-caribbean-differentiation|Post-Saladoid Caribbean diversification]] — the emergence of Meillacoid agricultural communities on Hispaniola (c. AD 800) and the protoagrícola stage in Cuba (c. AD 700–1000) created expansionary populations with the maritime technology and agricultural toolkit to colonise new islands.

contributed_to: Availability of exceptional marine resources — nowhere else in the Caribbean are sea turtles, iguanas, and large fish found in such abundance as in the Turks & Caicos. The Coralie site documents extirpation of mature breeding turtles within 200 years of arrival; the resource gradient between depleted Hispaniolan coastal zones (as documented at contemporaneous Île à Rat) and pristine Bahamian waters was a powerful pull factor.

contributed_to: Social dynamics of paired-settlement organisation and matrilineal descent — the structural need to establish new residential units through fissioning of existing communities drove ongoing colonisation northward through the archipelago.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: Permanent settlement of the last uninhabited major Caribbean island group, completing the Ceramic Age settlement of the insular Caribbean.

produced: A distinct Bahamian cultural florescence (Lucayan/Palmetto ware tradition) that was neither a simple extension of Hispaniola nor of Cuba, but a locally developed adaptation to the unique calcareous limestone environment and seasonal resource cycles.

enabled: Export economy linking the Bahamas to Hispaniola — particularly salt (at MC-6, Middle Caicos), salt fish, cotton thread, and Chama sarda shell beads. These goods supplied the large Hispaniolan populations with preserved protein and ritual/exchange items. Indigenous Bahamians controlled production and exchange; their status was expressed in the construction of MC-6 as a unique port of trade.

produced: The first documented encounter between Columbus and the Americas (October 12, 1492, Guanahaní/San Salvador), which triggered the [[columbian-exchange-1492|Columbian Exchange]] and the rapid destruction of the indigenous Bahamian population. Peter Martyr reported 40,000 individuals exported from the Bahamas as slave labour, especially as divers for the pearl-fishing industry at Nueva Cádiz, Venezuela. Juan Ponce de León encountered only one old man traversing the Bahamas in 1513. Some radiocarbon dates document continuation of indigenous lifeways into the early 17th century, suggesting pockets of refuge or semi-independent production under Spanish tolerance.

## Actors

**[[lucayans|Lucayan communities]]**: The indigenous Bahamian population encountered by Columbus. The name "Lucayan" derives from the Spanish Las Islas de Los Lucayos. Keegan and Hofman (2017) follow the convention of avoiding the name for the pre-contact period to prevent anachronistic ethnic labelling.

**Hispaniolan communities**: Meillacoid fishing and craft-specialist expeditions to the southern Bahamas (Coralie, Governors Beach), likely operating from northern Haiti. Controlled ownership of fishing territories in the Turks & Caicos (seasonal occupation patterns at Coralie document repeated reoccupation of a 240 m² area over 400 years). Governors Beach was a Meillacoid specialist bead-making workshop (c. AD 1100–1280): Chama sarda shell beads (brilliant scarlet, long-lasting color) manufactured for export to Hispaniola, where their redness, marine origin, and distant-place provenance gave them high exchange value.

## Key Sites

**Coralie (GT-3), Grand Turk** (c. AD 700–1100): Oldest site in the southern Bahamas. Ostionoid pottery sourced to Hispaniola. Faunal sequence documents rapid depletion of turtle and iguana populations within 200 years. Thin deposit over large area; radiocarbon dates assigned to different areas document a 400-year sequence of seasonal/short-term reoccupations. Canoe paddle radiocarbon dated AD 1100 recovered from North Creek peat.

**Governors Beach (GT-2), Grand Turk** (c. AD 1100–1280): Meillacoid shell-bead workshop. 1,500+ complete Chama sarda beads; 4,400+ blanks, partially drilled beads, and production failures; 550+ chert drill bits. Elaborate production infrastructure including concrete polishing platforms constructed on site. Site abruptly abandoned c. AD 1280 — valued objects (including 400 beads thrown into fire, effigy vessels, a long-used Triton's trumpet) discarded or destroyed, suggesting forcible ejection, possibly by established Bahamian communities asserting territorial control. Faunal remains (mostly grunt fish heads) indicate all food procurement was minimised relative to the primary bead-making activity; smoked fish fillets were likely transported back to Haiti.

**MC-6, Middle Caicos** (c. AD 1400–1600): The defining Late Ceramic Age Bahamian site. A locally controlled entrepôt — 90% Palmetto ware; 10% imported Chicoid vessels and manioc-beer bottles from Hispaniola. Unique layout: 270 × 70 m, with midden ridges enclosing two plazas, eight stone-lined pit structures around a central stone-lined court (31 × 15–19 m, astronomically aligned to summer solstice and Orion transit), and an indigenous road connecting the site to Armstrong Pond (salt source). Armstrong Pond produces vast quantities of solar-distilled salt during summer (480 litres collected in 15 minutes during July 1977 survey). MC-6 combined salt production, marine productivity of the Caicos Bank (salt fish), and cotton cultivation (the eastern "Plaza II" was probably a 3-hectare cotton garden) to supply Hispaniola with essential preserved protein and exchange goods. AMS dates suggest activities continued to AD 1630, possibly unmolested by Spanish who found indigenous salt, fish, and cotton production economically useful.

## Historiography

The colonisation sequence was reconstructed primarily through Keegan and Carlson's (1995–1999) excavations at Coralie and Governors Beach. The previous consensus model attributed Bahamian colonisation to Ostionoid expansion from Puerto Rico westward (Berman and Gnivecki 1995; Keegan 1992; Sears and Sullivan 1978); calibration of radiocarbon dates shifted all recognised pottery series two centuries later, revealing that the Bahamas were colonised three centuries before the full Ceramic Age in Cuba (Persons 2013). The revised model attributes first permanent settlement to Cuban protoagrícola communities (Archaic Age communities with incipient pottery use), with Hispaniolan Meillacoid communities conducting resource-extraction and craft-specialist expeditions to the southern Bahamas from the same period.

Berman et al. (2013) divide Bahamian prehistory into Non-Lucayan (AD 700–1300), Early Lucayan (AD 700/800–1100), and Late Lucayan (AD 1100–1530+) periods, distinguishing sites with non-local imported pottery from those with locally made Palmetto ware. Keegan and Hofman (2017) avoid the "Lucayan" label for the archaeological period.

The site of MC-6 has been studied intensively (Sullivan 1981; Keegan 2007; Morsink 2012) and is the best-documented pre-Columbian entrepôt in the Caribbean.
