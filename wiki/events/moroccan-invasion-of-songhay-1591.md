# The Moroccan Invasion of Songhay (1591)

The **1591 Moroccan invasion** ended the **[[songhay-empire|Songhay Empire]]**, the last
and largest of the great medieval **[[western-sudan-states|Western Sudanic]]** empires, and
closed the millennium-long era of large Sahelian states tied to the
**[[trans-saharan-trade|trans-Saharan trade]]**. Documented for the wiki through John
Hunwick's chapter in [[ajayi-crowder-history-west-africa-v1-1971|*History of West Africa*
Vol. I]] (1971).

## Narrative

In **October 1590** the Saadian sultan **Ahmad al-Mansur** dispatched a force of some **4,000
fighting men and 600 engineers**, equipped with muskets and ten stone-firing mortars, under a
young Spanish renegade, **Judar Pasha**. Carried by 10,000 camels and 1,000 pack-horses, the
army crossed the Sahara in the cool season and reached the Niger after a two-month march. On
**13 March 1591** it met a Songhay army of perhaps **40,000** (other estimates 30,000 infantry
+ 12,500 cavalry) under **Askia Ishaq II** at **Tankondibo, near Tondibi**, about 35 miles north
of Gao. Neither the Songhay's vast numerical superiority nor their stratagem of driving a herd
of cattle ahead of their lines could break the disciplined Andalusian and renegade musketeers:
the **firearms proved decisive**, exactly as al-Mansur had predicted, and the Songhay army was
routed (casualties on both sides were, in fact, light).

Judar occupied Gao — and was "greatly disappointed with the poverty and drabness" of the
capital — then moved to **Timbuktu**, where his troops' seizure of the merchants' quarter, grain
levies, and boorishness provoked serious rioting. The new pasha **Mahmud Zarqun** answered with
savage reprisals, including the exile in chains of the celebrated scholar **[[ahmad-baba|Ahmad
Baba]]** and his family.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the long-running **Taghaza salt-tax dispute** — successive sultans claimed the
  revenue of the central-Saharan salt mines; Askia Ishaq I had answered an earlier demand with a
  punitive raid, but al-Mansur's aim was far larger: direct control of the **Sudanese gold**.
- `contributed_to:` **Songhay's internal weakness** — the nepotic succession system (offices
  redistributed among the askia's brothers each reign) and the **1588 civil war** of the western
  governors, reported to al-Mansur by his spy at Gao, convinced him the empire was ripe.
- `enabled:` the **military-technological gap** — a small mobile force of musketeers against a
  numerically superior army with "no tried tactics against gunpowder and shot." Al-Mansur's
  council at first opposed the venture as logistically mad; he persuaded them that firearms would
  decide it. (See [[gunpowder-weapons]].)

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **end of the Songhay Empire** and of the era of large Sahelian empires.
- `produced:` the **[[western-sudan-arma-pashalik|Arma pashalik]]** — Moroccan rule through
  pashas and puppet askias over the Gao–Jenne stretch, though al-Mansur soon found the region
  **impossible to govern** as a true province (its drabness and distance disappointed him).
- the **fragmentation** of the Niger Bend: the western provinces fell to Tuareg, Fulbe and
  Bambara raiders within the year; the **askia dynasty survived only in Dendi** to the south-east,
  "now just another minor Sudanic state."
- the **decline of Timbuktu** as a centre of trade and Islamic learning.
- in the long run, a **reorientation of West African trade** away from the Saharan north and
  toward the [[atlantic-slave-trade|Atlantic coast]] — the European-driven economy that the rest
  of this volume's chapters trace.

## Historiography

The event is exceptionally well documented from **both sides**: the Timbuktu chronicles (*Ta'rikh
al-Sudan* of al-Sa'di, *Ta'rikh al-Fattash*) from within the Sudan, and Moroccan/Spanish sources
(al-Fishtali's *Manahil*, the anonymous Spaniard's account, the *Chronique anonyme*) from the
conquerors' side — a rare convergence that lets historians cross-check numbers and chronology.
Hunwick (himself the editor of the *Ta'rikh al-Sudan*) treats Tondibi as the textbook case of the
**gunpowder revolution** reaching the Sahel. The **aftermath** is treated in this volume's Ch. 12
(John Ralph Willis): the conquerors' offspring became the **[[western-sudan-arma-pashalik|Arma]]**
soldier-caste, ruling the Middle Niger from Timbuktu through the *Makhzan* administration and
breaking free of Morocco by c. 1612–60. Willis **tests and qualifies** the old "time of troubles"
thesis — Arma rule was administratively weak and mercenary-dependent, but not the wholesale
collapse of Sudanese Islamic civilisation it is sometimes painted as; the capture of
[[ahmad-baba|Ahmad Baba]] was political, not religious, expediency. The volume's narrative closes
with the **Kunta clerical realignment** under al-Mukhtar al-Kunti (d. 1811), the rise of
trans-political Qadiri authority that prefigures the [[west-african-jihads|jihads]].
