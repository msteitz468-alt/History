# D-Day (Normandy Invasion), 6 June 1944

**Event page.** Discrete bounded occurrence: the Allied amphibious and airborne invasion of Normandy (Operation OVERLORD). Narrative overview draws from broader WWII sources; this page incorporates specific grounding from Jean Edward Smith, *FDR* (2007), especially endnotes for the D-Day chapter (Range 08 of source ingest).

## Narrative

The largest amphibious invasion in history. Allied forces under Supreme Commander Dwight D. Eisenhower landed on five beaches in Normandy on June 6, 1944, after months of buildup and deception operations. Air and naval bombardment preceded the landings; airborne divisions secured key objectives inland. Despite heavy casualties on some beaches (especially Omaha), the lodgment was secured. German response was hampered by command confusion, Allied air superiority, and prior weakening of forces.

From FDR's perspective as Commander-in-Chief (per Smith endnotes): the operation capped years of strategic debate within the Grand Alliance over timing of the cross-Channel attack versus Mediterranean priorities.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by / contributed_to**: Long diplomatic and military preparation. Tehran and earlier conferences set the 1944 date (cross-referenced in notes). FDR's insistence on unified command and "Germany first" priority. Intelligence, logistics, and air campaign (preceding months) enabled success. German high command failures (Hitler and Rundstedt) contributed.

**preceded_by**: Casablanca (1943 unconditional surrender policy and de Gaulle/Giraud meeting); TRIDENT conference decisions on OVERLORD timing.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced**: Secure beachhead leading to liberation of France; enabled rapid advance across Western Europe; forced Germany into two-front war at full intensity; political consequences for French governance (see de Gaulle recognition below).

**enabled**: Final campaigns in Europe; accelerated end of war in 1945; shaped postwar occupation zones and French role in alliance.

## Historiography (from Smith 2007 notes)

Primary sources heavily cited: FRUS Conferences at Cairo and Teheran; Churchill, *Closing the Ring*; Pogue, *The Supreme Command*; de Gaulle, *War Memoirs* vol. 3; Sherwood, *Roosevelt and Hopkins*; Leahy, *I Was There*.

Key grounded claims (verbatim from Range 08 endnotes for Ch. 25):

- FDR to Eisenhower, May 13, 1944: direct instructions on political/military coordination with French elements (FDRL).
- On June 13, 1944 (six days after D-Day), the Joint Chiefs of Staff (Marshall, King, Arnold) advised Roosevelt: “The situation is serious and the effect on military operations unhappy at best, and may be dangerous in view of possible reactions of the French underground and resistance groups, who have generally expressed their allegiance to General de Gaulle.” (JCS to FDR, June 13, 1944, FDRL).
- De Gaulle broadcast (June 6 context): “The supreme battle has been joined... The orders given by the French Government [de Gaulle’s provisional regime] and its leaders must be followed precisely.” (de Gaulle, 3 *War Memoirs* 256).
- Halifax (British ambassador) to Eden on eve of D-Day: Leahy had advised the president that only Pétain could help the Allies in the liberation of France (Berthon, *Allies at War*; Black, *Franklin Delano Roosevelt*).
- Earlier strategic context (notes cross-referencing planning): Eisenhower noted cross-Channel attack could not be staged before August 1944 due to force requirements (quoted in Pogue).

FDR prayer (epigraph, delivered to nation June 6): from 13 *Public Papers and Addresses*.

Controversies noted: concurrent de Gaulle recognition fight (FDR reluctant until military necessity compelled; see JCS memo); Pétain vs. de Gaulle options weighed by some advisers.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)

Evans's framing: deception fixed German expectations on Calais or Norway; on 5–6 June 1944 over 4,000 landing craft and 1,000+ warships carried the force across while three airborne divisions dropped inland. With the navy finished, the air force broken by the escort war (see [[strategic-bombing-campaign]]), and the crack divisions in the East, resistance collapsed everywhere but Omaha; 155,000 men were ashore by nightfall, Cherbourg fell by 27 June, and Rundstedt and Rommel had "no effective strategic plan." The two-front war was now literal; Hitler answered with a purge (Zeitzler broken, Rundstedt and Sperrle sacked) — and, within weeks, faced [[operation-bagration-1944|Bagration]] in the East and the [[july-plot-1944|July Plot]] at home.
