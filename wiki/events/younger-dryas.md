# The Younger Dryas

The **Younger Dryas** (c. 12,900–11,700 BP) was an abrupt return to near-glacial conditions that
interrupted the warming at the end of the last Ice Age. Named for the Arctic wildflower *Dryas
octopetala* whose pollen marks the cold interval in European sediments, it ended as sharply as it
began — Greenland ice cores record a temperature jump of several degrees within decades at its
close (~11,700 BP, the conventional base of the Holocene).

## Narrative

After the warm Bølling–Allerød interstadial, temperatures across the North Atlantic plunged back
toward glacial values for roughly 1,200 years before recovering abruptly. The reversal was most
intense around the North Atlantic but registered, with varying sign and magnitude, across much of
the globe.

## Causal Analysis

The leading explanation is a sudden weakening of the Atlantic thermohaline ("conveyor")
circulation, triggered when a vast pulse of glacial meltwater — classically attributed to the
draining of proglacial **Lake Agassiz** — freshened the North Atlantic and suppressed deep-water
formation. The trigger remains debated (a rival hypothesis invokes an extraterrestrial impact),
but the oceanographic mechanism is widely accepted. `caused_by:` deglacial instability of the
terminal Pleistocene.

## Consequence Analysis

The Younger Dryas is central to the debate over [[agricultural-origins|why agriculture began]].
In the Levant it stressed the **late Natufian** foragers, whose economy depended on wild cereals
and nut harvests; the cold, dry downturn is widely argued to have pushed them toward
pre-domestication cultivation, so that the return of warmth in the early Holocene met populations
already managing plants — one of the proposed drivers of the [[neolithic|Neolithic transition]].
This climate-stress model is laid out in [[early-agriculture-southwest-asia|the southwest-Asian
agricultural sequence]] and weighed in [[why-did-agriculture-begin|the live controversy]] (see
[[barker-cwh-v2-2015|Barker, CWH II]]); critics caution that climate was an enabling backdrop, not
a sufficient cause.

## Historiography

The event is dated by multiple independent proxies — radiocarbon, annually layered ice cores
(GISP2/GRIP), varves, and pollen — giving it unusually firm chronology for a prehistoric climate
phase. Its sharp onset and termination made it the type case for **abrupt climate change** in
paleoclimatology. How much causal weight it carries for the origins of farming remains contested,
the central methodological problem being the move from correlation (cold snap + cultivation) to
causation. Absence of written sources is no barrier here: the dating rests on physical proxies.
