# The Gutian Invasion and the Fall of Akkad

The disintegration of the [[akkadian-empire|world's first empire]] and the roughly
century-long interval of **Gutian** overlordship that followed, before a Sumerian
hero, **Utu-khegal of Uruk**, expelled them and cleared the way for the
[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]]. Treated in
[[edwards-cah-v1p2-1971|CAH Vol. I, Part 2]] (Ch. XIX, §§V–VI, C. J. Gadd).

## Narrative

The empire built by [[sargon-of-akkad|Sargon]] and [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin]] broke
apart under Naram-Sin's son **Shar-kali-sharri**, who — like the later Ibbi-Sin of
Ur — was caught between converging enemies: the **[[unknown|Amorites]]** he fought
at **Basar** (Jebel Bishri) in the northwest, the **Gutians** of the Zagros, and a
seceding **Elam**. After his ~25-year reign the dynasty fell into anarchy: the
[[sumerian-king-list|king-list]] breaks its monotone to ask *"Who was king? Who
was not king?"* — four claimants in three years. A truncated Agade dynasty and a
shadowy Fourth Dynasty of Uruk flickered on, but the **Gutians** — mountain tribes
the Babylonians remembered as "the stinging serpent of the hills" — held a loose,
predatory supremacy (the king-list allows them ~21 kings and **125 years**).

Gutian rule left "very little mark": few monuments, outlandish names slowly taking
on Babylonian colour, and — at **Ashur** — the great Ishtar temple reduced to a
level of squalid hovels. Trade withered; a victor's inscription said the last
Gutian king "had made long grass to grow upon the highways of the land." Yet their
control was always partial: local dynasties ruled alongside them, and at **Lagash**
the governors **Ur-Baba** and especially **[[gudea|Gudea]]** presided over an
independent and brilliant cultural revival.

The end came through **Utu-khegal, king of Uruk**, whose own vivid inscription
survives: invoking Enlil and Inanna, he marched out with the citizen-levies of
Uruk and Kullab, defeated the Gutian king **Tirigan** (whose reign the king-list
gives as **forty days**), captured him with his family, "set his foot upon his
neck, and restored the kingship of Sumer into its own hand." The deliverance was
hailed, CAH says, as fervently as Egypt's later expulsion of the
[[unknown|Hyksos]].

## Causal Analysis

CAH's account is firmly **political and military**, not climatic:

- **contributed_to:** [[naram-sin|imperial overextension]] — wars across the whole
  arc from Anatolia to Magan that "exhausted his resources and left his successors
  enfeebled."
- **contributed_to:** the unreconciled **Sumerian population**, whose revolt at
  every Agade accession is read as a structural weakness of the
  [[akkadian-empire|empire]].
- **caused_by:** the convergent external assaults — **Amorite** infiltration from
  the northwest and the **Gutian** descent from the Zagros — with **Elam's**
  secession stripping the east.
- Later Mesopotamian tradition gave a **theological** cause instead: in *The Curse
  of Agade*, Enlil looses the Gutians and famine to punish [[naram-sin|Naram-Sin's]]
  sacrilege at Nippur. This providential reading competes with the material one
  (see Historiography and
  [[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism|the collapse controversy]]).

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** the **Sumerian "national" idea.** Two centuries of subjection —
  first to the Akkadians, then to the "execrable" Gutians — "kindled the national
  sentiment into a flame." CAH suggests the [[sumerian-king-list|King List]]
  itself, with its premise of a single unified kingship passing between cities, may
  be a **product of Utu-khegal's day**.
- **enabled:** the **[[third-dynasty-of-ur|Third Dynasty of Ur]]** — Ur-Nammu,
  who supplanted Utu-khegal, channelled the released energy into the great Sumerian
  bureaucratic empire (Ch. XXII).
- **produced:** the **Lagash II flowering** under [[gudea|Gudea]] — the classic age
  of Sumerian sculpture and the Sumerian literary "classic."

## Historiography

A textbook case of competing causal frameworks. The contemporary evidence
(year-names of Shar-kali-sharri, Utu-khegal's inscription, the archaeology of
Ashur) supports a **political-military** collapse; the famous Sumerian poem *The
Curse of Agade* supplies a **moral-providential** one. Modern palaeoclimate
research (Weiss and others, from the abandonment of **Tell Leilan**) has added a
third: the **4.2-kiloyear aridification** as a driver of the Akkadian collapse —
the same climatic horizon implicated in the [[fall-of-old-kingdom-2160bce|fall of
the Egyptian Old Kingdom]] and the [[early-bronze-age-levant|Levantine EB
collapse]]. CAH (1971) predates this thesis and does not entertain it. The Gutian
"dark age" is also a historiographical artefact of hostile Sumerian sources — its
depth and uniformity are probably overstated. See
[[societal-collapse-and-environmental-determinism]] and
[[cambridge-ancient-history-methodology]].
