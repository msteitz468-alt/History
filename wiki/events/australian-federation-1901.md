# Australian Federation (1901)

On **1 January 1901** the six self-governing Australian colonies united as the
**Commonwealth of Australia**, the defining political achievement narrated at the close of
[[bashford-macintyre-cha-v1-2013|CHA Vol. 1]] (Ch 10, "Making the federal Commonwealth").
Federation followed a decade of constitutional conventions, popular referendums, and the
"federal movement" of the 1890s, and marks the transition handled in CHA Vol. 2.

## Historiography

The motives for Federation are contested (detailed in the "From CHA Vol. 1 (Ch 10)" section
below). Four broad explanations compete: a **utilitarian/economic** reading (dominant
1950s–70s; federation served trade and investment, with tariff divergence "the lion in the
path"); a **class-interest thesis** (Manning Clark, Botsman: federation suppressed labour);
a **sentimental/romantic** account (Hirst, Irving: idealism and the "independent Australian
Briton" cannot be reduced to interest); and **White Australia** as a unifying force that
"brought together erstwhile antagonists." Helen Irving's resolution denies a stark
imperial–national conflict — federationists thought of themselves as "both visionary and
practical… Australian and British." See also [[greater-britain]].

## From CHA Vol. 1 (Ch 10 — Helen Irving, "Making the federal Commonwealth, 1890–1901")

### Inauguration

On 1 January 1901, under a white pavilion at Sydney's Centennial Park (built for the
1888 centenary of British colonisation of NSW), the Lord's Prayer was recited and
Queen Victoria's Proclamation read. The first governor-general, the Earl of Hopetoun,
was sworn in by the Chief Justice of NSW; then [[edmund-barton]] and his ministers
were sworn in. "A massed choir broke into the Hallelujah Chorus and children sang
Australian and British patriotic songs." Alfred Deakin — the new Attorney-General,
who was simultaneously the *Morning Post*'s anonymous Australian correspondent —
described "a very self-conscious nation…[that] had just made its appearance in the
centre of the Southern Seas."

### What Kind of Polity?

The Constitution was a British Act (*Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act* 1900,
Royal Assent 9 July 1900) and simultaneously a constitution written by elected
colonial delegates. It established three arms of government (parliament, executive,
judicature), conferred national powers, protected the right to vote of already-
enfranchised women (South Australia, Western Australia), and included provisions
keeping Australia tied to Britain: the monarch remained, the governor-general could
reserve assent on bills, and the Queen retained power to disallow Acts. "No
Commonwealth Acts were ever disallowed." British common law persisted for 40 more
years; full sovereignty came only with the 1926 Balfour Declaration and the 1931
Statute of Westminster.

### Motivations — The Historiographical Debate

Ch 10 surveys competing explanations:
- **Utilitarian/economic** (dominant from 1950s–70s): federation served trading and
  investment interests; colonial differences in tariff policy were the "lion in the
  path." Critics note internal inconsistency: were the economic interests British/
  international (Norris) or regional (Parker) or Depression-recovery-driven (Meredith)?
- **Class-interest thesis** (Manning Clark, Botsman): federation suppressed labour;
  the free trade and commerce clause was a constitutional synecdoche for capitalist
  interests.
- **Sentimental/romantic** (Hirst, Irving): the "sentimental nation" — idealism,
  aspiration, the model of Garibaldi's Italy and the US Constitution — cannot be
  reduced to economic interest. The "independent Australian Briton" (Deakin's
  self-description) held no contradiction between nationalism and imperialism.
- **White Australia** as unifying force: the goal of racial exclusion "brought
  together erstwhile antagonists" and helped "convert" colonial labor parties.

Irving's resolution: "There was no stark conflict between imperialism and nationalism.
Those who imagined an Australian Commonwealth… thought of themselves as both
visionary and practical, socially progressive and economically interested, Australian
and British."

### Steps to Federation

**1880s groundwork.** The Federal Council, suggested by Parkes in 1880, formalised
in 1885 (Victoria, Queensland, Tasmania, Western Australia, Fiji joined; NSW and
NZ did not). Described by Deakin as "unique, like a platypus." The 1887 Colonial
Conference in London secured British naval cooperation and an Australian defence
review. Major-General Edwards' 1889 report found the colonies "incapable of
defending themselves" — prompting Parkes's Tenterfield Oration.

**Federal Conference, Melbourne (February 1890).** Thirteen delegates from seven
colonies; public proceedings; agreed to federation "under the Crown" on a US-style
model; rejected the Canadian model as "too centralist." Agreed the Federal Council
could not accomplish full federation; called for a National Australasian Convention.

**National Australasian Convention, Sydney (2 March – 9 April 1891).** 42
representatives from seven colonies, appointed by colonial parliaments. Key figures:
Barton (NSW), Samuel Griffith (Queensland, later first Chief Justice of the High
Court), Kingston (SA), Deakin (Victoria), John Forrest (WA), Andrew Inglis Clark
(Tasmania). Clark's "Americanist" draft (including an equal-protection clause from
the US Fourteenth Amendment) shaped the document; Kingston's federal arbitration
clause was defeated in 1891 but reappeared in the final Constitution.
The Constitution Bill was set aside after 1891 due to the depression and the
NSW Labor Party's 1891 electoral breakthrough.

**The popular turn — Corowa Plan (1893).** Citizens' Federation Leagues emerged
from 1893, beginning in the Riverina (the district most inconvenienced by colonial
customs barriers). At the **Corowa Conference** (late July 1893), Victorian
parliamentarian John Quick and NSW federationist Robert Garran successfully proposed:
an elected convention → a new Constitution Bill → colonial referendums → imperial
passage. Premier Reid adopted the plan at Hobart (January 1895). The **People's
Federal Convention at Bathurst** (November 1896) built further momentum.

**1897–98 Convention (three sessions: Adelaide, Sydney, Melbourne).** 50 elected
men from five colonies (Queensland unrepresented; NZ abstained). Convention leader:
Barton. Key changes from 1891: senators to be directly elected; Constitution amended
by popular referendum (not State conventions); Kingston's arbitration power restored;
God added to the preamble. First-round referendums (1898) failed in NSW (short of
the 80,000 threshold) but succeeded in Victoria, SA, Tasmania.

**1899 premiers' conference and second referendums.** Concessions to NSW: Senate
powers slightly reduced; House of Representatives strengthened; federal capital to be
in NSW (but not Sydney). New South Wales voted yes in 1899; Queensland joined.
Western Australia (Premier Forrest, resisting) held out under goldrush prosperity.
The other colonies' parliaments adopted addresses to the Queen.

**London (1900).** Barton-led delegation (Barton, Deakin, Kingston, Fysh, Dickson)
negotiated with Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain over the Privy Council's
jurisdiction. Chamberlain had covertly asked Reid in 1897 to promote British amendments
as his own — revealing, in Irving's words, that "Australians…were obstinate and
likely to resent any outside interference in their constitution making." The compromise:
the Privy Council available for criminal/civil appeals and a limited class of
constitutional matters; the High Court conclusive on most constitutional questions;
Commonwealth parliament empowered to end Privy Council appeals when it chose.
Constitution Bill passed imperial parliament 5 July 1900; Royal Assent 9 July 1900.

**Western Australia.** Held its referendum after the imperial bill passed. With
goldrush-era men threatening "Separation for Federation," Forrest at last submitted.
Western Australia voted yes with a "high turnout and resounding 'Yes' vote" —
including women, who were enfranchised in WA from 1899.

**New Zealand** declined after its Royal Commission reported negatively: "too
advanced in race, labour and social relations to risk its own national aspirations"
by merging with Australia. New Zealand's more generous approach to its indigenous
population was noted as a counterfactual variable.

**The 'Hopetoun blunder.'** Governor-General Hopetoun initially nominated NSW Premier
Lyne (an anti-federationist) as first PM. A campaign led by Deakin forced Lyne to
step aside; Barton was appointed.

### The Constitution's Design

- Hybrid: Westminster responsible government + American federalism (rejected
  Canadian model)
- Six colonies became States with preserved powers; listed national powers conferred
  on Commonwealth (defence, customs, immigration, postal, currency, arbitration,
  pensions, marriage/divorce, corporations)
- Senate = equal State representation; directly elected
- Constitutional amendment by double-majority popular referendum
- Racial provisions: "races power" (s.51 xxvi) and immigration power (s.51 xxvii)
  enabled the *Immigration Restriction Act 1901* (White Australia); Aboriginal
  Australians "excluded from reckoning the numbers" (s.127, deleted 1967) and from
  the races power (s.51 xxvi, until 1967)
- Aboriginal affairs constitutionally a matter for the States alone

### National Identity at Inauguration

The ceremony featured trade unions, non-British communities (German, Scandinavian,
Italian, French, American, Chinese), and Aboriginal men performing a "corroboree"
and re-enacting Cook's 1770 landing — "a pantomime of inclusiveness…not insincere.
It was the expression of aspirations: a generous, celebratory moment, before the
reality…inevitably took over." Queen Victoria died three weeks after the inauguration.
