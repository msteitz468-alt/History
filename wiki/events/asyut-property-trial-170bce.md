## Narrative

The **Asyut property trial** is a lawsuit heard in **170 BCE** before the **[[legal-pluralism|laokritai]]**
— the Egyptian priest-judges (here the priests of the wolf-god **Wepwawet**) who administered
**native Egyptian (demotic) law** — in the temple at **Asyut (Siut / Lykopolis)** in Upper
Egypt. Its record, **pSiut 1 (= P. BM inv. 10591, recto)**, part of a small family archive,
is one of the **most detailed surviving demotic trial records** from the ancient world, and
[[sources/manning-last-pharaohs-2010|J. G. Manning]] reproduces his own translation of it as
the **appendix** to *The Last Pharaohs* — the book's showcase of [[ptolemaic-state-formation|the
Ptolemaic legal order]] in operation at the village level.

The dispute is between **Chratianch**, an "endowed woman" and widow of **Tut son of Petetum**,
and her late husband's younger half-brother **Tefhape**, a lector-priest, over the estate of
their father **Petetum** — specifically a one-third share of property and ten *arouras* of
land that Chratianch claims are secured to her and her sons by an **endowment deed** (a
maintenance settlement registered "in the treasury of Ptah"). Each party pleads in writing,
quoting documents and invoking a specific statute — **"the law of year 21"** — on how a man's
property devolves among the children of successive wives. The case also reveals the **rougher
texture of local power**: Chratianch alleges that Tefhape, using a *strategos* and armed
retainers (a herdsman and a camel-keeper), had her husband thrown in jail and forced a
division of the estate "under duress," and that the disputed land had already been leased to
**Heraclides, a cavalryman** (a Greek cleruch) who gave Tefhape "protection."

The court is a hybrid in miniature: the **judges are Egyptian priests** applying Egyptian
law in demotic, but seated with them is **Andromachos, the *eisagogeus*** — a royal officer
who "introduced" cases and represented the crown's oversight of the proceeding. The record
is dated by the regnal year of **Ptolemy (VI Philometor)** and by the dynastic
[[ruler-cult|ruler cult]] titulary of the judges.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by: [[legal-pluralism]]` — the trial exists because Ptolemaic Egypt maintained a
  functioning system of **Egyptian-law courts** alongside Greek ones; Egyptians litigating
  among themselves over Egyptian-law instruments went to the laokritai.
- `contributed_to: [[egyptian-bureaucracy]]` — the case turns on **written documents**
  (endowment deeds, deeds of division) registered with temple treasuries and on a cited
  **written statute**, evidence of a deeply literate, record-keeping legal culture inherited
  from pharaonic and [[saite-egypt|Saïte]] Egypt.
- The immediate cause is mundane: a contested intra-family inheritance complicated by
  successive marriages, half-siblings, and coercive self-help.

## Consequence Analysis

The trial's significance is **evidential and structural** rather than world-historical. It
documents:

1. **The laokritai system in working order** in 170 BCE — Egyptian priest-judges applying a
   cited body of Egyptian law, with the crown present but not displacing them.
2. **The crown's supervisory hand** through the **eisagogeus**, illustrating Manning's
   thesis that the Ptolemaic state **channeled and licensed** plural legal orders rather than
   abolishing them (see [[legal-pluralism]], [[ptolemaic-state-formation]]).
3. **The intersection of the two worlds** — a Greek cavalryman (cleruch) entangled in an
   Egyptian land dispute heard under Egyptian law — concretely showing the
   [[ptolemaic-economy|interpenetration]] of immigrant and native society.

Within a generation, royal policy increasingly shifted such business toward the Greek-law
**chrematistai**, assigning jurisdiction by **the language of the contract** rather than the
parties' ethnicity — a change the Asyut archive helps date.

## Actors

- **Chratianch** — plaintiff; an "endowed woman" suing to protect her sons' inheritance.
- **Tefhape son of Petetum** — defendant; lector-priest and the late husband's half-brother.
- **The laokritai** — three named priest-judges of Wepwawet, applying Egyptian law.
- **Andromachos** — the **eisagogeus**, the royal officer seated with the court.
- **[[ptolemaic-egypt|The Ptolemaic crown]]** — author and guarantor of the legal order
  within which the court operated.

## Historiography

The text's *editio princeps* is **H. Thompson (1934)**; later editions and corrections
include **Seidl (1967)**, **Seidl & Stricker (1937)**, and **den Brinker et al. (2005)**.
Manning stresses that his appendix translation is "rough-and-ready and slightly abridged"
and that the difficult text "requires more scholarly attention" — a candid note on the
**still-young state of demotic legal studies**, which is itself part of his argument about
why the Egyptian-language dimension of the Ptolemaic state was long neglected (see
[[ptolemaic-papyri]]). The trial is a standard reference point in the scholarship on
Ptolemaic [[legal-pluralism|legal pluralism]] (e.g. J. Mélèze Modrzejewski).
