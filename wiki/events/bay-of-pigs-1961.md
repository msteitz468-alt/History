# Bay of Pigs Invasion

## Narrative
On April 17–20, 1961, the CIA launched an invasion of Cuba using roughly 1,500 Cuban exiles organized as "Brigade 2506," landing at the Bay of Pigs under the Zapata Plan (which had replaced an earlier Trinidad Plan). Preliminary CIA air strikes on April 15 — cut from sixteen to eight aircraft at Kennedy's insistence — failed to destroy the Cuban air force, and Kennedy cancelled a second air strike after a botched cover story was publicly exposed. Fidel Castro personally led a counterattack of roughly 25,000 troops backed by Soviet T-34 tanks, crushing the beachhead within three days. Survivors surrendered; roughly 1,200 were later ransomed for $53 million in goods.

The operation's planning inherited personnel (Richard Bissell's team included Guatemala-coup veterans Esterline, Hunt, and Phillips) and the general model from [[guatemala-coup-1954|the 1954 Guatemala coup]]. According to McCoy, CIA leadership (Bissell and Allen Dulles) repeatedly cited a fallback guerrilla-retreat option to skeptics of the plan but excluded it from serious operational planning; the Bay of Pigs terrain — fifty miles of mangrove swamp separating the landing site from the Escambray Mountains, with no roads or boats — made that fallback, in McCoy's words, an "absurdity."

In the invasion's aftermath, the CIA pursued assassination plots against Castro in cooperation with organized crime: in September 1960 a CIA intermediary offered mafiosi Santo Trafficante Jr. and Sam Giancana $150,000 through Johnny Roselli, and the CIA supplied a botulinum poison pill intended for Castro's milkshake, which failed. In November 1961 Kennedy authorized a further $50 million covert campaign, Operation Mongoose, under General Edward Lansdale, combining sabotage raids, psychological warfare, and (per a cited CIA memo) assassination planning; it was disbanded after the October 1962 Cuban Missile Crisis began.

## Causal Analysis
- `caused_by:` CIA planning that directly reused the Guatemala coup's personnel and operational model without adjusting for Cuba's much stronger security apparatus — McCoy frames this as institutional overconfidence stemming from the perceived "success" of [[iran-coup-1953]] and [[guatemala-coup-1954]] (see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]]).
- `contributed_to:` Castro's 1959 nationalization of roughly $968 million in US assets (McCoy's figure), which hardened US hostility.

## Consequence Analysis
- `produced:` the military failure of the invasion and the capture and later ransom of Brigade 2506 survivors.
- `produced:` continued CIA-Mafia assassination plotting against Castro (Giancana was shot dead in Chicago in 1975 days before scheduled Senate testimony; Roselli was found dead in a steel barrel in Miami Bay in 1976; Trafficante survived and later testified to Congress that trying to kill Castro was "a good thing").
- `produced:` Operation Mongoose (Nov 1961–Oct 1962).
- `followed_by:` [[cuban-missile-crisis-1962|the Cuban Missile Crisis]] (October 1962).

## Actors
- **[[central-intelligence-agency|Central Intelligence Agency]]** — planned and executed the invasion under Richard Bissell and Allen Dulles.
- **[[kennedy-john-f|John F. Kennedy]]** — approved a reduced air-strike plan, cancelled the second strike, and later authorized Operation Mongoose.
- Fidel Castro — personally led the Cuban counterattack.

## Historiography
McCoy's account draws on the CIA's own inspector general report and statements from Bissell, Esterline, and Phillips. His central interpretive claim — that the Bay of Pigs failure is substantially attributable to CIA overreliance on the Guatemala precedent rather than to Cuba's specific circumstances — is presented as an institutional-overconfidence thesis; see [[mccoy-covert-empire-thesis]] for the fuller argument and its limits. Source: Alfred W. McCoy, *Cold War on Five Continents* (2024).
