# Marbury v. Madison (1803)

Landmark U.S. Supreme Court case in which Chief Justice John Marshall, writing for a unanimous Court, established the principle of judicial review — the power of federal courts to invalidate laws that conflict with the Constitution. The case arose when William Marbury, appointed a justice of the peace in the final hours of John Adams's presidency, sued to compel newly-installed Secretary of State James Madison to deliver his commission, which the incoming Jefferson administration had withheld.

Marshall's opinion navigated a serious political trap: a direct order to deliver Marbury's commission risked being ignored by the hostile Jefferson administration, which would have exposed the Court's practical powerlessness, while simply denying Marbury's claim outright would have avoided the conflict but ceded no lasting judicial authority. Marshall's solution declared that Marbury indeed had a right to his commission, but that the section of the Judiciary Act of 1789 granting the Supreme Court original jurisdiction to issue the writ of mandamus he sought was itself unconstitutional — thereby denying Marbury his remedy while asserting, for the first time with binding force, the Court's power to strike down an act of Congress.

## Causal Analysis

The case was **caused by** the political conflict between the outgoing Federalist and incoming Democratic-Republican administrations over the "midnight judges" appointments, and **enabled by** Marshall's own position as both a party to the underlying dispute (as Adams's outgoing Secretary of State who had failed to deliver the commissions) and the sitting Chief Justice.

## Consequence Analysis

The ruling **produced** the enduring doctrine of judicial review, which **enabled** the Supreme Court's later role in landmark constitutional interpretation across two centuries of American history, from McCulloch v. Maryland's expansion of federal power to twentieth-century civil rights jurisprudence.

## Historiography

The case is foundational to American constitutional law and extensively studied; historians debate how novel Marshall's assertion of judicial review actually was (English and colonial precedents existed) versus how consequential its specific American articulation proved for the long-term balance of powers. Marshall's political skill in avoiding direct confrontation with Jefferson while securing lasting judicial authority is widely regarded as one of the most consequential strategic moves in American legal history.
