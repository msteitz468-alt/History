The Battle of Ankara (1402) was the decisive military clash between [[timur]] (Tamerlane) and the [[ottoman-empire]] under Sultan Bâyezîd I ("Yldrm," the Thunderbolt). Timur's total victory — he captured Bâyezîd on the battlefield — came close to destroying the early Ottoman state and sent shock waves through the entire Islamicate world, from the Byzantine emperor to the Mamluk sultan.

## Background

By the late fourteenth century, Bâyezîd had consolidated Ottoman power across northwestern Anatolia and Thrace, absorbed most of the independent Anatolian Muslim amîrates (including the proud Ḳaramanl principality), and was besieging Constantinople. The displaced Anatolian amîrs — whose independence Bâyezîd had ended — fled to Timur and invoked his protection.

Timur's attack came in the context of his western campaign (1400–02), which had already sacked Sivas and Damascus. His stated grounds for attacking both the Ottomans and the Mamluks were that they were harbouring his enemies — though Hodgson notes "there were murmurs that the Ottomans were, after all, fighting the Frankish infidels." The logic of Timur's universalist Mongol claim left no room for independent peer rulers: any Muslim state not under his suzerainty was, by definition, in rebellion.

## The Battle

Timur met the main Ottoman army under Bâyezîd at Ankara in 1402. Bâyezîd was defeated and captured — an event without precedent in Ottoman history. Hodgson notes:

> "Bâyezîd had to witness the pillage of Bursa, seat of his ancestors, and of Izmir (Smyrna), which Timur now took from the Latin knights who had seized it, a remnant of the Crusader age."

At Sivas, before the main engagement, Timur had ordered 4,000 Christian (Armenian) soldiers among the captured Ottoman troops buried alive while sparing the Muslim contingent — a characteristic manipulation of religious categories in the service of political terror.

## Consequences

### Short-Term

- The Anatolian amîrates were restored to independence, and the Ottoman state appeared destined to fragment.
- The Byzantine emperor, who had formed a precautionary alliance with Timur before the campaign, sent in his eager submission.
- The Mamluk sultan in Egypt also submitted in awe, though Timur did not proceed to Egypt.
- Bâyezîd died in captivity (1403), precipitating the Ottoman interregnum (1402–13), during which his sons fought for succession.

### Long-Term

The Ottoman state, despite appearances, recovered. The interregnum ended with Mehmed I (r. 1413–21) reunifying Ottoman territory, and the empire subsequently resumed its expansion toward Constantinople (finally taken 1453). The restoration of the Anatolian amîrates under Timur's patronage proved temporary — by the mid-fifteenth century the Ottomans had re-absorbed them.

The battle thus constitutes a major shock to but not a permanent check on Ottoman expansion. Hodgson treats it as a demonstration of Timur's capacity to intervene in the western Islamic world without permanently displacing the institutional structures already in place there.

## Significance

The Battle of Ankara illustrates the paradox at the heart of Timur's career: despite spectacular military success across the Islamicate world, his empire and its political work dissolved rapidly on his death (1405), and most of the structures he disrupted — the Ottomans, the Mamluk sultanate — resumed their trajectories. The [[military-patronage-state]] model he most fully embodied required continuous personal military success to function; without it, the dynastic appanage reverted to fragmented competing successors.
