# The Italian Campaign and the Fall of Fascism (1943–1945)

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]]. Operational battle detail lives in the warfare hub (Husky, Salerno, etc.); this page carries the political collapse and the occupation's crimes.

## The Fall of Mussolini

North Africa ended at Tunis (mid-May 1943, 250,000 Axis POWs; Rommel's own verdict: "the war in North Africa was decided by the weight of Anglo-American material"). Sicily was invaded 10 July 1943. On 24–25 July the Fascist Grand Council — meeting for the first time since 1939 — passed Dino Grandi's motion stripping Mussolini of powers, 19–7 (Grandi carried a live grenade throughout); the King dismissed and arrested him the next day, Badoglio took office, and the Fascist Party dissolved almost without resistance. The armistice of Cassibile (signed 3, announced 8 September 1943) triggered the German seizure of the peninsula and the disarming of the Italian army. Skorzeny's glider raid freed Mussolini (12 September) for the puppet **Salò Republic**, which executed the Grand Council "traitors" including his son-in-law Ciano and "soon degenerated into a morass of violence, corruption and terror."

## German Reprisals and Crimes in Occupied Italy

- **Cephalonia (September 1943):** after the island's Italian garrison resisted for a week, German forces executed more than 6,000 Italian soldiers and sailors, "shooting almost all the Italian officers in batches over a four-hour period of cold-blooded carnage." Of ~650,000 Italian troops seized as "military internees" and sent to the Reich as forced labor, up to 50,000 died — the highest death rate of any Western POWs in German hands (Goebbels: "a Gypsy people gone to pot").
- **The Holocaust in Italy:** Himmler ordered deportation on 12 September 1943. In Rome, SS chief Kappler extorted 50 kg of gold with a false promise of safety; Dannecker's round-up of **16 October 1943** seized 1,259 Roman Jews (200 children under ten) — 15 of the deportees survived Auschwitz. Kesselring, Möllhausen, and Weizsäcker tried to divert the Jews to labor, fearing a public protest from Pius XII "under whose windows" the deportation took place; Hitler overrode them, and the papal protest never came — the Vatican newspaper's vague article was, Weizsäcker noted, unrecognizable as referring to the Jews at all. The Salò regime interned Jews on its own initiative; ~3,800 more went to Auschwitz in 1944. Overall ~80% of Italy's 43,000 Jews survived, "not least thanks to help from ordinary, non-Jewish Italians."
- **The Pontine marshes (1943–45):** German medical officers (Martini, Rodenwaldt — both tied to Himmler's Ahnenerbe) deliberately re-flooded Mussolini's drained marshes with brackish water to reintroduce malaria as biological warfare against the "treacherous" Italians, confiscating quinine stocks: registered cases rose from ~1,200 (1943) to ~55,000 (1944).

## Historiography

Evans frames Italy 1943 as the Axis's political unraveling: "if Mussolini's regime could collapse overnight, then so too could Hitler's" — the fear that drove both the rescue and the German radicalization at home. The failed papal protest of October 1943 is central evidence in the Pius XII debate.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

**Prelude.** At the April 1943 Salzburg summit Hitler told Goebbels he had "succeeded in pushing Mussolini back on the rails"; the Duce arrived looking "like a broken old man" and left "in high fettle, ready for any deed" (Goebbels diary). At their thirteenth conference, Feltre, 19 July 1943 — coincidentally a year to the day before the Rastenburg bomb — Hitler talked for five hours while a heavy daylight Allied air raid hit Rome during the meeting; interpreter Paul Schmidt found the atmosphere "most depressing," and Mussolini asked Schmidt for his own notes because he could no longer follow the discussion.

**The Grand Council vote and arrest (24–25 July 1943), German reaction.** Shirer's account of the vote and the King's dismissal of Mussolini largely corroborates Evans above; per Mussolini's own postwar Memoirs (a self-serving primary source, treated with due caution) the King told him: "My dear Duce, it's no longer any good. Italy has gone to bits... At this moment you are the most hated man in Italy." Goebbels, summoned to Rastenburg on 26 July, recorded in his diary Hitler's order to Himmler for "most severe police measures" in Germany against any imitation of Badoglio's move, and the decision to announce publicly that Mussolini had resigned "for reasons of health." A verbatim transcript of that evening's Hitler–Jodl conference records Hitler's immediate response: "The Duce has resigned... Badoglio, our most bitter enemy, has taken over the government... We'll play the same game while preparing everything to take over the whole crew with one stroke" — including a plan (never executed) to seize the Vatican itself, discussed with startling casualness ("I'll go right into the Vatican... We'll get that bunch of swine out of there"). Four German codenames covered the crisis: *Eiche* (the Mussolini rescue), *Student* (reoccupying Rome), *Schwarz* (military occupation of Italy), and *Achse* (seizing the Italian fleet); *Schwarz* and *Achse* later merged as "Axis."

**Skorzeny's rescue and the Salò Republic.** Otto Skorzeny's glider-borne raid on the Gran Sasso hotel (13 September 1943) freed Mussolini without a shot fired; he reportedly shouted from the window, "Don't shoot, anybody! Don't shed any blood!" Two days after his proclamation of the Italian Social Republic at Hitler's insistence, Shirer records Mussolini's own heart "was not in it" and that "it never amounted to anything." Hitler forced Mussolini to formally cede Trieste, Istria, and South Tyrol to Germany as the price of his restoration, and pressured him into having his son-in-law Ciano arrested and, on 11 January 1944, executed at Verona; all captured Grand Council members who had voted against Mussolini were tried and shot alongside Ciano, including Marshal Emilio de Bono, one of the original 1922 March on Rome quadrumvirate. Ciano's diary and a final letter were smuggled out of his Verona cell by his wife Edda, concealed under her skirt, across the Swiss border.

**[SHIRER-THESIS, flagged not adopted]** Shirer offers his own comparative judgment that Mussolini, "not unintelligent" and "had read widely in history," made the "fatal mistake" of trying to build a martial Great Power on a country lacking the industrial base for it and a people "too civilized, too sophisticated" at heart to embrace fascism — Italians, in his phrase, "merely suffered it." This is Shirer's own national-character interpretive framing, offered here as one dated (1960) position rather than settled historiography; see [[controversies/german-sonderweg]] and [[concepts/theories-of-fascism]] for the comparative debate it touches.
