# The French Conquest of Vietnam (1858–1885)

The piecemeal French subjugation of the Vietnamese state, extending over roughly a quarter-century
in distinct phases, ending the independence of the [[nguyen-dynasty|Nguyen empire]] and producing
the colonial edifice of [[french-indochina|French Indochina]]. K. W. Taylor
([[taylor-history-of-the-vietnamese-2013|*A History of the Vietnamese*]]) narrates it as a process
driven as much by Hue's internal weakness — chronic Le-pretender rebellion in the north, an
alienated literati, an emperor ([[tu-duc|Tu Duc]]) who could not fight the French and the rebels at
once — as by French ambition.

## Narrative

**The Da Nang expedition (1858–1860).** A commission convened by Louis Napoleon in 1857 recommended
intervention on three grounds: naval-strategic standing among the European powers in Asia, commerce
(access to south China and markets for French industry), and protection of missionaries and
Christians. A Franco-Spanish force under Rigault de Genouilly seized Da Nang harbour in August 1858
but could not reach Hue overland; disease, the Italian war of 1859, and the demands of the
simultaneous Anglo-French war in China (the Arrow War) starved the expedition. Rigault de Genouilly
shifted south and took Saigon in February 1859. Nguyen Tri Phuong besieged the small garrison; only
after the China war ended did reinforcements relieve it.

**Cochinchina (1861–1867).** Admiral Charner destroyed Nguyen Tri Phuong's fortress at Ky Hoa (1861);
Bonard extended French control and imposed the **Treaty of Saigon (June 1862)**, by which Tu Duc —
facing a massive Le Duy Minh rebellion in the north — ceded three provinces, ended his anti-Christian
policy, and paid an indemnity. Phan Thanh Gian negotiated for Hue and later, as governor, chose not to
resist the peaceful French seizure of the three remaining Mekong provinces (1867); he committed suicide,
and Tu Duc made him the scapegoat for the loss of the south. La Grandière ("era of the admirals")
consolidated the **Colony of Cochinchina** and imposed a protectorate over Cambodia (Norodom, 1863),
overriding Cambodia's vassalage to Siam.

**Tonkin and the north (1873–1883).** Seeking the Red River route to Yunnan, French officers (the
Garnier affair, 1873) briefly seized the Red River plain; the new Third Republic, reeling from the
Franco-Prussian War, disavowed it, and the **Treaty of 1874** established only a loose protectorate over
Hue. Rising commercial and imperial-competitive pressure in the early 1880s reversed this: French forces
(the Rivière affair) occupied Hue in 1883 and forced the Hue court to accept "protection."

**The Sino-French War and the fall of Hue (1884–1885).** France's assertion of control over Tonkin
collided with Chinese suzerainty, producing the Sino-French War. With its end in spring 1885, the
regent Ton That Thuyet launched a night attack on the French garrison at Hue (July 1885), then fled with
the boy-king **Ham Nghi** to the mountains, issuing a proclamation calling the Vietnamese to rise — the
beginning of the **Can Vuong** ("aid the king") resistance, strongest in the scholar-gentry provinces
of Nghe An, Ha Tinh, and Thanh Hoa. The French installed the compliant Dong Khanh at Hue.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: French strategic-commercial-religious calculation of 1857, endorsed by Louis Napoleon.
- contributed_to: the Hue court's structural weakness — endemic [[le-dynasty-vietnam|Le]]-pretender
  rebellion in the Red River plain, an anti-foreign but militarily hollow literati, and [[tu-duc|Tu Duc]]'s
  inability to concentrate resources against the south while the north burned.
- enabled: French naval technological and logistical superiority (Tu Duc's own six-point analysis of 1858
  conceded enemy superiority in intelligence, communications, equipment, mobility, tactics, and morale).
- concurrent_with: the Arrow War in China, which both diverted and supplied French forces.

The Sino-French War links the conquest to the collapse of the Sinocentric tributary order:
Vietnam's severance from Chinese suzerainty was the diplomatic hinge of its colonization.

## Consequence Analysis

France dismembered the empire into three units — the colony of Cochinchina and the protectorates of
Annam and Tonkin — federated with Cambodia (and, after 1893, Laos) as [[french-indochina|French
Indochina]] under Governor-General Paul Doumer (1897–1902), whose fiscal-administrative regime, Taylor
notes, "would endure with minor adjustments to the end of French rule in 1945" and in some respects
beyond it as a Hanoi-directed strategic bloc. The Can Vuong movement failed but seeded the scholar-gentry
tradition of resistance that fed twentieth-century nationalism.

## Actors

The [[nguyen-dynasty|Nguyen dynasty]] and [[tu-duc|Tu Duc]] on the Vietnamese side; France on the other;
Spain as a junior partner at Da Nang; Qing China as the tributary overlord displaced by the Sino-French
War. See [[french-indochina|French Indochina]] for the colonial state that resulted.

## Historiography

Taylor's account is notable for de-emphasizing a simple imperial-aggression narrative in favour of the
internal fragility of the Hue state, and for tracing how the old "restore the Le" rebellion narrative
fused with a new "European intervention to protect Christians" narrative in the Red River delta on the
eve of conquest. Nationalist historiography stresses instead heroic resistance (Nguyen Tri Phuong, the
Can Vuong); the wiki's fuller treatment of the colonial aftermath is in
[[goscha-vietnam-a-new-history-2016|Goscha]] and the [[french-indochina|French Indochina]] pages. The
casualty figures for Christians killed during the Can Vuong upheaval (Taylor cites published estimates of
40,000 to over 50,000) are uncertain.
