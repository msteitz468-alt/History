# British General Strike of 1926

## Narrative

Britain's coal industry, foundational to nineteenth-century national wealth and still its largest single employer, faced a chronic postwar glut. British mines were increasingly uncompetitive: markets interrupted during World War I, outdated equipment, and fragmented ownership among many small companies. Mine owners concluded that wage cuts were the only path back to world-market competitiveness; miners refused. A government-appointed commission under Herbert Samuel tried and failed to broker a deal trading government-backed modernization for lower wages. Negotiations deadlocked, and the miners struck at the start of May 1926.

Nearly all organized British labor joined the miners in solidarity. Close to 4 million workers were out on the peak day, May 13, 1926. The Trades Union Congress (TUC) general council deliberately avoided the term "general strike" (with its revolutionary syndicalist overtones), preferring "national strike." After nine days, the TUC accepted a compromise — government enforcement of the Samuel Commission's modernization recommendations in exchange for any wage reductions — but the miners themselves held out for six more months before being starved back to work at lower wages.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by**: [[gustav-stresemann|the 1925 return to the gold standard]] at the prewar exchange rate (engineered by Chancellor of the Exchequer Winston Churchill), which overvalued the pound and further undercut already-uncompetitive British coal and textile exports; the mine owners' insistence on wage cuts rather than modernization as the route to competitiveness.

**contributed_to by**: the general glut in world coal markets after World War I; fragmented, undercapitalized mine ownership resistant to consolidation.

**preceded_by**: the Samuel Commission's failed attempt (early 1926) to negotiate a modernization-for-wage-restraint bargain between owners and miners.

## Consequence Analysis

**produced**: a 1927 law outlawing sympathy strikes; a temporary rupture in British-Soviet diplomatic and trade relations (some Conservatives accused Moscow of funding the strikers); strengthened, not weakened, trade-union commitment to collective bargaining within the existing parliamentary and industrial system.

**enabled**: the broader "normalcy" narrative of stable, moderate 1920s British politics — the strike is retrospectively read as an end point of postwar class conflict rather than a beginning of revolutionary confrontation, since neither side invoked soviets or worker management, unlike the Clydeside unrest of 1919.

**did not resolve**: Britain's underlying loss of export competitiveness or its chronically high (10%+) interwar unemployment, which persisted through the "prosperous" late 1920s and left the country poorly positioned for the Great Depression.

## Actors

- Trades Union Congress (TUC) general council
- British coal miners and mine owners
- Stanley Baldwin (Conservative prime minister, 1924–1929) — pursued a policy of wage reduction as the path back to industrial competitiveness
- Winston Churchill — as Chancellor of the Exchequer, both engineered the 1925 gold-standard return and, during the strike itself, predicted (wrongly) that it could "only end in the overthrow of Parliamentary Government or its decisive victory"

## Historiography

Paxton and Hessler frame the strike, in retrospect, as anticlimactic rather than revolutionary: contemporary conservative press invoked "microbes of Bolshevism," but no fatalities occurred and neither side sought to overturn the parliamentary system. The episode is best read within the broader 1920s "normalcy" narrative — see [[interwar-mass-culture]] and the source page [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]] — as evidence that Britain's outward political stability concealed unresolved economic decline.
