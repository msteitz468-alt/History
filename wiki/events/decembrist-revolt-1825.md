# Decembrist Revolt

The **Decembrist Revolt** was an abortive military uprising that took place in St. Petersburg on December 26, 1825 (December 14, Old Style). Staged by reform-minded army officers seeking a constitution and the abolition of serfdom, the revolt took advantage of a succession crisis following the death of Emperor Alexander I. It was quickly crushed by the new emperor, Nicholas I, but became the founding event of the Russian revolutionary tradition.

## Narrative

Following the sudden death of Alexander I in December 1825, a succession crisis arose. Alexander left no children, and the next in line, his brother Constantine, had secretly renounced his rights to the throne years earlier to marry a Polish non-royal. However, this renunciation had not been made public, and Nicholas, the youngest brother, initially swore allegiance to Constantine, while Constantine in Warsaw swore allegiance to Nicholas. During this confusion, reformist officers organized in secret political groups (the Northern and Southern Societies) resolved to act.

On December 26, 1825, approximately three thousand soldiers of the St. Petersburg garrison, led by Decembrist officers, marched to Senate Square. They refused to swear the required oath of allegiance to Nicholas, demanding instead "Constantine and Constitution" (with some soldiers reportedly believing "Constitution" was Constantine's wife). 

Nicholas I spent the day trying to resolve the standoff peacefully, sending the popular Metropolitan and the Governor-General of St. Petersburg (who was shot and mortally wounded by the Decembrist Peter Kakhovsky) to persuade the mutineers. As night fell and the mutinous forces refused to disperse, Nicholas ordered loyal troops and artillery to fire grapeshot into the crowd. The rebel ranks broke and fled, and the uprising was crushed. A separate uprising by the Chernigov Regiment in Ukraine, organized by the Southern Society, was put down a few days later.

Following the revolt, Nicholas personally interrogated many of the conspirators. Five leaders—Pavel Pestel, Kondraty Ryleyev, Sergey Muravyov-Apostol, Mikhail Bestuzhev-Ryumin, and Peter Kakhovsky—were hanged, and over a hundred other officers were stripped of their ranks and exiled to hard labor in Siberia.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **Exposure to Western Liberalism:** Russian officers who participated in the campaigns against Napoleon (1812–1815) had witnessed the social and political progress in western Europe. They returned to Russia deeply disillusioned by the persistence of autocracy and serfdom, which they saw as national embarrassments.
- `caused_by:` **The Succession Crisis of 1825:** The sudden death of Alexander I and the confusion over Constantine's renunciation created a brief vacuum of authority. This succession crisis forced the conspirators to launch their coup prematurely, before their plans and organization were fully mature.
- `contributed_to:` **Autocratic Indecision:** The Unofficial Committee and the constitutional plans of Novosiltsev and Speransky had created a climate of expectation among the educated elite, which was ultimately frustrated by Alexander's turn to reaction, leading officers to seek reform through violent coup rather than imperial decree.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **Repressive Autocracy of Nicholas I:** The revolt traumatized Nicholas I, instilling in him a permanent fear of revolution and a suspicion of social initiative. It led directly to the creation of the **Third Section** (political police) and the ideology of **Official Nationality** to freeze social progress.
- `produced:` **Siberian Exile and Legend:** The exile of the Decembrists (and the decision of many of their wives, the "Decembrist wives," to follow them to Siberia) created a powerful romantic legend of self-sacrifice that inspired future generations.
- `produced:` **Birth of the Russian Revolutionary Tradition:** Although the revolt failed, it was the first time that a group of Russians had actively taken up arms against the autocracy to demand a constitution and social reform. As Herzen later observed, the Decembrists "awakened" the revolutionary consciousness of the Russian intelligentsia.

## Actors

- **The Decembrists:** A group of noble army officers organized into the Northern Society (based in St. Petersburg, favoring a constitutional monarchy and federation) and the Southern Society (based in Ukraine, led by Pavel Pestel, favoring a centralized, radical republic).
- **Nicholas I:** The newly acceded emperor who personally directed the suppression of the revolt, shaping his subsequent thirty-year reign of reaction.
- **Constantine Pavlovich:** Alexander I's brother, whose secret renunciation of the throne triggered the succession crisis.

## Historiography

Nicholas I's official history depicted the Decembrists as a small group of crazed, unpatriotic mutineers with no support in Russian society. By contrast, the nineteenth-century radical intelligentsia, led by Herzen, glorified them as pure-minded martyrs for freedom. Soviet historians placed the Decembrists in the Marxist framework as the first, "gentry" phase of the Russian revolutionary movement, emphasizing their class limitations but celebrating their role in challenging autocracy. Modern scholars, such as Raeff, focus on the Decembrists as products of the Enlightenment and the Napoleonic Wars, analyzing the tension between their Western ideas and the realities of the Russian Empire.
