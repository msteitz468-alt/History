# Second Partition of Poland

## Narrative
The **Second Partition of Poland** took place in January 1793, resulting in a further dismemberment of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth by the Russian Empire and the Kingdom of Prussia (L3454).

Following the First Partition of 1772, Polish reformers enacted sweeping changes, culminating in the **Constitution of May 3, 1791**, which established a hereditary constitutional monarchy, abolished the *liberum veto* in favor of majority rule, and strengthened the central executive (L3444-3450). 

Viewing this reform as a threat to her influence, Catherine the Great instigated conservative Polish magnates to form the **Confederation of Targowica** in May 1792 in defense of the old decentralized order (L3454). On the invitation of the Confederates, Russian troops entered Poland (L3454). Prussia, reversing its previous support of the Polish reformers, joined the invasion (L3454).

By the partition treaty of 1793:
* **Russia:** Annexed more of Lithuania and most of the western Ukraine (including Podolia and Volhynia) with 3,000,000 inhabitants (L3454).
* **Prussia:** Seized Danzig, Thorn, and Great Poland with 1,000,000 inhabitants (L3454).
* **Austria:** Did not participate, being militarily engaged against revolutionary France (L3454). 

Additionally, Russia obtained the legal right to station troops in the remaining rump Polish state and completely control its foreign policy (L3454).

## Causal Analysis
The partition was caused by:
* **Reaction to Reform:** The progressive May Constitution threatened to transform Poland from a weak buffer state into a strong, centralized, and independent nation (L3438, L3444-3450).
* **Prussian Opportunism:** Prussia abandoned its alliance with Poland when it realized it could secure valuable commercial ports (Danzig and Thorn) and territory by cooperating with Russia (L3450-3454).
* **French Revolution Diversion:** The European powers, particularly Austria, were preoccupied with the outbreak of the French Revolutionary Wars, allowing Russia and Prussia to partition Poland without fear of western interference (L3406, L3454).

## Consequence Analysis
The Second Partition produced:
* **Loss of Sovereignty:** Poland was reduced to a small, dependent vassal state under direct Russian military occupation (L3454).
* **National Insurrection:** The humilation of the partition provoked a desperate national uprising in March 1794 led by Thaddeus Kosciuszko, which in turn triggered the Third Partition and the complete destruction of Poland (L3456).
* **Serfdom Extension:** The Russian annexation of western Ukraine legally tied the local peasantry to their Polish and Polonized landlords under the authority of the Russian crown (L3468).

## Historiography
As with the First Partition, this entry rests on a single secondary account rather than primary Polish, Russian, or Prussian archival material; the fullest primary record — the debates of the Four-Year Sejm, the text of the May 3 Constitution, and Targowica's own justificatory documents — is not represented in this collection. Historians disagree over how genuinely reformist the May Constitution was versus how much its threat was exaggerated by Russian and Prussian propaganda seeking pretext for annexation; Polish historiography treats 1791–92 as a tragic near-miss at reform, while some court-centered Russian and Prussian accounts of the period downplay the constitution's substance. The role of the French Revolution as a distraction that enabled the partition (rather than a coincidental parallel) is also debated. This period would benefit from direct engagement with Polish national historiography, largely absent from the collection's holdings.
