# The Closing of the Athenian Academy (529)

The traditional date for the **end of the pagan Neoplatonist school of Athens** — when **[[justinian|Justinian]]**
in **529** forbade pagans to teach philosophy or law, prompting the head of the school, **Damascius**, and
six colleagues (including **Simplicius**) to leave Athens for the **Persian court of [[sasanian-empire|Khusro
I]]**. Long taken as the symbolic "end of antiquity" in thought — the silencing of the thousand-year-old
tradition of Greek philosophy. Treated in [[cameron-cah-v14-2000|CAH XIV]] Ch. 28 (philosophy and the
philosophical schools).

## Neoplatonism and the school of Athens

The dominant pagan philosophy of the fifth and sixth centuries was **Neoplatonism** — Platonism that had
absorbed Aristotle (studied as preparation for Plato), while Epicureanism and Scepticism had faded. In the
Iamblichean tradition, **philosophy and religion fused**: the metaphysics of the **One, Intellect and Soul**
went hand in hand with **theurgy** (ritual magic of the Chaldaean Oracles) and devotion to the old gods. The
Athenian school — heir in name to **Plato's Academy**, though not the original institution — reached its
height under **Proclus** (d. 485), a prodigious teacher and systematizer, and revived again under
**Damascius**, its last head, "as strongly pagan as Proclus."

## Justinian's ban and the migration to Persia

As part of his drive to **enforce orthodoxy** (the same campaign that legislated against pagans, heretics
and Jews), Justinian in **529** prohibited pagans from teaching. By the account of **Agathias**, Damascius,
**Simplicius**, Priscianus Lydus and four others thereupon left for **Persia and the court of Khusro I** —
disillusioned, the story goes, by the realities of the Sasanid realm. **Archaeology** supports a real
rupture: houses on the **Areopagus** plausibly identified as philosophical schools were suddenly abandoned
around 530, pagan sculpture hidden in a sealed well; the "House of Proclus" was given up in the sixth
century.

## The aftermath — and the historiographical debate

CAH XIV stresses how **contested** the event is. The philosophers' Persian stay was short: the **peace
treaty of 532** between Justinian and Khusro explicitly **guaranteed they might return and live
unmolested**. Where they went is disputed:

- **Alan Cameron** argued they returned to **Athens** (the school's endowments, the *diadochika*, are said by
  Olympiodorus to have survived).
- **Michel Tardieu** argued none returned, and that Simplicius and others settled at **Harran** in
  Mesopotamia — citing a tenth-century Platonist school there (in still-pagan Harran) reported by **al-Masʿūdī**
  and topographical allusions in Simplicius's works.

The evidence is inconclusive; what is clear is that **Simplicius's great commentaries** were written after
529, so the tradition did not simply die. Many scholars now resist the dramatic "closing of the schools"
reading: Justinian's edict was real and damaging, but Neoplatonism continued — notably at **Alexandria**
(which survived, increasingly Christianized) — and passed, through commentary, into the Byzantine, Syriac
and Arabic philosophical traditions.

## Significance

The closure is the **symbolic terminus of the open pagan intellectual tradition** of antiquity — the moment
the Christian empire's enforcement of orthodoxy reached even the philosophers' lecture-halls. Yet, like
CAH XIV's larger story, it is less a clean ending than a **transformation**: the content of Greek philosophy
survived its institutional home, carried into the medieval worlds of Byzantium and Islam.

- `contributed_to:` the long **Christianization of education** and the marginalization of overt paganism.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the migration of the last Athenian Neoplatonists to **Persia**, and (by one reading) to
  **Harran** — a conduit for Greek philosophy into the Syriac and Arabic worlds.
- The **survival of Neoplatonism** through Simplicius's commentaries and the Alexandrian school, refuting
  the simplest "end of philosophy" narrative.

## Historiography

The narrative source for the philosophers' departure is **Agathias** (*Histories* ii.30–31),
writing decades later under Justin II — a Christian author generally well-informed but with
no personal sympathy for pagan philosophy, whose brief and somewhat moralizing account is the
only literary testimony to the migration to Persia. Justinian's actual legislation survives
independently in the **Codex Justinianus**, giving unusually solid documentary grounding for
the ban itself, even where its consequences are murkier. As the body of this page discusses,
modern scholars (Alan Cameron against Michel Tardieu) disagree sharply over where the
philosophers ended up and whether the "closing" is better read as a brief, largely symbolic
disruption or a genuine institutional death — a debate that turns partly on archaeological
evidence from the Areopagus houses and partly on how much weight to place on later, thin
testimony like al-Masʿūdī's report of a Harranian Platonist school. The label "closing of the
Academy" is itself now often treated as a later historiographical overstatement, since
Neoplatonism demonstrably continued at Alexandria and through Simplicius's commentaries.
