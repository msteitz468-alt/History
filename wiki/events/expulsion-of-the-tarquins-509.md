## Narrative

According to the Roman annalistic tradition preserved chiefly by Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Rome's seventh and last king, Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, was overthrown around 509 BCE after his son Sextus Tarquinius raped Lucretia, a virtuous noblewoman, who then took her own life rather than live with the dishonor. Her kinsman Lucius Junius Brutus displayed her body publicly to incite outrage, led the Roman aristocracy in expelling the Tarquin royal family, and is credited with establishing the annually elected consulship — two magistrates holding shared, term-limited executive power — as a structural safeguard against any return to one-man rule. The event marks the traditional founding date of the Roman Republic.

## Causal Analysis

The expulsion was `caused_by` the rape of Lucretia and the moral outrage it generated, and was `contributed_to` by longer-building aristocratic resentment of Tarquinius Superbus's high-handed and increasingly autocratic style of kingship, as related in the surviving tradition.

## Consequence Analysis

The event `produced` the overthrow of the Roman monarchy and `enabled` the establishment of the Republican constitution centered on the consulship, along with a durable Roman political culture defined in opposition to *regnum* (kingship) that would later shape reactions to figures perceived as seeking excessive personal power, down to the assassination of Julius Caesar over four centuries later.

## Historiography

Modern historians treat the Lucretia narrative and Brutus's specific role as later literary/moralizing elaboration rather than reliable eyewitness history, given that Livy and Dionysius wrote nearly five centuries after the events; archaeological and epigraphic evidence broadly supports a genuine transition from monarchy to a republican, magistrate-based constitution around this general period, even though the vivid narrative details cannot be verified and likely reflect later Roman political ideology projected backward.
