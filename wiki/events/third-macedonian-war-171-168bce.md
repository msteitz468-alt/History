# The Third Macedonian War (171–168 BCE)

The war that destroyed the Macedonian kingdom at **Pydna** and inaugurated a new,
harsher phase of Roman domination in Greece. Treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH
VIII]] Ch. 9 (Derow).

## Narrative

**[[perseus-of-macedon|Perseus]]**, succeeding Philip V in 179, revived Macedon's
standing in Greece by debt-relief, amnesties, and skilful diplomacy (Polybius'
*ellenokopein*, "to court the Greeks"). This **alternative focus** for Greek
politics — attractive especially to "the many" (the lower classes and the
moderates weary of Roman dictation) — was, Derow argues, **simply incompatible with
Roman supremacy**: "there could not be two arbiters." Fed by **[[pergamon|Eumenes
II]]'s** prepared list of charges (172), Rome declared Perseus a *hostis* and
mobilised.

The early war (171–169) went badly: Roman commanders (Licinius, Lucretius,
Hortensius) achieved little but **rapacity and cruelty** (the sack of Haliartus,
the enslavement of Abdera), eroding Greek goodwill, while Perseus even won a cavalry
action at Callicinus. **Q. Marcius Philippus** (169) forced an entry into Macedon.
In 168 the consul **[[aemilius-paullus|L. Aemilius Paullus]]** took command and
won the decisive **Battle of Pydna** (22 June 168): the Macedonian phalanx, broken
on uneven ground, was annihilated — 20,000 dead. Perseus fled and surrendered at
Samothrace.

## Aftermath: the axe falls

"With that the need for moderation was over." The settlement was deliberately
crushing:
- The **[[antigonid-dynasty|Antigonid kingdom was abolished]]** and Macedon split
  into **four separate "republics"** — barred from intermarriage and cross-border
  landholding, paying Rome half their former royal taxes, the gold/silver mines
  closed. A "province, but not quite" (Derow).
- **Illyria** (King Genthius defeated) was similarly broken into three.
- **Epirus** was devastated: 70 Molossian towns sacked, **150,000 enslaved** on the
  Senate's decree.
- In Achaea, **1,000 leading men** of suspect loyalty — including **[[polybius]]** —
  were **deported to Italy** (held without trial for sixteen years; fewer than 300
  returned in 150); in Aetolia 550 were murdered.
- **Rhodes** was punished (Delos made a free port, draining Rhodian trade); even
  **Eumenes** fell under suspicion.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the **incompatibility** of Perseus' Macedonian revival with Roman
control of Greece (Derow's central thesis) — *not* genuine Macedonian aggression;
the charges (Eumenes', the Delphi assassination plot) were largely manufactured
pretext (see [[nature-of-roman-imperialism]]).

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the end of the Antigonid monarchy and the four republics; the assertion
of Roman power through **deportations, bloodbaths, and fear**, and the entrenchment
of the pro-Roman quislings (Callicrates, Charops).
enabled: the eventual annexation of **Macedonia as a province** (148, after
Andriscus' revolt) and the final crushing of Greek autonomy in the
[[achaean-war-146bce|Achaean War of 146]].

## Historiography

[[polybius]] — himself a deportee and a participant — is the central, deeply
engaged source (with [[livy]] to 167, then epitomes and Pausanias). Derow reads the
war and its aftermath as the moment Roman *imperium* (obedience to Roman orders)
became openly brutal; the class dimension ("the many" favouring Perseus, Rome
backing the wealthy and reliable) is a recurring theme. Pydna's tactical lesson —
the vulnerability of the phalanx on broken ground — became a commonplace.
