## Narrative

The High Treason Incident (大逆事件, *taigyaku jiken*) was the arrest, trial, and execution of
twenty-six Japanese anarchists and socialists accused of plotting to assassinate Emperor Meiji.
The plot was discovered on May 20, 1910, when police raided the rooms of Miyashita Taikichi of
the "Shinshū Band" and found two tin canisters along with bomb-making chemicals. Arrests
cascaded from one anarchist cell to another, the last made on October 18, 1910, ultimately
netting twenty-six defendants including the movement's leading intellectual, Kōtoku Shūsui.

The trial ran from December 10 to December 29, 1910, conducted entirely in secret by the Supreme
Court. The defendants were charged under Article 73 of the criminal code, which prohibited
harming or intending to harm the emperor or imperial family. Verdicts were read on January 18,
1911: twenty-four death sentences and two life-imprisonment sentences. Keene notes that it was
"almost unbelievable" that Emperor Meiji did not learn of the trial until the day verdicts were
announced — courtiers aware that he did not read newspapers had deliberately withheld the
information from him. On learning of the sentences, the emperor directed Prime Minister Katsura
Tarō "to consider an amnesty and a reduction of the sentences." Twelve sentences were accordingly
commuted to life imprisonment; the remaining twelve, including Kōtoku, were executed by hanging
on January 24–25, 1911.

### Kōtoku Shūsui and the Path to Anarchism

Kōtoku Shūsui (1871–1911), journalist, translator, and the movement's spiritual mentor, had
begun his career as a moderate socialist and even a defender of the emperor. His 1901 work
*Imperialism, the Monster of the Twentieth Century* praised Meiji as a ruler who "does not
delight in the barbarous vanity of victory for one country." His trajectory radicalized through
successive shocks: expulsion from Tokyo under public security ordinances, a five-month prison
term in 1905 for publishing a translation of the *Communist Manifesto* in the *Heimin shimbun*,
and a period in San Francisco where contact with Russian anarchist exiles converted him from
parliamentary socialism to direct action. He wrote to an American correspondent that he had
"returned to the outside world as a 'radical anarchist.'"

On returning to Japan, Kōtoku preached the general strike rather than electoral politics,
widening a split in the left between his "hard-line" faction (drawing on Bakunin) and the
"soft-line" parliamentary socialists (following Marx). The Red Flag incident of June 1908 — when
hard-line followers paraded anarchist banners through Tokyo — led to severe police reprisals and
the installation of the more repressive Katsura cabinet.

The actual bomb plot was organized by Miyashita Taikichi, who recruited Kanno Suga, Niimura
Tadao, and Furukawa Rikisaku. On May 17, 1910, the four drew lots to decide roles in an attack
on the emperor's carriage during the November 3 military review. Kōtoku's actual involvement was
contested even at trial: Kanno Suga testified that only four persons were involved, and the
evidence suggested he had not joined the operational conspiracy. He was nonetheless convicted as
having "inspired the others with his anarchist teachings."

  network of anarchist cells that formed in the years after Kōtoku's return from the United
  States in 1906, radicalized by his preaching of direct action.
- `caused_by: [[meiji-repression-of-socialism]]` — State suppression, including the Security
  Police Law (1900), successive bans on socialist publications, and imprisonment of leaders,
  pushed the movement from parliamentary socialism toward anarchism.
- `contributed_to: [[russo-japanese-war-1904]]` — The war era heightened both nationalist
  fervor and socialist-pacifist opposition, sharpening the conflict.
- `preceded_by: [[red-flag-incident-1908]]` — The 1908 crackdown on hard-line anarchists
  accelerated radicalization and intensified police surveillance.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced: [[suppression-of-japanese-socialism-1910s]]` — Keene observes that the
  convictions "satisfied the authorities who were eager to stamp out socialism. It would take
  another decade for the socialists to emerge from the winter of their discontent."
- `enabled: [[death-of-emperor-meiji-1912]]` — The shock of learning that any of his subjects
  had wished to kill him visibly affected Emperor Meiji, noted by contemporaries as one of the
  final blows to his already declining health.
- `concurrent_with: [[annexation-of-korea-1910]]` — The incident unfolded in the same year
  as the formal annexation of Korea, emblematic of the Meiji state's simultaneous imperial
  expansion abroad and domestic repression.

## Actors

- **[[kotoku-shusui]]**: Spiritual mentor of Japanese anarchism; journalist, pacifist, translator
  of the *Communist Manifesto*; executed January 24–25, 1911.
- **[[kanno-suga]]**: The most operationally committed of the conspirators; drew the "lucky
  number" to throw the first bomb; executed.
- **[[miyashita-taikichi]]**: Organizer of the actual bomb-making; the plot's operational core.
- **[[katsura-taro]]**: Prime minister who reported the verdicts to the emperor and later
  implemented the commutations.
- **[[emperor-meiji]]**: The intended victim; reportedly distressed on learning of the trial
  and directed clemency.

## Historiography

The trial was secret and the evidence against most defendants (especially Kōtoku) tenuous.
Keene assesses that "at this distance from the events, one tends to sympathize with the executed
men and woman, who were motivated by ideals, not by a lust for power." Contemporary reaction
was mixed: most of the public believed the sentences justified, while some in the literary world
protested; foreign governments objected. The subsequent "winter of discontent" for Japanese
socialism lasted a decade. The incident is now read by historians as a turning point in Meiji-era
civil liberties and as evidence that the Meiji state's modernization project was deeply
incompatible with dissent — even dissent explicitly framed in loyalist terms by men like Kōtoku
who had formerly praised the emperor.
