# The Fall of Constantinople (1453)

The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople on **29 May 1453**, ending the eleven-centuries-old
[[byzantine-empire|Roman (Byzantine) Empire]]. Treated by Elizabeth Zachariadou (the Ottoman world,
Ch. 32) and Anthony Bryer (the Roman Orthodox world, Ch. 30) in [[allmand-ncmh-v7-1998|NCMH VII]].
The two contributors offer a striking double perspective: a date "made... one to remember" in the
West, yet whose deepest significance, Bryer argues, was for the **Roman Orthodox** themselves.

## Narrative

By 1453 Byzantium was a shadow — a single underpopulated city and a few outliers (the Morea,
Trebizond), the Ottoman state already ruling "far more Orthodox Christians than did the Byzantine
emperor." The young sultan **Mehmed II** surrounded the city in April 1453, encamping with his
janissaries, battering the walls "day and night" with **technically advanced cannon**, and dragging
seventy-two ships overland into the Golden Horn. On **29 May** the city fell; the last emperor,
**Constantine XI Palaiologos**, died in the assault; **Hagia Sophia** became a mosque. Per a sworn
agreement, the soldiers were given the city to **sack** — moveable property and persons to the army,
buildings and land to the sultan; the inhabitants were enslaved or ransomed.

Mehmed at once **repopulated and reorganised** his new capital — inviting back former
Constantinopolitans, deporting merchants and craftsmen (*sürgün*) from other towns, and
**re-establishing the religious communities**: he named the anti-unionist **Gennadios Scholarios**
Orthodox patriarch, a rabbi for the Jews, and a patriarch for the Armenians. The Ottoman Empire
"became the successor of the Byzantine."

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the long Ottoman encirclement of a hollowed-out Byzantium and Mehmed II's **gunpowder
siege train**. `contributed_to:` the **failure of western aid** — the **Union of the Churches**
agreed at the Council of Florence (1439) was "generally rejected" by the Orthodox people (some
preferring, as the saying went, "the sultan's turban to the cardinal's hat"), so no effective crusade
materialised. Byzantine internal weakness — a century of civil war, territorial loss and economic
decay — made the city, in Bryer's words, "far from being a bulwark of the west."

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` (1) the **end of the Byzantine Empire** and the Ottoman absorption of its remaining
fragments (the Morea 1460, Trebizond 1461); (2) the **Orthodox Church under the sultan** — Bryer's
paradox that "it was now possible to have a Church with a sultan," the patriarchate surviving the
empire; (3) **Moscow as "Third Rome"** — Orthodox Russia (Ivan III, who married Constantine XI's
niece) inheriting the imperial mantle of an Orthodoxy that had "rejected Florence"; (4) in the West, a
**spur to Italian [[renaissance-humanism|humanism]]** (Greek scholars and manuscripts flowing to
Italy) and, by raising the value of an alternative spice route, an impetus to the
**[[age-of-discovery|Atlantic voyages]]**. The event is the Mediterranean counterpart of the
**[[fall-of-granada-1492|fall of Granada]]** (1492): the two faiths' frontiers moving in opposite
directions within forty years.

## Historiography

Zachariadou sets the conquest within the **war-party vs. moderate** struggle in the Ottoman state:
after 1453 Mehmed executed the moderate vizier Halil Çandarlı and "the war party, with the sultan at
its head, assumed all power," opening a generation of relentless **holy war** and conquest. Bryer
deflates the western myth of 1453 as a cosmic rupture, stressing continuity for the Roman Orthodox
under a new *taxis* (order) — though he acknowledges the date's enduring symbolic weight.

## Mehmed the Conqueror and the new Rome (Kinross)

Lord Kinross's [[kinross-ottoman-centuries|*The Ottoman Centuries*]] frames the conquest as the
making of an empire: from his accession **[[mehmed-ii|Mehmed II]]** "had seen himself as the
heir to the classical Roman Empire and its Christian successor," and the capture of the city
confirmed him as **Kaisar-i-Rûm** ("Caesar of Rome," successor to Augustus and Constantine).
He rebuilt the ruined capital as **Istanbul**, repopulated it, and — preserving Hagia Sophia's
mosaics and re-establishing the **Greek Orthodox Patriarchate** under his protection — turned a
conquest into an imperial and multi-confessional order. The fuller portrait is on
[[mehmed-ii]].
