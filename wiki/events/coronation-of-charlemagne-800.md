# The Imperial Coronation of Charlemagne (800)

## Narrative

On **Christmas Day, 25 December 800**, in Old St Peter's Basilica in Rome, **Pope Leo III**
crowned **[[charlemagne]]** *Imperator* — reviving the western imperial title that had lapsed
with the deposition of the last western Roman emperor in 476. The act came in the context of
Charlemagne's intervention to restore Leo III, who had fled north to the Frankish court after
being attacked and deposed by a Roman faction in 799. It set the seal on Charlemagne's
unprecedented stature as ruler of many peoples and leader of Latin Christendom.

## Causal Analysis

NCMH II (Fouracre, Ch. 3) treats the coronation as requiring "little historical imagination":
by 800 Charlemagne already ruled an empire and had assumed the trappings of Roman emperorship —
the new palace at **Aachen** (Roman in inspiration), the commissioning of orthodoxy on images
(the *Libri Carolini*), the condemnation of Adoptionism. A title was acquired to match the reality.

- `caused_by:` [[carolingian-empire|Charlemagne's rule over a Roman-scale, multi-ethnic realm]]
- `contributed_to:` his role as protector of the papacy (the restoration of Leo III in 799–800)
- `enabled:` the **vacancy of a legitimate male emperor in Byzantium** (the empress Irene then ruling),
  which the Lorsch annalist cites as justification

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the revival of the **western imperial title**, later the institutional germ of the
  **Holy Roman Empire**
- `produced:` the **"two emperors" problem** — a standing rivalry and diplomatic friction with
  [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]], which only grudgingly recognised Charlemagne as *western* emperor in 812
- `enabled:` the ideology of a renewed Roman-Christian universal rule in the Latin West

## The Significance Debate

NCMH II is pointed about over-reading the event. Charlemagne plainly *arranged* the coronation —
Einhard's claim that he was reluctant is "the age-old literary convention of humility in the great and
good." But Charlemagne "then did not seem to attach anything like as much significance to the title as
later rulers and commentators would do": the imperial title was omitted from the 806 division plan for
his realm, and the great reforming capitulary of 802 contains "little which cannot be found in earlier
legislation." The disproportion between the symbolic weight later ages assigned to 800 and the modest
practical change it produced is the heart of the historiographical puzzle.

## Nelson's Reading (2019)

[[sources/nelson-king-and-emperor-2019|Janet Nelson]] is categorical on the planning question:
"there is plenty of evidence that **Charles and [[pope-leo-iii|Leo]] between them … carefully
planned and stage-managed the sequence of rituals. Any argument to the contrary simply can't be
sustained**." [[einhard|Einhard]]'s claim that Charles "would never have entered the church that
day … if he had known the pope's plan" is a topos of humility — though Nelson allows residual
unease over the *order* (coronation before acclamation), which "did not accord with 'Roman'
(Byzantine) tradition." She reads the choreography as deliberate: Leo walked out to meet Charles
at Mentana (c. 20 km from Rome), "doubling" the customary distance to signal "the new emperor was
to be in a different league"; the return of Zacharias from Jerusalem "with the keys and the
banner" was "timed to a tee" for 23 December. The *Vita Leonis* uniquely records that Leo also
anointed Charles's son Charles the Younger as king the same day. On the title, Nelson stresses the
"significant evasion" of ***Romanum gubernans imperium*** ("governing the Roman empire") — "a
substitute for 'emperor of the Romans', a title Charles never used."

## Historiography

Sources — Einhard's *Vita Karoli*, the royal annals, the Lorsch annals — are court-centred and
retrospective. Debate has long centred on the *sequence* of events at the ceremony (did Leo III
crown Charlemagne before he expected it?) and on the *meaning* of the title to Charlemagne himself.
Folz's *The Coronation of Charlemagne* remains the classic full discussion; Nelson (2019) is the
current revisionist statement.
