## Narrative

The Chremonidean War (268–261 BCE) was the last coordinated attempt by the two
traditional leaders of Greece, **[[athens|Athens]] and [[sparta|Sparta]]**, backed by
[[ptolemaic-egypt|Ptolemy II]], to throw off the hegemony of
**[[antigonus-ii-gonatas|Antigonus Gonatas]]**. It takes its name from the Athenian decree
of **Chremonides** (archonship of Peithidemus, 268/7) that proclaimed the alliance and
accused Antigonus of treaty-violation — a document, Walbank notes, "redolent of panhellenic
sentiment," consciously recalling the united struggle of Athens and Sparta against the
Persians and the [[lamian-war-323|Lamian War]] against Macedon.

The coalition gathered Athens, Sparta (under King **Areus I**), the
[[achaean-league|Achaean League]], Elis, and Arcadian and Cretan cities, with Ptolemaic
backing. But it failed for want of coordination: Antigonus' grip on **Corinth and the
Isthmus** kept the Spartan and Athenian armies apart, Areus' attempts to force the Isthmus
were repulsed (he was killed c. 265), and **Ptolemy's help was half-hearted** — his admiral
Patroclus operated from the islands and Crete but brought no real expeditionary force to
Attica. After several years of siege (Antigonus even employing pirates against Attica),
**Athens capitulated, c. 262/1**, receiving a Macedonian garrison on the Museum hill. The
war was probably sealed by Antigonus' naval victory over the Ptolemaic fleet at **Cos**
(c. 261).

## Causal Analysis

Walbank weighs the motives. The least convincing explanations are economic (a threatened
corn-market); the most likely is that **Ptolemy feared an Antigonid naval revival** that
would threaten the ring of Aegean outposts protecting Egypt — all the more dangerous given
the new friendship between Macedon and the [[seleucid-empire|Seleucids]]. At Athens the
driving hope was the **recovery of the Piraeus**; at Sparta, Areus' ambition for
**Peloponnesian hegemony**. (Habicht argues the main impetus came from Athens itself.)

## Consequence Analysis

- **The end of Athenian independence**: a Macedonian garrison, curtailment of the assembly,
  suspended coinage, and economic decline; the death of an intellectual era (Zeno of
  Citium died around the war's end; the local historian Philochorus was executed). Nominal
  "freedom" was restored in 256/5 but Macedonian control was unrelaxed.
- **Consolidation of Antigonid Macedon** as master of southern Greece — though the rise of
  the [[aetolian-league|Aetolians]] in the north and, later, [[aratus-of-sicyon|Aratus']]
  Achaean League would erode it.
- **Cos**: a severe blow to Ptolemaic sea-power, though (Walbank cautions) the evidence
  does not prove Ptolemy was wholly "ousted from his general control of the Aegean."
- **Sparta's eclipse**: Areus' failure exposed the deep weakness that would erupt in the
  reforms of [[agis-iv|Agis IV]].

## Historiography

No connected ancient narrative survives; the war is reconstructed from inscriptions (the
Chremonides decree; honours to the general Epichares at Rhamnus) and scattered notices in
Pausanias and Plutarch — a textbook case of [[hellenistic-historiography|the documentary
reconstruction]] the period demands.
