# Chola Invasion of Srivijaya (1025)

In **1025 CE**, the South Indian **Chola** emperor **Rajendra I** launched a naval expedition against ports of the [[srivijaya|Srivijayan]] mandala—including Sumatran centres and **Kedah (Kadaram/Kaṭāha)** at the north end of the Straits of Melaka. The Tanjor (Thanjavur) inscription of **1030** is the principal narrative source. Primary synthesis: [[miksic-goh-ancient-southeast-asia-2017|Miksic & Goh 2017]] Middle Classic chapter.

## Narrative

According to the Tanjor inscription of 1030, Rajendra Chola captured **Saṅgrāmavijayottuṅgavarman**, king of Kadaram; seized his great war gate; and confiscated his riches. The text “gives the impression that the fleet went from port to port, defeating local garrisons, appropriating treasure, and taking the king prisoner.” The inscription lists (in no strict order):

| Region | Places named |
|---|---|
| Sumatra | Srivijaya (perhaps Palembang), Panai (northeast coast), [[malayu|Malayu]], Lamuri (Aceh) |
| Mainland | Takkola, Langkasuka, Kadaram (Kedah) |

Kadaram is mentioned twice. The king was captured and taken to India. The inscription **does not give any reason** for the invasion.

Rulers around the attacks: **Māravijayottuṅgavarman** (1008–c.1025) and Saṅgrāmavijayottuṅgavarman. The **Tiruvalanggadu** inscription of **1017–1018** calls Rajendra “victor of Kaṭāha.” R.C. Majumdar concluded Kedah was attacked unsuccessfully in 1017–18 and that only **1025** achieved victory—so a failed earlier attempt may have **preceded** the successful campaign (temporal sequence with possible causal learning, not proven causation of 1025 by 1017 alone).

Miksic and Goh interpret the strategic objective as occupation of the **north end of the Straits**; the south end seems left to its own devices. Chinese observers “would not necessarily have noticed any major change.”

## Causal Analysis

Distinguish carefully:

- **preceded_by**: Chola maritime expansion and prestige warfare under Rajendra I; first Chola China mission **1015**; possible unsuccessful 1017–18 Kataha attack; long Srivijayan control of Straits tolls.
- **contributed_to / hypothesized caused_by** (not proven sole causes):
  1. Srivijaya may have tried to **prevent Cholas from direct access to China** (no Chola follow-up China mission after 1025 in this reading).
  2. **Indian merchant guilds** may have been eager to monopolize trade at the northern Straits.
- **concurrent_with**: Middle Classic reordering of [[angkor|Angkor]] and east Java; Song Chinese trade growth.
- Chinese ceramics in south India are mainly thirteenth–sixteenth century—weak ceramic proof of intense earlier Chola–China bulk trade, which complicates simple “blocked China route” models.

No single cause is asserted in the Chola eulogy itself; commercial-rivalry explanations are modern reconstructions.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced**: capture of the Kadaram king; shock to Srivijayan prestige; listed devastation of named ports.
- **enabled / contributed_to**: **Jambi–[[malayu|Malayu]] supersession of Palembang** as overlord of the east Sumatran mandala after 1025; Sanfoqi mission to China in **1028** (possibly now Malayu) shows rapid diplomatic reappearance; no attempt by Cholas to stop Malayu reviving.
- **produced** (medium term): Tamil colonization/presence at the **north end of the Straits lasting about a century**. Visible traces include Kedah Śiva sanctuaries (especially **Candi Bukit Batu Pahat**) and the **Barus inscription of 1088**. Strong South Indian influence appears on **Sumatran** sculpture in this period, **none in Java**.
- Lambri/Lamuri is remembered as one of the Srivijaya ports conquered; Tamil trade guilds remained active in the Aceh region into the twelfth–thirteenth centuries (Neusu inscription similar to Quanzhou 1281; ~1200 Vishakhapatnam Añjuvannam guild merchant from Pasai; 1204 Nagapattinam gift).
- **Perumber inscription (1069–70)**: Vīrarājendradeva conquered Kadaram then “was pleased to give it back to its king who worshipped his feet”—plausibly a revolt against a Chola-installed ruler, quelled and restored under suzerainty.
- **Kulottuṅga / Divākara puzzle**: Guangzhou 1957 inscription says Divākara had been king of Srivijaya since **1064–67**, donated **500,000** gold cash for a Daoist temple reconstruction completed **1079**; in **1070** he became Chola king (Chinese Di-hua-jia-luo). Chinese still saw him as Sanfoqi ruler after he became Chola king. Miksic and Goh prefer the simple explanation: Kulottuṅga was a Tamil noble/viceroy in Kedah who later became Chola king; Chinese mistook this for Kedah subjugating the Cholas. Wolters denied identity, treating the name similarity as coincidence.

## Actors

- **[[chola-empire|Chola]] dynasty** under Rajendra I (and later Vīrarājendra, Kulottuṅga in aftermath politics).
- **[[srivijaya|Srivijaya]] / Sanfoqi** rulers Māravijayottuṅgavarman and Saṅgrāmavijayottuṅgavarman.
- **Kedah (Kadaram)** as named royal seat and north-Straits hinge.
- **[[malayu|Malayu]]–Jambi** as beneficiary of post-raid reordering.
- Tamil merchant guilds as hypothesized commercial stakeholders.

## Historiography

The event is known almost entirely from **Indian eulogistic inscriptions**; Southeast Asian textual response is thin. Archaeology of post-1030 Palembang, Kedah Śaiva temples, Barus, and Sumatran sculpture tests the scale and duration of disruption. Debates:

1. Was 1017–18 a real failed attack (Majumdar) or rhetorical foreshadowing?
2. Commercial blockade vs prestige raid vs guild monopoly as motive.
3. Duration and nature of “Tamil colonization” (~one century of north-Straits influence).
4. Kulottuṅga–Divākara identity (Miksic & Goh vs Wolters).
5. How far 1025 “destroyed” Srivijaya versus reallocating overlordship within a resilient mandala (Jambi rise; 1028 mission).

Wolters’s broader thesis that Srivijaya did not recover after 1025 is flagged elsewhere on the wiki as in tension with continued Sanfoqi prosperity rankings and Jambi’s Middle Classic florescence—see [[srivijaya]] and [[malayu]]. Source base for this page: Miksic & Goh 2017.
