# Thermopylae and Artemisium (480 BCE)

In **September 480 BCE** the Greek League made its first stand against [[xerxes-i|Xerxes']]
invasion at the linked positions of **Thermopylae** (the coastal pass) and **Artemisium**
(the straits off north Euboea) — a combined land-and-sea operation that ended in tactical
defeat but bought time, drew Persian blood, and gave Greece its most enduring symbol of
disciplined courage. [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 10, N. G. L. Hammond) treats
them as one interdependent action.

## Strategy

The League chose the **Thermopylae–Artemisium line** because the two were close enough to
support each other: so long as the fleet held the entrance to the Oreus Channel, the Persian
navy could not land troops behind the pass; so long as the army held the pass, the Persian
army could not threaten the fleet's line of retreat at the Euripus. The defence was
under-resourced because the muster fell in the **Carneian and Olympic festival truces** —
hence King **Leonidas** brought only his personal guard of **300 Spartiates** (men with
living sons), with Helots, perioikoi, and contingents that brought the force to ~5,000–7,000.

## Artemisium (the sea)

The Persian fleet was first shattered by a **three-day north-easterly gale** off the
Magnesian coast (the Greeks crediting Poseidon and the winds, per Delphi's advice), and a
**detachment of 200 ships sent to round Euboea** was wholly wrecked on "The Hollows." In
three engagements the Greek fleet under **Eurybiades** (with **Themistocles**) tested and
honed its **ramming tactics** against the larger Persian fleet, fighting it to a costly draw
— "where Athens' sons laid the bright foundation of Liberty" (Pindar).

## Thermopylae (the land)

For two days Leonidas held the narrow pass, the Greeks' **longer spears, heavier armour and
drill** inflicting far more casualties than they took, even on the Persian **Immortals**. On
the third day the Malian **Ephialtes** betrayed the mountain **Anopaea path**; the 1,000
Phocians guarding it were driven off, and Hydarnes' Immortals descended in the Greek rear.
Leonidas dismissed most of the allies but **stayed with his 300 Spartiates, 700 Thespians
and the Thebans**, dying in a last stand on a hillock — overwhelmed by missiles after their
spears broke. Xerxes, breaking his usual practice of honouring brave dead, had Leonidas'
head impaled (revenge for Sparta's killing of his envoys).

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: [[xerxes-i|Xerxes']] invasion and the League's forward-defence decision.
contributed_to: the defeat was made possible by the **turning-path** (Ephialtes) — the same
intelligence failure that had wrecked the earlier Tempe position; and by the reduced force
the festival truces permitted.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **forcing of the pass** and the Persian advance into Phocis, Boeotia and
Attica (the **sack of Athens**); the withdrawal of the Greek fleet from Artemisium.
enabled: the **naval experience** — three days of ramming-combat — that proved decisive at
**[[battle-of-salamis-480bce|Salamis]]** weeks later; and the moral example ("Tell them in
Lacedaemon, passer-by: obedient to their orders, here we lie") that **stiffened Greek will**.
Persian losses included the cream of the infantry and two sons of Darius.

## Historiography

Built on **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]** (Books VII–VIII), with **Aeschylus' Persae**
and **Simonides'** epitaphs. Hammond defends the narrative and sequence as reliable
contemporary memory, while treating Herodotus' Persian numbers as vast exaggerations; the
chronology of the storm, the engagements, and the Anopaea march are the technical disputes.

### Osborne (2000)

[[sources/osborne-classical-greece-2000|Osborne (ed., 2000)]] (Ch. 7, Kallet) gives the
concise narrative frame: the pass was held ultimately by the small force of **300
Spartans under King Leonidas**, and the Persians broke through on land only "by an act of
treachery" — the Anopaea path betrayal. The concurrent naval battle off **Artemisium was
indecisive**. Osborne highlights Herodotus's story of the exiled Spartan king **Demaratus**
telling Xerxes that the Spartans would fight at Thermopylae because, though not wholly
free, "they fear [the law] much more than your men fear you" (Hdt. 7.104) — nomos as the
Spartans' true master.

### Hall (2014)

[[sources/hall-history-archaic-greek-world-2014|Hall (2014)]]: Hellenic League of 481 under Spartan leadership; 480 strategy after Tempe abandoned — ~7,000 infantry at Thermopylae and 271 triremes at Artemisium. Hall stresses Thermopylae was a **total defeat** despite Spartan death-and-glory propaganda: after the Anopaia path betrayal, Leonidas kept 300 Spartans, suspect Thebans, and volunteer Thespians; yet delay, Persian casualties, and Artemisium storm losses reduced the force that then sacked Boeotia/Attica/Athens.
