## Narrative

During the construction of the [[feathered-serpent-pyramid]] (FSP) in the Miccaotli ceramic phase (ca. 100–170 CE), approximately 200 individuals were sacrificed and interred in a series of elaborately organized mass graves within and around the pyramid. The burial program was detected by Cabrera's 1980–82 project, which found 18 individuals in an elongated east-west pit along the pyramid's south edge plus single burials at either end. The Cabrera-Sugiyama-Cowgill project of 1988–89 found 119 more individuals, bringing the total to 137; further finds in 1992–94 raised it higher. Symmetry considerations across the unexcavated portions of the pyramid "imply that a total of around 200 victims had been buried at ground level during construction of the pyramid" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

The burials are not randomly distributed. They fall into a "highly structured pattern of mass graves" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6) organized by status level, numerical regularity, and spatial position relative to the pyramid's center and cardinal axes. The numbers 8, 9, 18 (= 2 × 9), and possibly 20 recur repeatedly. Nine has wide significance in Mesoamerica as an underworld number; sets of 9 appear also in the nine pairs of large obsidian figures in Tomb 6 at the [[moon-pyramid]].

## Victim Profiles

**Physical condition and binding**: "Most or all had their hands tied behind their backs, and many seem also to have been bound at the ankles. They were in good physical condition at the time of their deaths and it is possible they were buried alive" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Origins**: Stable isotope analyses of bones and teeth (White et al. 2002, cited in Cowgill 2015) suggest "that many had not lived for long in the Teotihuacan region." This has been interpreted as evidence of foreign captives, but Cowgill doubts this interpretation: captured enemies in Mesoamerica were often stripped, while the FSP victims are elaborately and consistently dressed; the uniformity of attire within groups argues against diverse foreign origins (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Military vs. foreign origins**: The predominance of imitation maxilla collars over real human trophy jaws "suggests that possibly there had not been much recent warfare to provide real victims" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Musculoskeletal stress marker analysis (Rubel 2009, cited in Cowgill 2015) found only limited evidence for atlatl use or shield practice, but unexpectedly some evidence for bows — absent from Teotihuacan imagery.

Cowgill's preferred interpretation: "the victims in military attire were members of an elite guard attached to the household of whoever was at the apex of the Ciudadela hierarchy." He compares them to Varangian guardsmen of Byzantine emperors, Ottoman janissaries, and Vatican Swiss Guards — all cases of foreigners valued for their lack of local loyalties (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Status Levels

The graves reveal a clear hierarchy of at least four, possibly five, status levels:

**Level 1** (Graves 10, 11, 16, 17): Sets of 8 female victims in their early teens, positioned farthest from the pyramid, with minimal offerings — shell beads, disks, earspool components, large and small obsidian projectile points but no ceramics. The presence of projectile points with young women is noted as "interesting" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Level 2** (Graves 190, 2, 4, 5, 6, 2043): Elongated pits at ground level, some just outside the pyramid and others inside, containing sets of 9 or 18 males generally in their early twenties. Offerings include abundant obsidian projectile points — bimodally distributed: smaller (2–4 cm, probably dart points) and larger (6–10 cm, probably spear points) — and slate disks on the lower back serving as backings for pyrite mirrors (tezcacuitlapilli), as depicted in Teotihuacan imagery of soldiers. The most striking attire is the shell-maxilla collar: plaques from which suspended imitation human upper jaws (maxillae) made of individual teeth carved from white marine shells. A few wore real human maxillae; some real and imitation canid and felid maxillae were also present. The same collar appears in Moon Pyramid Tombs 2 and 6. "The relative uniformity of attire of the level 2 victims resembles the uniformity of dress of elaborately costumed figures in Teotihuacan processional murals" — evidence of the city's "ethos of uniformity" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

The only known parallels for maxilla collars outside Teotihuacan come from Oaxaca (Cerro Tilcajete and Epiclassic ceramic figures), suggesting the practice may have lasted longer in Oaxaca than at Teotihuacan.

**Level 3** (Grave 14, singles at Graves 1, 153, 172, 203, disturbed remains atop pyramid): The twenty individuals in Grave 14 at the pyramid's exact center were laid directly on the ground and accompanied by numerous greenstone ornaments (beads, earspools, rattlesnake-rattle nose pendants), huge quantities of marine shell, small stone and obsidian figurines, obsidian serpent figures, bifacial obsidian points, and "hundreds of exceptionally fine pressure-flaked obsidian prismatic blades" showing no signs of use — "clearly as offerings." "Bags with rather standardized combinations of objects were strewn over the bodies." No trophy jaws; no ceramic figurines; only two black Storm God jars (of a type distinct from those in the Moon Pyramid) and a few coarse ware bowls. "Grave 14 seems roughly comparable in richness to the tombs at the Moon Pyramid" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

**Level 4** (Grave 13, largely looted): A large pit (~4.2 × 3.3 m) on the east-west center line, west of Grave 14, was "almost totally emptied by ancient looters who tunneled into the pyramid from its southeast corner." One undisturbed individual remained — "an unusually robust male" with exceptionally large greenstone earspools and a Storm God nose pendant of a distinctive type (rectangular crossbar, three incised circles, split tongue). Cowgill notes this Storm God pendant type (signs 150 and 153 in Langley's catalog) has been incorrectly claimed as a "Great Goddess" diagnostic; its appearance on a robust male argues against that attribution (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). Other finds in looter backdirt include fine serving vessels and a wooden feathered serpent staff.

**Level 5 (possible)**: Grave 13 and a completely looted pit just beyond the FSP stairway "may have held heads of the state" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6).

## Causal Analysis

contributed_to: `[[teotihuacan-formation]]` — The burial program coincided with the construction of the Ciudadela and the FSP, themselves part of the vast Miccaotli phase civic-ceremonial building program. The sacrifices were embedded in and enabled by the political mobilization of labor and coercive capacity that built the Ciudadela.

preceded_by: Dedicatory burial programs at the Moon Pyramid (Tombs 2 and 6 in Stage Four), which share common elements (obsidian feathered serpent figures, Storm God jars, shell maxilla collars) and suggest a common ritual tradition slightly predating the FSP program.

The event demonstrates that Teotihuacan was "not egalitarian, even in theory" in its early flowering: "hierarchy was already publicly symbolized at Teotihuacan by marked differences in costume, at least among the military and other office holders" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6). "In openly recognizing status differences, it resembled other early states in Mesoamerica and elsewhere. What was unusual about Teotihuacan was the de-emphasis on individuality, at least at intermediate status levels."

## Consequence Analysis

produced: Evidence for militarism and hierarchical political organization in the Miccaotli phase of Teotihuacan, predating what was formerly thought to be "late" military symbolism.

enabled: Interpretive frameworks linking the FSP and the Ciudadela to specific political institutions (whether priestly, royal, or both).

The scale and organization of the burial program is unprecedented in the Mesoamerican record to this date and implies substantial coercive capacity in the Teotihuacan state, organizational sophistication in managing large sacrificial rituals, and a symbolic system in which numerical regularities (8, 9, 18, 20) structured the cosmic legitimation of political power.

## Actors

No identifiable individual actors are recoverable from the FSP burial program. No individual likely to have been a Teotihuacan ruler appears in any of the systematically excavated burials. The "persons whose activities intertwined the sacred and the secular" (Cowgill 2015, Ch. 6) who organized the program are unknown. The victims themselves represent at minimum two sexes, multiple age groups, and possibly multiple geographic origins.

## Historiography

The FSP burial program has generated substantial interpretive debate. Sugiyama (2005) argues the victims were dedicated to gods — a cosmological act — rather than to rulers. Cowgill (2015) does not dispute the cosmological dimension but emphasizes the political implications: the structured hierarchy of victims, the military attire, and the probable elite-guard identity of the Level 2 victims all point to a political context. The "ancient looters" of Grave 13 who tunneled into the FSP from its southeast corner, apparently in antiquity, may themselves reflect a political event — possibly desecration of the FSP by a subsequent regime — though this connection is speculative.

Stable isotope evidence for geographic origins of victims (White et al. 2002) remains contested. The local Teotihuacan "signature" is based on a small sample and may not adequately represent the full range of local variation (Cowgill 2015, Box 6.1). Whether victims were foreign captives, high-status local guardsmen, low-status individuals costumed as soldiers, or some combination is unresolved.

The FSP sacrifices are compared most directly to the Moon Pyramid tomb series, with which they share the shell maxilla collar, obsidian feathered serpent forms, Storm God jars, and the broader Feathered Serpent/Storm God symbolic frame. The presence of both programs at the same broad horizon (Miccaotli / Early Tlamimilolpa) suggests a city-wide ritual tradition rather than isolated cult practices.
