# The Concordat of Worms (1122)

The **Concordat of Worms** (23 September 1122) was the compromise that ended the
[[investiture-contest|Investiture Contest]] between the reformed papacy and the German empire —
the half-century struggle, opened by the [[gregorian-reform|Gregorian reform]], over who invested
bishops and abbots with their office. Agreed between **Pope Calixtus II** and **Emperor Henry V**,
it is one of the foundational settlements of medieval Europe, drawing a durable line between
spiritual authority and secular power. From the [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|New Cambridge Medieval
History, Vol. IV]].

## Narrative

After fifty years of conflict — excommunications, the humiliation at Canossa (1077), civil war in
Germany, and rival popes — both sides were exhausted. The settlement owed less to the hard line of
the Gregorian canonists (Placidus, Deusdedit) than to the moderate **"théorie chartraine"** of
**Ivo of Chartres**, which distinguished the bishop's *spiritual* office from the *temporal*
properties and rights (the **regalia**) attached to it.

By the terms of the Concordat:
- The **emperor renounced investiture with ring and staff** — the spiritual symbols — abandoning
  the claim that had defined imperial control of the church.
- In return, the pope conceded that **elections of German bishops and abbots should take place in
  the emperor's presence** ("without simony or any violence"), and that the emperor could confer
  the **regalia** on the elect with a **touch of the sceptre** — a *secular* ceremony of
  investiture replacing the ecclesiastical one.
- The elect would **perform what he owed** the emperor in respect of the regalia (Calixtus avoided
  the hated word *homage*, but in effect lifted the prohibition on it).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` the [[investiture-contest]] and the [[gregorian-reform]]'s campaign to free the
  church from lay control of spiritual office.
- `contributed_to:` the mutual exhaustion of papacy and empire, and the availability of a
  face-saving conceptual compromise (the regalia/spiritualia distinction).

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **formal end of the lay-investiture conflict** and a working settlement that
  separated the spiritual office from its temporal endowment — a distinction with long
  consequences for European thinking about church and state.
- `produced:` a relative **weakening of imperial authority** over the German church and princes,
  contributing to the decentralisation of the empire that [[frederick-i-barbarossa|Frederick
  Barbarossa]] would later struggle against.
- `enabled:` the maturing of the **papal monarchy** of the twelfth century (see the
  [[gregorian-reform|reform papacy]] and the canon law of Gratian).

## Actors

**Pope Calixtus II** (formerly Archbishop Guido of Vienne, earlier a critic of compromise) and
**Emperor Henry V**, with the German episcopate as the contested prize. See [[investiture-contest]]
and [[salian-dynasty]].

## Historiography

The NCMH treats Worms as a genuine but **limited** settlement: it resolved the legal-symbolic
question without ending the deeper rivalry of *regnum* and *sacerdotium*, which resumed under the
Hohenstaufen. Historians debate whether it represents a papal "victory," a draw, or a pragmatic
fudge; most now stress the role of the moderate Chartres theology over the maximalist Gregorian
position in making agreement possible.
