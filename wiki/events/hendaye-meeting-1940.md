# The Hendaye Meeting, 23 October 1940

The only face-to-face meeting between [[franco-francisco|Franco]] and [[adolf-hitler|Hitler]], held in Hitler's train at the Hendaye border station to negotiate Spain's entry into the Second World War on the Axis side. No agreement was reached: Franco's price — Gibraltar, French Morocco, Oran, massive supplies of grain and arms — exceeded what Hitler would pay, since satisfying Spanish claims in Africa would push Vichy France toward defection. A secret protocol committed Spain in principle to eventual entry at a date of its own choosing, which never came. Hitler's remark that he would rather have "three or four teeth pulled" than repeat the nine-hour session is among the most quoted lines of the war.

## Detail (Payne 1987)

The meeting (three days after Himmler's Madrid visit) ran roughly seven hours of "polite, fawning, and evasive but obdurate conversation"; Franco silenced Hitler with an hour-long monologue on Spain's history in Morocco that reduced him to yawns, and Hitler privately called him a "Latin charlatan." Hitler had already decided to prioritize conciliating Vichy — hence no guarantee of Oran, Morocco, the Sahara extension, or the Cameroons linkage (the list Spain had first tabled on 19 June 1940). The secret protocol — its existence conclusively demonstrated only later by Carlos Rojas — replaced the German draft (entry "when Germany should see fit") with a Spanish text pledging Tripartite Pact adherence and entry against Britain at a date Spain would itself fix: it deliberately "lacked teeth." Aftermath: Serrano's Berchtesgaden summons (November 1940, Hitler conceding the African guarantees were impossible and slumping "silently onto a sofa"), Hitler's 31 December 1940 verdict to Mussolini that Franco "had made the biggest mistake of his life," the threatening 6 February 1941 letter, and the fruitless Franco–Mussolini meeting at Bordighera (12 February 1941). Payne's considered judgment: Franco held an "ingenuous conviction" of Hitler's friendship up to Hendaye and was willing in principle to enter for the right price — prudence and inability to pay were "inseparably intertwined," and the abstention that saved Spain was at first Hitler's choice, not Franco's.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: the [[fall-of-france-1940|Fall of France]], which made Axis victory appear imminent and irredentist gains available.
- contributed_to (negatively, by its failure): Spain's slide from "non-belligerence" (June 1940) back toward neutrality after 1942, and thus the regime's survival past 1945.

## Historiography

The traditional Francoist account ("skillful prudence" that kept Spain out of the war) is rejected by most scholarship: Payne (1987) shows Franco was willing to enter, at his price, during 1940–41 — the obstacle was the mismatch of price and payment, plus Britain's survival and Spain's prostration, not pacific intent. The chief interpretive dispute is thus willingness vs. prudence; Payne sits firmly on "willing but unpurchasable."
