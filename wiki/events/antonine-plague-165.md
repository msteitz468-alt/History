# The Antonine Plague (from 165 CE)

The devastating **pandemic** — probably **smallpox** — that swept the Roman Empire from the
mid-160s, brought back by the army returning from the **Parthian War**, and recurred for a
generation. It struck at the worst possible moment, as the empire faced war on two frontiers, and
is one of the markers of the **turning-point** from the Antonine peace to the age of crisis.
Treated in [[bowman-cah-v11-2000|CAH XI]] Ch. 3.

## Origin and course

The epidemic was contracted by the eastern army — some men caught it at the sack of **Seleucia** —
and carried west with the returning troops (the praetorians in particular), **reaching Rome with
[[lucius-verus|Lucius Verus']] returning forces (166)** and spreading across the empire. The great
physician **Galen**, present at Rome in 166, fled to Pergamum to escape it (his writings are a
chief medical source). The *Historia Augusta* gives a graphic account: "the dead were carried away
on carts and wagons... the plague carried off many thousands, including many prominent figures,"
and Marcus had to authorize **public funerals** for the poor. Rescripts in the *Digest* reflect
the surge in death-rate and the difficulties over burial; emergency religious ceremonies (the
*lectisternium*) were held to assuage public panic.

## Identification and scale

CAH XI notes the disease's **nature is uncertain** (smallpox, exanthematous typhus, and bubonic
plague have all been proposed, with smallpox the usual modern guess from Galen's symptoms) and
that its **scale is debated** — but its **seriousness is unquestionable**. It recurred in waves
over a generation, peaking in the early 170s, and modern estimates of total mortality range
widely (commonly cited as a substantial fraction of the population over its duration).

## Consequence Analysis

- `contributed_to:` the **strain on military manpower** exactly as the [[marcomannic-wars-166-180|Marcomannic
  Wars]] broke out — Marcus had to raise new legions and even enrol slaves, gladiators, and outlaws (Harper 2017, line 1522).
- `contributed_to:` **economic and fiscal stress** (Marcus auctioned imperial treasures), a temporary collapse of provincial mint production and debasement of silver coinages (such as a 10-year cessation of silver coinage in Alexandria) (Harper 2017, line 1526), and a sharp drop in real land values due to demographic contraction (Harper 2017, line 1530).
- `concurrent_with:` and aggravating the broader **crisis of Marcus' reign**, the plague is
  conventionally grouped with the Danube wars and the accession of Commodus as ending the "golden
  age."

## Significance

The Antonine Plague is the first securely attested **empire-wide pandemic** in Roman history, caused by the smallpox virus (*Variola major*) (Harper 2017, line 1230). Smuggled into the empire via the Red Sea trade axis, it carried an estimated overall mortality rate of 10% (and up to 20% in heavily hit urban areas), claiming 7 to 8 million lives (Harper 2017, lines 1208, 1544). Its demographic and economic impact — striking simultaneously with sustained barbarian pressure and costly frontier war — is widely seen as a key element in the **weakening of the High Empire** and the acceleration of its provincialization (Harper 2017, line 1557).

## Historiography

### A Silk Roads Pandemic? (Benjamin 2018)

[[benjamin-empires-ancient-eurasia-2018|Benjamin]] places the Antonine Plague within the
disease-history of the [[silk-roads|First Silk Roads Era]]. He traces its entry to the
Roman sack of [[ctesiphon|Ctesiphon]] and Seleucia in **163 CE** during Marcus Aurelius'
[[parthian-empire|Parthian]] war, after which infected troops carried it home (Galen, at
Aquileia in 168/169, describes smallpox-like pustules); modern demographers estimate
7–10% empire-wide mortality, 15% in cities. [CONTROVERSY] Benjamin highlights evidence of
**parallel epidemics in Later [[han-dynasty|Han]] China (c. 151–185 CE)**, which Rafe de
Crespigny (using Ge Hong) thinks "most probably connected to the simultaneous outbreak of
the Antonine Plague at the other end of the Silk Roads" — a striking but unproven claim that
the trade network spread disease across all of Eurasia, prefiguring the heavier
[[plague-of-cyprian-250|Plague of Cyprian]] and the
[[collapse-of-first-silk-roads-era-250|collapse of the era]].
