## Narrative

In 106 CE the governor of Syria, **Cornelius Palma**, annexed the [[nabataean-kingdom|Nabataean
kingdom]] and created the Roman **province of Arabia**; by 107 the first governor, Claudius
Severus, was in office. No significant military confrontation is attested — Roman coinage
celebrated *Arabia adquisita* ("acquired," pointedly not *capta*, "captured"), though local
resentment may lie behind some Safaitic inscriptions (Macdonald). The provincial capital was
established at **Bostra** (Bosra), the **Via Nova Traiana** was built from Bostra to the Red
Sea port of Ayla, and a new dating era was reckoned from the annexation. Several Decapolis
cities — Philadelphia (Amman), Adraa, Gerasa (Jerash) — joined the province. The garrison
was **Legio III Cyrenaica**, based at Bostra, with auxiliaries; Roman military presence is
attested as far south as Hegra in the Hijaz (2nd-c. inscriptions, including units raised
from the Arab tribe **Thamud** at the remote temple of Ruwafa, datable 166–169 CE).

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by`: the situation at the death of the last Nabataean king, **Rabbel II** (r.
70–106) — though the sources are near-silent on the circumstances. Fisher notes the absence
of the loyalty-suspicions that doomed Commagene, and quotes Millar ("speculation on
motives... is fruitless") and Isaac's wry remark that "the Romans did not agonize about the
annexation of a client kingdom." Likely `contributed_to` by Trajan's eastern ambitions
(soon realized in his [[trajans-parthian-war-114-117|Parthian war]]) and a wish to deepen
Roman presence in the Red Sea.

## Consequence Analysis

The province had no clearly delineated southern boundary; documented Roman presence south of
the core (Dumah, Hegra, Ruwafa) fades after the early 3rd century as imperial attention
shifted to Persia and South Arabia, with security thereafter delegated to proxies and
diplomats. In the long run the former kingdom's rural churches and monasteries fed the
power of the Roman Arab leaders [[al-harith-ibn-jabala|al-Harith]] and
[[al-mundhir-ibn-al-harith|al-Mundhir]] (see [[super-phylarchate]]), and its southern
territory is central to tracing the development of the [[petra|Nabataean-to-Arabic script]]
that would carry the text of the Quran.

## Historiography

The "peaceful annexation" reading rests on the *adquisita* coin legend and the lack of a
conquest narrative; the extent and permanence of Roman control in the deep south remains
debated on epigraphic grounds.
