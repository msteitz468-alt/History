## Narrative

The Time of Troubles began with the death of the last Rurikid tsar, Feodor I, in 1598, ending a dynasty that had ruled Muscovy since its origins. Boris Godunov, elected tsar but never fully accepted by the boyar elite, faced a catastrophic famine (1601–1603) that killed a substantial share of the population and triggered widespread unrest. His death in 1605 opened over a decade of chaos: a succession of pretenders claiming to be the murdered tsarevich Dmitry (the "False Dmitrys"), open civil war among boyar factions, and direct foreign intervention as Polish-Lithuanian forces occupied Moscow (1610–1612) and Swedish forces seized Novgorod (1611–1617).

A national volunteer army under Kuzma Minin and Prince Dmitry Pozharsky expelled the Polish garrison from Moscow in 1612, and a national assembly (Zemsky Sobor) elected the young Michael Romanov as tsar in 1613, founding the dynasty that would rule Russia until 1917.

## Causal Analysis

The crisis was caused_by the extinction of the Rurikid line and contributed_to by famine-driven social collapse, which together removed the legitimating basis of central authority and opened the way to foreign intervention by Muscovy's western neighbors.

## Consequence Analysis

The Time of Troubles produced (produced) the Romanov dynasty and, through the territorial losses confirmed in the Treaty of Stolbovo (1617, with Sweden) and Truce of Deulino (1618, with Poland-Lithuania), a weakened Muscovite state that took decades to recover full territorial and institutional strength.

## Historiography

The period is documented through a mix of Russian chronicle sources, foreign observer accounts (Polish and Swedish), and later Romanov-era historiography that had a strong interest in emphasizing the chaos of the interregnum to legitimate the new dynasty's restoration of order — a bias modern historians take into account when assessing the period's sources.
