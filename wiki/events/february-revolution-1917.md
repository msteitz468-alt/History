# February Revolution (1917)

The **February Revolution** of 1917 was a spontaneous, popular revolution that centered in the Russian capital of Petrograd. Sparked by shortages of food and fuel under the strains of World War I, the revolution culminated in the mutiny of the Petrograd military garrison, the collapse of the imperial administration, and the abdication of Emperor Nicholas II, ending three centuries of Romanov rule.

## Narrative

The revolution began spontaneously on March 8, 1917 (February 23, Old Style, which was International Women's Day). Strikes and demonstrations broke out in Petrograd, driven by working-class women protesting in bread lines and workers locked out of the Putilov factory. The demonstrations grew rapidly over the next two days, with protestors demanding bread, peace, and the overthrow of the autocracy.

On March 10, the strike became general. Nicholas II, who was at military headquarters in Mogilev, ordered the Petrograd military command to suppress the disorders by force. The following day, Sunday, March 11, troops fired on demonstrators, causing numerous casualties.

The turning point occurred on March 12 (February 27, Old Style). The reserve battalions of the Petrograd garrison, incensed by the shooting of civilians and sharing the grievances of the crowd, mutinied and fraternized with the demonstrators. Lacking loyal troops, imperial authority in the capital collapsed. 

Faced with this vacuum, two bodies emerged in the Duma building:
- Members of the State Duma sidestepped a dissolution decree and created a **Provisional Committee**, which quickly formed the **Provisional Government** under Prince George Lvov to restore order.
- Workers and socialist intellectuals organized the **Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies** to represent the revolutionary masses.

Recognizing that he had lost the support of both the population and his generals, Nicholas II abdicated on March 15, 1917, for himself and his son Alexis, in favor of his brother, Grand Duke Michael. Michael renounced the crown the following day, transferring authority to the Provisional Government pending the election of a constituent assembly.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **The Strains of World War I:** The war mobilized 15.5 million men and caused catastrophic losses. It broke the Russian economy, leading to runaway inflation and severe transport bottlenecks that prevented food and fuel from reaching Petrograd.
- `caused_by:` **Autocratic Mismanagement:** Nicholas II's decision to take personal military command in 1915 left the government in the hands of Empress Alexandra and Rasputin, alienating both the Duma and the military command.
- `caused_by:` **Garrison Mutiny:** The Petrograd garrison consisted of recent peasant recruits undergoing training who were highly receptive to revolutionary propaganda and unwilling to fire on fellow citizens. Their mutiny converted bread riots into a revolution.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **End of the Romanov Dynasty:** The abdication of Nicholas and Michael ended three centuries of Romanov rule, leaving Russia without a traditional source of authority.
- `produced:` **Dual Power:** Established a fragile power-sharing arrangement between the liberal **Provisional Government** (which held formal authority) and the socialist **Petrograd Soviet** (which held popular legitimacy and physical control over the garrison and infrastructure).
- `produced:` **Political Radicalization:** The collapse of the police and the introduction of full civil liberties allowed political parties, unions, and soviets to organize openly, paving the way for the radicalization of the masses.

## Actors

- **Nicholas II:** The last Romanov emperor, whose abdication formalized the end of the monarchy.
- **The Petrograd Garrison:** The reserve troops whose mutiny destroyed the military defense of the regime in the capital.
- **The Petrograd Proletariat:** The workers whose strikes and demonstrations initiated the revolution.

## Historiography

Historians generally agree that the February Revolution was a highly spontaneous, leaderless event. Radical leaders, including Lenin and Trotsky, were in exile and did not expect the monarchy to collapse when it did. Liberal historians view February as a genuine popular revolution that opened the path for democracy, which was later hijacked by the Bolsheviks. Soviet historians, while acknowledging the spontaneity of the initial strikes, argued that the Bolsheviks had prepared the working class for the struggle, analyzing the event as the transition from the bourgeois-democratic to the socialist revolution.
