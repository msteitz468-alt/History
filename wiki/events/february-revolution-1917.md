# February Revolution 1917

**February Revolution 1917** (23 Feb–2 Mar 1917 old style): spontaneous strikes and bread riots in Petrograd that overthrew the Romanov dynasty and established the Provisional Government.

## Narrative

Wartime economic collapse (peasants hoarding grain due to currency depreciation and shortage of industrial goods; transport breakdown; inflation from paper rouble emission; armaments priority starving consumer production) combined with military failures and Rasputin scandal to destroy the monarchy’s authority. Nicholas II’s decision to assume personal command at Mogilëv (1915) isolated him from the capital. On 22 February a strike began at the Putilov armaments plant. Women textile workers demonstrated over bread queues on 23 February and called on male workers for solidarity. By 24 February there was virtually a general strike in Petrograd. Nicholas prorogued the Duma on 26 February. Troops sent to suppress demonstrations handed over rifles or joined the protesters. The capital became a "maelstrom of revolt." Advised that his position was hopeless, Nicholas abdicated on 2 March while en route from Mogilëv (first offering the throne to his son Aleksei, then to Grand Duke Mikhail). An unofficial Duma committee formed the Provisional Government (announced 3 March), headed by Prince Lvov, with Milyukov (Foreign Affairs), Guchkov (War) and Kerenski (Justice). Universal civil freedoms, abolition of privileges, and elections to a Constituent Assembly with universal adult suffrage (including women) were immediately proclaimed. "These measures immediately made wartime Russia freer than any other country even at peace."

From [[cambridge-history-of-russia-v3-2006|Cambridge History of Russia Vol. III]] (Smith ch., lines 3703–3748): 'On 23 February 1917 thousands of female textile workers and housewives took to the streets of Petrograd to protest against the bread shortage and to mark International Women’s Day.' Protest drew in workers; by 25 Feb students and middle classes joined singing Marseillaise, waving red flags, 'Down with the War'. 'On Sunday, 26 February... the Volynskii regiment mutinied and by evening 66,700 soldiers had followed their lead.' 'Demonstrators freed prisoners from the Kresty jail, set fire to police stations, “blinded” portraits of the tsar...' 'Despite orders from Tsar Nicholas II... the military authorities were unable to summon sufficient loyal troops.' Pro-war Mensheviks called for soviet; within week 1,200 deputies. 'On the night of 27 February, the tsar’s cabinet resigned...' Duma committee formed. 'Faced with the loss of confidence of his generals, Nicholas abdicated in favour of his brother, Grand Duke Mikhail.' Mikhail refused; 'on 3 March the 300-year-old Romanov dynasty came to an end.' 'The two forces that brought down the monarchy – the movement of workers and soldiers and the middle-class parliamentary opposition – became institutionalised in... “dual power”.' Soviet EC 'agreed that it would support the Provisional Government in so far as it carried out a programme of democratic reform but would not be bound by its domestic or foreign policies.' 'From the outset the new revolutionary order expressed the deep social division between the “democracy” and propertied society.' Provisional manifesto committed to rights and Constituent Assembly 'but said nothing... about the burning issues of war and land.' 'It was the war that focused the otherwise disparate grievances of the people' (lines 4277).

**Causal Analysis**:
- caused_by: wartime strains on the fissured tsarist order (Service lines 561–565, 585).
- contributed_to: Nicholas’s autocratic intransigence and loss of elite support (even United Gentry reconsidered loyalty).
- preceded_by: 1905 Revolution, Lena massacre (1912), renewed strikes 1914–16.

**Consequence Analysis**:
- produced: "dual power"; Provisional Government’s inability to satisfy soldiers (peace), peasants (land) and workers (wages/conditions).
- enabled: Bolshevik rise and [[october-revolution-1917]].
- followed_by: Kornilov mutiny (Aug 1917).

## Actors
- Primary: [[nicholas-ii]], Petrograd workers/garrison.
- Secondary: Duma liberals and moderate socialists who formed the Provisional Government.

## Historiography (per Service 2009, lines 541–599; Freeze 2002 Orlovsky ch. lines 7501–7660)
Service emphasizes inevitability ("some kind of revolutionary clash was practically inevitable") given the "creaky structure of power" and Nicholas’s refusal to constitutionalize. The revolution was driven by material grievances (wages failing to keep pace with inflation; bread queues) more than ideology. Even those who had benefited materially from the war turned against the Emperor. The collection notes that the monarchy retained almost no support by late February 1917. Gaps: deeper analysis of provincial reactions and non-Russian regions beyond the capital’s maelstrom.

Freeze (2002) details pre-war regime weakness: Durnovo Feb 1914 memo warned Germany natural ally, war strains would topple order (lines 7569–7572); "crisis was at the top" mutual state/Society alienation (lines 7629–7631); Progressive Bloc demanded responsible govt, Nicholas assumed personal command (lines 7633–7634); public orgs (Zemgor, War Industries Committees) and special councils blurred public/private, creating rival power centres (lines 7639–7642); 1916 Turkestan conscription revolt "claiming thousands of lives" (lines 7645–7646). Feb: "On 23 February (8 March NS) a crowd of women seized the occasion of International Women’s Day to demonstrate against the high bread prices... The next day crowds of strikers... Desperate... on 26 February the authorities resorted to firearms: the Volhynian regiment opened fire... the next day the same Volhynian unit (joined by several guards regiments) took sides with the crowds... By 28 February the tsarist ministers were under arrest" (lines 7657–7658). Nicholas "final act... characteristically illegal: in contravention of the 1797 Law of Succession, Nicholas abdicated not only for himself but also for his son" (lines 7659–7660). Duma Committee (propertied) vs Petrograd Soviet ("two-stage revolution"); "Order No. 1" unleashed army democratization (lines 7757–7778). Provisional "Programme" promised self-gov to volost level and Constituent Assembly; enacted grain monopoly "declaring all grain to be state property" (lines 7769–7832). Miliukov note crisis (18 Apr reaffirming treaties) ignited demonstrations; first coalition formed (lines 7837–7841). Recent scholarship (Freeze) stresses social dimension (strikes 1,019 involving 2.44m workers per Koenker/Rosenberg; peasant maximalism on land) against "un-revisionist" views denying worker role (Pipes/Malia). Historiography notes Provisional's liberal agenda undone by war and "dual power" encroachment.
