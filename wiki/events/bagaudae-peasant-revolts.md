# Bagaudae (Peasant Revolts)

The **Bagaudae** (also Bacaudae) were groups of rebels attested mainly in late Roman Gaul (c.280s; 410s–440s Loire–Seine) and the Ebro valley (440s–450s). [[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005|Chris Wickham]] reads them as **mostly peasants with peasant aims**, not mere autonomist landowners or pro-barbarian clients.

## Narrative

Wickham places the risings against a **weakened but still functioning** imperial regime: taxation and judicial burdens became rejectable when Roman ideological hegemony cracked. They end by about **455**, while imperial rule still held in the regions concerned—so they are not symptoms of total state collapse, but of cracked consent under continued fiscal demand.

## Causal Analysis

- `contributed_to:` evidence for peasant agency under fiscal pressure (see [[peasant-mode-of-production]], [[tax-based-vs-land-based-states]]).
- `preceded_by:` third- and fifth-century crises that loosened ideological hegemony without immediately ending Roman government.
- Not simply `caused_by:` “barbarian invasion” as primary explanation in Wickham’s framing.

## Consequence Analysis

- Large-scale Western peasant revolt becomes rare after the mid-fifth century until much later compressions of feudalization (e.g. [[stellinga-revolt-841]] in Saxony).
- Analogous, in Wickham’s comparative set, to later Egyptian tax revolts under early Islamic rule (720s–830s) when fiscal pressure met contested hegemony.

## Historiography

Older readings cast Bagaudae as bandits, separatist aristocrats, or barbarian clients. Wickham’s peasant-aims reading is part of his broader recovery of peasant collective capacity in 400–800 ([[wickham-framing-the-early-middle-ages-2005]] Ch. 9).
