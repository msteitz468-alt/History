# Founding of Rome (traditional, 753 BCE)

The founding of Rome is the traditional origin-point of Roman history, dated by later Roman
reckoning to **753 BCE** and attributed to **Romulus**. In Mary Beard's *SPQR* the
"foundation" is treated as two quite different things that must be kept apart: the **legend**
(a charter-myth of Roman identity) and the **archaeological reality** (a gradual coalescence
of Iron Age hilltop settlements). See [[sources/beard-spqr-2015]].

## Narrative

In the legend, Romulus founds the city on the Palatine, kills his twin Remus in a quarrel
over the founding, opens Rome as an **asylum** to attract inhabitants, and secures wives by
abducting the Sabine women — episodes treated on [[actors/romulus]]. The traditional date of
753 BCE and the whole "ab urbe condita" chronology derive from later Roman antiquarian
calculation, not from any eighth-century record.

## Causal and Consequence Analysis

There is no contemporary evidence for a single founding act, so the "causes" are properly
`[[unknown]]`; what archaeology shows instead is a **process** — Iron Age villages on Rome's
hills growing together (synoikism), with genuine urban development and Etruscan cultural
contact by the sixth century BCE. As a *symbol*, however, the foundation had enormous
consequences: it anchored Roman chronology, supplied the master-myth of Roman identity
(open, composite, born in violence), and stood as the notional beginning of the sequence that
runs through the [[actors/roman-kingdom]] and [[actors/roman-republic]] to the
[[actors/roman-empire]].

## Historiography

The date, the founder, and the founding act belong to reconstruction and myth, not to
documented history; the precision of "753 BCE" is spurious (hence `date_precision: disputed`).
Archaeology supplies the only hard evidence — early settlement, later monumentalisation — and
consistently shows growth-by-process rather than a datable foundation event. Beard's handling
is a model of the wider methodological point of *SPQR*: for early Rome the historian's task is
to squeeze fragmentary material evidence and to read the literary tradition as a source for
Roman self-understanding rather than for events. Cf. [[actors/romulus]],
[[actors/roman-kingdom]].
