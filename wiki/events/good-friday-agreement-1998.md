## Narrative

The Good Friday Agreement — formally the 'Agreement Reached in the Multi-Party Negotiations', signed on 10 April 1998 (Good Friday) at Stormont — was the foundational document of the Northern Ireland peace settlement. It was endorsed by 71.1 per cent of voters in Northern Ireland and 94.39 per cent in the Republic in simultaneous referendums on 22 May 1998 (Jackson, *Oxford Handbook*, ch. 37).

The agreement was the product of decades of conflict management after [[the-troubles]], multiple failed constitutional experiments, and a shift in republican and unionist strategic thinking during the late 1980s and 1990s. Its official title was not universally used: 'the "agreement", the "Belfast agreement" and the "Good Friday agreement" were used as forms of ethnic shorthand' (ch. 37), signalling residual communal contestation over even the name of the settlement.

**The diplomatic process.** The Anglo-Irish Agreement of 15 November 1985, signed by Thatcher and Fitzgerald at Hillsborough, was the hinge point: it gave the Irish government a formal role in Northern Ireland governance, alienated Unionists, and confronted republicans with evidence that Britain was seeking a political rather than purely security solution. The IRA's Christmas message of 1985 described it as 'a highly sophisticated, counter-revolutionary plan', but republicans began to re-examine their strategy. John Hume (SDLP leader from 1979) initiated dialogue with Gerry Adams from 1988; their joint statement of September 1993 was described as having 'sanitized Irish republicanism' by indicating that Sinn Féin took seriously an unarmed ballot-box strategy.

The Downing Street Declaration of December 1993, a 'minor diplomatic masterpiece' (David Goodall), gave republican and unionist political leaders a framework for engagement. The IRA announced its ceasefire on 31 August 1994, followed by the UDA and UVF on 13 October. President Clinton's November 1995 visit to Belfast consolidated US engagement. The Canary Wharf bombing of February 1996 (which resumed the IRA campaign after 17 months) reflected republican frustration at British insistence on decommissioning as a precondition for negotiations. The IRA resumed its ceasefire on 19 July 1997, after Labour's landslide victory removed the Conservative government's dependence on Unionist votes. Multi-party talks chaired by former US Senator George Mitchell ran through 1997–98; Mitchell set 9 April as the deadline and published his own draft on 7 April. Agreement was reached in the early hours of 10 April.

**Content.** The agreement confirmed 'the birthright of all the people of Northern Ireland to identify themselves and be accepted as Irish or British, or both, as they may so choose' and recognised that any future change in Northern Ireland's status required majority consent — the 'principle of consent'. The Republic agreed to amend Articles 2 and 3 of its constitution, removing the irredentist claim to the territory of the six counties. A power-sharing Executive and Assembly were established. Cross-border bodies linking North and South, and a British-Irish Council, were created.

**Implementation difficulties.** The gap between agreement in principle and delivery in practice proved substantial. The IRA did not begin decommissioning until November 2001, and only formally ordered an end to its armed campaign on 28 July 2005. The Independent International Commission on Decommissioning (IICD) confirmed completion of IRA decommissioning on 26 September 2005. The Assembly was suspended four times on issues of decommissioning and trust. David Trimble (UUP, First Minister) presided over this precarious period before resigning as UUP leader in May 2005. In the political vacuum, the DUP overtook the UUP and Sinn Féin overtook the SDLP — the two parties that had made the Agreement were replaced by their harder-line rivals.

**St Andrews and the DUP-Sinn Féin settlement.** Following talks at St Andrews in October 2006, assembly elections in March 2007 produced a DUP/Sinn Féin-led result. On 8 May 2007, Ian Paisley (DUP) and Martin McGuinness (Sinn Féin) took up positions as First Minister and Deputy First Minister — a political outcome that would have seemed impossible a decade earlier. As Paul Arthur notes, former Taoiseach Albert Reynolds had been told in 1992 that Paisley would only negotiate 'when he is number one! . . . once he became number one all things became possible' (ch. 37).

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Anglo-Irish Agreement 1985 — institutionalised British-Irish diplomatic cooperation and demonstrated to republicans that the British state was seeking a political solution
- `caused_by:` IRA ceasefire (August 1994) and loyalist ceasefires (October 1994) — created the space for multi-party negotiations
- `caused_by:` US engagement — American pressure (Four Horsemen; Bipartisan Congressional Friends of Ireland; Clinton presidency) shaped British policy and 'sanitized' Sinn Féin for talks
- `contributed_to:` hunger strikes 1981 — the death of Bobby Sands and nine others galvanized the 'ballot box and Armalite' strategy and diverted republicanism toward politics
- `contributed_to:` Enniskillen bomb 1987 — public relations catastrophe that accelerated republican strategic rethinking
- `preceded_by:` [[the-troubles]]

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` power-sharing Northern Ireland Executive — institutionalized consociational democracy between unionist and nationalist parties
- `produced:` Police Service of Northern Ireland (PSNI) — the RUC was replaced in 2001 following the Patten Report (1999); Catholic recruitment increased; Sinn Féin joined the Policing Board in 2007
- `produced:` constitutional change in Republic — Articles 2 and 3 amended; the Republic formally abandoned its territorial claim to Northern Ireland
- `produced:` decommissioning and demilitarization — the IICD dissolved in February 2010 following confirmation of IRA decommissioning
- `produced:` transformation of Northern Irish political landscape — DUP and Sinn Féin replaced UUP and SDLP as dominant parties, paradoxically strengthening rather than undermining the settlement

## Historiography

The Agreement generated a large political-science and historical literature almost immediately. It has been compared to an 'archaeological site' that can be 'read as a brief constitutional summary of the troubles' (Aughey, cited in ch. 37). Christine Bell's framework classifies peace agreements as 'a form of transitional constitution'. The comparison with the 1973 Sunningdale Agreement is frequently made: the Belfast Agreement offered fuller recognition of the right to self-determination, was more inclusive, and — crucially — offered something Sunningdale could not: a complete end to political violence.

Historians note the role of 'constructive ambiguity' in the Agreement's language: 'It offers a version of multiple identities, of a kind for which no legal language yet exists' (Declan Kiberd, cited in ch. 37). This ambiguity enabled the parties to sign while maintaining incompatible interpretations — creating short-term problems for implementation (especially on decommissioning) while preserving long-term viability.

By the time the DUP-Sinn Féin executive took office in 2007, a survey found that 57.5 per cent of Protestant participants felt the Agreement was 'a good thing for Northern Ireland', signalling 'greater Protestant investment in power sharing and a willingness to rely less heavily on external British support' (ch. 37).
