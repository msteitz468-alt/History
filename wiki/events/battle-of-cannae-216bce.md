# The Battle of Cannae (216 BCE)

The greatest battlefield disaster in Roman history and the most celebrated
double-envelopment in ancient warfare. Part of the [[second-punic-war-218-201bce|
Second Punic War]]; treated in [[astin-cah-v8-1989|CAH VIII]] Ch. 3 (Briscoe).

## Narrative

After [[hannibal|Hannibal's]] victories at the Trebbia (218) and Lake Trasimene
(217), Rome resolved to confront him with overwhelming force. The consuls of 216,
**L. Aemilius Paullus** and **C. Terentius Varro**, were given an unusually large
army — Polybius reports eight legions with their allied complement, c. 80,000 men.
Hannibal seized **Cannae**, a Roman supply depot on the River Aufidus in Apulia,
drawing the Romans onto flat ground favourable to his superior cavalry. In the
battle (late June) Hannibal's centre gave way in a controlled crescent while his
African veterans on the wings and his cavalry closed behind the Roman mass,
surrounding and destroying it. Paullus fell; Polybius says only 14,500 Romans
escaped death or capture.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: the Roman strategic decision — taken, Briscoe argues from Polybius,
by **the Senate as a whole**, not by Varro alone — to abandon caution and seek a
decisive engagement.
**[CONTRADICTION-note]** The tradition (Polybius and Livy) blaming the "demagogue"
Varro and absolving Paullus (Scipio Africanus' father-in-law) is rejected by
modern scholarship: the Senate's vote of thanks to Varro after the battle and his
continued employment show the hostile portrait is Scipionic-family apologetic.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the **defection of southern Italy** — Capua, much of Samnium, Lucania,
and the Greek cities of the south (Tarentum followed in 212); the defection of
**[[syracuse]]**. It did *not* produce the collapse of the Roman alliance: no
Latin colony and no Roman citizen community joined Hannibal, and Etruria and
central Italy held.
enabled: the entrenchment of the [[fabian-strategy|Fabian strategy of attrition]],
which governed the war in Italy from 216 to 210. Polybius devoted the whole of his
sixth book to explaining the constitution of a state that could climb out of such
an abyss.

## Historiography

Cannae is reported in full by both [[polybius]] (III.106–117) and [[livy]]
(XXII.41–50). The factional reading of the **elections for 216** (the disputed
interregnum, the Scipionic dictator declared *vitio creatus*) is a much-debated
prosopographical problem (Briscoe's Additional Note). The casualty figure of
~70,000 dead is a maximum; the size of the engaged Roman army (80,000) Briscoe
accepts. The battle became the archetype of annihilation by envelopment in later
military theory.
