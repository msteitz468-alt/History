# VOC Conquest of the Banda Islands (1621)

The **1621 conquest of the Banda Islands** — the world's sole source of nutmeg and
mace — was the most violent single act of the [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC]]'s
monopoly drive: a near-total destruction of an indigenous society to secure a spice
monopoly. It is analysed in
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]]
by Martine van Ittersum (treaty-making and dispossession, ch. 6) and Adam Clulow
(the Japanese soldiers who carried out the executions, ch. 7).

## Narrative

The Banda Islands, 2,000 km east of Java, were governed as "confederations of
villages" — the *ulilima* (five) and *ulisiva* (nine) — whose **orangkayas**
(aristocrats) met on Nera to negotiate; their subsistence economy had largely given
way to dependence on the nutmeg and mace trade. From 1608 the VOC directors ordered
their commanders to sign exclusive-supply contracts "with the consent of the
Indians" and to build forts (Nera 1609, Pulo Way 1616, Great Banda 1621), reasoning
that no rival could "trade in… those places… where we have possession or exclusive
contracts." Bandanese resistance — including the murder of admiral Verhoef in 1609 —
and their attempts to play the [[english-east-india-company|EIC]] against the Dutch
(Nathaniel Courthope's December 1616 treaty placed Pulo Run under James I) made
monopoly impossible without force.

**Jan Pieterszoon Coen** resolved the problem by invasion. In 1621 he commanded
"sixteen warships and nearly a thousand soldiers"; the VOC–EIC Treaty of Defence
(London, June 1619) ensured the English did not interfere. On **8 May 1621** six
**Japanese soldiers in VOC service** were ordered to execute 44 captured orangkaya —
to "hack the eight leading orangkaya through the middle, then cut off their heads and
then quarter their bodies." In all, 48 orangkayas were tried and executed and about
789 people deported to Batavia as slaves. **Of an estimated 15,000 original
inhabitants, only about a thousand remained.**

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` [[dutch-east-india-company|VOC monopoly policy]] — the directors'
  standing instruction to secure exclusive nutmeg/mace supply by contract and fort.
- `contributed_to:` Bandanese political disunity (governed, per Reael, "entirely in a
  democratic fashion… like a republic") and their instrumental use of EIC treaties,
  which convinced Coen that only depopulation would secure the monopoly.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the **perken** system — land divided into plots worked by *perkeniers*
  (many former VOC soldiers) using imported slaves; by 1638 slaves were two-thirds of
  the population, three-quarters by the late eighteenth century.
- `produced:` a secured VOC monopoly on the world nutmeg supply.
- The episode anchors van Ittersum's thesis that **treaties were instruments of
  dispossession**, not alternatives to conquest — she calls the massacre an
  "inconvenient truth" for historians who stress indigenous agency through
  "negotiating" and "resisting" empire (see
  [[european-exceptionalism-early-modern-asia]] and [[interpolity-law]]).

## Historiography

The Banda conquest is a touchstone in the debate over **indigenous agency versus
European coercion** in early modern Asia. A revisionist current (including Adam
Clulow's earlier work) emphasises how the Bandanese manipulated treaty texts and
ceremonies and played the EIC against the VOC. Martine van Ittersum, in
[[clulow-mostert-dutch-english-east-india-companies-2018|Clulow & Mostert (2018)]],
treats the massacre as an "inconvenient truth" for that school: whatever agency the
Bandanese exercised, the VOC's material superiority and willingness to depopulate the
islands were decisive, and the treaties served dispossession (see
[[interpolity-law]] and [[european-exceptionalism-early-modern-asia]]). The population
figures (c. 15,000 reduced to c. 1,000; 789 enslaved; 48 orangkayas executed) derive
from VOC records and are broadly accepted, though exact totals are debated. Giles
Milton's *Nathaniel's Nutmeg* is the best-known popular account; the scholarship rests
on the VOC archive and Coen's own correspondence (Colenbrander edition).
