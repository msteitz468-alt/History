## Narrative

The Portuguese encounter with the peoples of coastal Brazil unfolded over a full century as a complex, uneven process of trade, alliance, warfare, epidemic, and forced congregation — not a single decisive campaign. John Monteiro's analysis in the *Cambridge History of the Native Peoples of the Americas* (Vol. III, Part 1, Ch. 13) provides the essential modern reconstruction.

**Pre-contact coastal world.** At European contact, the Brazilian coast was occupied by dozens of distinct peoples. Most of the coastal societies were grouped under the generic label *Tupi*, sharing a related family of languages (Tupi-Guarani) and broadly similar social organization, though constituting no political unity. As Gabriel Soares de Sousa observed, "even though the Tupinikin and Tupinamba are enemies, between them there is no greater difference in language and customs than that between the residents of Lisbon and those of Beira." The collective term [[tupinamba]] applied to the coastal Tupi-Guarani from the captaincy of Sao Vicente to the southern shore of the Amazon. Interior and hinterland peoples called *Tapuia* (including many Ge-speaking groups) occupied the backlands and were regarded by both Tupi and Portuguese as categorically different.

Population estimates remain contested. John Hemming's *Red Gold* (1978) placed the coastal Tupi (excluding the Guarani) at approximately 625,000 at contact, though Monteiro flags methodological reservations: spatial mobility, double-counting of migrant groups, and compression of five centuries of evidence into the single moment of 1500. Contemporary sources — supported by growing archaeological evidence — indicate that early sixteenth-century coastal and floodplain populations were substantially larger and denser than modern forest peoples.

**The early barter phase (c. 1500–1530).** During the first three decades, European presence was sporadic — "periodic reconnaissance missions and the modest extraction of dye woods." Portuguese and French traders dispatched irregular shipments of brazilwood and small numbers of Indian slaves, but indigenous lifeways were minimally disrupted. The barter relationship was not simply economic: to the Tupi, the supply of foodstuffs and forest products was embedded in a network of symbolic exchange, alliance, and reciprocity that European colonists systematically misread as market behavior.

**Alliance, intermarriage, and the captaincy system (1530–1549).** The expedition of Martim Afonso de Sousa (1530–1532) established the first permanent settlement at Sao Vicente. The crown then divided the coast into fifteen captaincies distributed among twelve proprietors (1534–1536), but "the captaincy plan proved a resounding failure in almost all cases." Success depended on alliance with local headmen cemented by marriage: castaways who integrated themselves into Tupi kinship networks — Joao Ramalho among the Tupinikin of Sao Vicente (son-in-law of headman Tibirica; Ulrich Schmidl reported he "could assemble five thousand warriors in a single day") and Diogo Alvares (*Caramurii*) among the Tupinamba of Bahia — became indispensable brokers. The Portuguese perceived that "the permanent state of war between Tupi groups offered a potentially unlimited source of Indian captives," while the Tupi saw European firearms as a valuable military asset within the existing logic of inter-village vengeance cycles.

**Royal government, Jesuits, and the onset of systematic conquest (1549–1560).** In 1549 the fleet of Governor-General Tome de Sousa established royal government at Salvador (Bahia) and brought the first Jesuit missionaries under Manuel da Nobrega. The governor's standing orders (*regimento*) from King Joao III inaugurated formal Portuguese Indian policy: protect "peaceful" Indians while authorizing the enslavement or destruction of those who resisted. This double-edged posture remained the structural logic of Luso-Indian relations for the rest of the century.

The Jesuits simultaneously began their aldeia (mission-village) system, concentrating over 40,000 Indians in the immediate area of Salvador by the early 1560s. Governor Mem de Sa (1558–1572) pursued both strategies vigorously: he supported the missions while conducting military campaigns that reportedly burned 160 villages along the Paraguacu River (1558–1559) and 300 villages near Ilheus during the suppression of Tupinikin resistance.

**The Tamoio War and French interlude (c. 1548–1567).** The most significant organized resistance emerged among Tupinamba groups along the southern coast (Cabo Frio to Sao Vicente), who formed a pan-village movement called the *Tamoio* (Tupi for "grandparent/ancestor"). Nobrega's 1557 account traced its origins directly to "the frequent Portuguese and Tupinikin raids on Tupinamba villages, the sack and destruction of rogas, and the taking of Indian slaves." In 1555 the French Calvinist Nicholas Durand de Villegaignon established *Antarctic France* in Guanabara Bay, providing firearms and military support to the Tamoio. After years of fighting punctuated by Jesuit diplomacy and epidemic crises, Mem de Sa stormed the French fort (1560) and by 1567 Rio de Janeiro was established under Portuguese control; surviving Tamoio retreated inland or submitted.

**Epidemics as conquest (1559–1580).** Disease struck before and alongside military campaigns. The first major smallpox epidemic, "possibly introduced by African slaves who participated in the campaigns against the Tupinikin," raged along the east coast in 1559, carrying off over 600 Indian slaves in Espirito Santo alone. By 1562 it had spread from Sao Vicente to Pernambuco. Measles broke out the following year. In Bahia, according to contemporary Jesuit accounts, "30,000 Indians and Africans perished in the brief interval of 3 months." Between 1560 and 1580 the Bahia aldeia population fell from over 40,000 to fewer than 10,000. Anchieta wrote in the early 1580s: "The people that have been wasted in Bahia over the last twenty years is something incredible; because no one considered that so many people could ever be expended, especially in so short a time."

**The slave-war cycle and the Law of 1570.** The "Just War" (*guerra justa*) against the Caete (1562) — justified because the Caete had killed Brazil's first bishop — set a precedent that shaped Portuguese law. Anchieta reported that within a few months over 50,000 captives were taken, but only 10,000 survived to serve as slaves, "the others falling victim either to ruthless massacres or uncompromising disease." The crown responded with the Law of 20 March 1570, restricting legitimate enslavement to wars duly authorized by the king or governor, and to the ransom (*resgate*) of captives destined for cannibalistic ritual sacrifice (the *indios da corda*, or bound Indians). In practice the law's loopholes — especially the *resgate* category — were systematically abused: "The expeditions often adopted the term *resgate* to describe their operation, which came to mean not only ransom but also any other sort of trade with the Indians."

**Sugar and the shift to African labor (1570–1600).** Between 1570 and 1600 the number of *engenhos* (sugar mills) along the northeastern coast grew threefold (24 to 100 in Pernambuco and the northeast). Indian slavery supplied the foundations of this expansion. Stuart Schwartz's analysis of Bahian plantations documents the persistence and complexity of Indian labor arrangements through 1620, when African slavery became predominant. Anchieta's 1583 account of 20,000 captives taken from the Aribo backlands six years earlier — of whom only 2,000 had survived — encapsulates the destructive cycle: high mortality from disease forced new expeditions into ever-more-distant lands, driving the frontier of slave-raiding progressively inland. "This cycle played out on the sugar-growing coast sometime during the first half of the seventeenth century, but it lasted well into the eighteenth century" in Sao Paulo, Goias, Maranhao, and Para.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Portuguese expansion under the captaincy system created land and labor demands incompatible with indigenous subsistence patterns.
- `caused_by:` sugar economy — plantation agriculture from the 1540s required permanent forced labor. Most sixteenth-century planters "turned exclusively to the local Indian population."
- `contributed_to:` preexisting inter-Tupi warfare — the Portuguese exploited the vengeance-cycle logic of Tupi conflict to generate captives. "The Portuguese soon perceived that the permanent state of war between Tupi groups offered a potentially unlimited source of Indian captives."
- `contributed_to:` epidemic disease, likely introduced via the [[atlantic-slave-trade]]'s early African slave imports to Brazil; smallpox (1559) and measles (1562–1563) devastated coastal populations.
- `concurrent_with:` French colonial competition — the establishment of Antarctic France (1555) intensified Portuguese military aggression along the southern coast.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` [[brazilian-indian-slavery]] — the conquest wars institutionalized the enslavement of coastal peoples as the economic foundation of the sugar colony.
- `produced:` [[jesuit-aldeias]] — the mission-village system arose as the crown's preferred alternative to unregulated slaving, though it accelerated cultural disintegration and epidemic spread.
- `enabled:` shift to [[atlantic-slave-trade]] — the demographic collapse of coastal Tupi peoples made African slave labor economically necessary; the transition was substantially complete on the coast by the mid-17th century.
- `produced:` near-total demographic and political extinction of independent coastal Tupi polities. Populations that had numbered in the hundreds of thousands were reduced to scattered remnants by 1600.
- `produced:` mixed *mamaluco* population as cultural brokers and later as leading agents of interior slave-raiding (*bandeiras*).
- `produced:` santidade resistance movements — syncretic indigenous cults emerged as responses to Jesuit conversion efforts and colonial dispossession.

## Actors

- **[[tupinamba]]**: The predominant coastal Tupi-Guarani grouping, whose social organization, warfare logic, and ritual life are the primary subject of Monteiro's analysis. Their internal fragmentation made resistance difficult; their military alliances (as Tamoio) represented the most significant organized challenge to Portuguese expansion.
- **Tupinikin**: Coastal Tupi of Sao Vicente; allied with the Portuguese through the mediation of Joao Ramalho and headman Tibirica. Later rebelled (1562) when pushed too far by slaving.
- **Caete**: Northeastern coastal Tupi; subjected to a "Just War" in 1562 following the killing of Brazil's first bishop; ~50,000 captured, most dying in transit or shortly after.
- **Portuguese Crown / Governor-General**: The regimento of Tome de Sousa (1549) established the double policy of protection/destruction that shaped the century's violence.
- **Mem de Sa (Governor-General 1558–1572)**: Pursued military conquest most aggressively; directed campaigns from Rio de Janeiro to Paraiba; simultaneously supported Jesuit missions.
- **Society of Jesus (Jesuits)**: Arrived 1549; established the aldeia system; opposed unrestricted Indian slavery; key figures include Manuel da Nobrega, Jose de Anchieta, and Azpilcueta Navarro.
- **French**: Maintained trade alliances with Tupinamba and Potiguar; established Antarctic France (Guanabara Bay, 1555–1567); expelled by Mem de Sa.
- **Mamalucos**: Mixed-descent backwoodsmen fluent in Tupi, key agents of interior slave-raiding from the 1570s onward. Domingos Fernandes Nobre (*Tomacauna*), deposed before the Inquisition in 1591, is a documented case.

## Historiography

**Source quality.** The primary sources are rich but institutionally biased: Portuguese and French chronicles, Jesuit letters and relations (especially the *Monumenta Brasiliae* and the correspondence of Nobrega and Anchieta), and German traveler accounts (Hans Staden; Ulrich Schmidl). These sources systematically simplify indigenous cultural complexity — the Tupi/Tapuia dichotomy is a Portuguese construct, not an indigenous self-description — and project European categories of political unity, slavery, and labor onto fundamentally different social realities.

**Debates.** The central demographic debate concerns precontact population size. John Hemming's *Red Gold* (1978) remains the most complete estimate (2,431,000 total; c. 625,000 coastal Tupi) but is subject to methodological reservations regarding spatial mobility, double-counting of migrant groups, and the compression of centuries of data into the static moment of 1500. The question of whether larger precontact populations implied more complex political structures (chiefdoms, as argued for [[amazonia]] by Anna Roosevelt and Antonio Porro) remains open for coastal Brazil; Monteiro's ethnohistorical evidence suggests the coastal Tupi-Guarani followed a different trajectory.

**Interpretive evolution.** Alfred Metraux and Florestan Fernandes produced foundational ethnographies of Tupinamba social organization (diffusionist and functionalist respectively), but Monteiro critiques both for treating the conquest period as mere "reactions" to colonial disruption rather than as genuine indigenous history. The most significant revisionism is Monteiro's insistence that indigenous warfare remained rooted in precolonial vengeance logic even as it was subordinated to colonial labor demands — alliance and resistance were not simple opposites but intertwined strategies.

**Collection coverage.** Moderate for this chapter; the primary Jesuit letters and Monteiro's own synthesis are the core. Coverage of Tapuia peoples (Ge-speakers and others) is acknowledged to be thin even by 16th-century standards.
