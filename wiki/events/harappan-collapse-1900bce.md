# Harappan Collapse (c. 1900–1700 BCE)

## Narrative

A decline set in around 1900 BCE in the dynamics of the Harappan (Indus Valley) system, and after 1700 BCE little remained of the Harappan Civilisation as an urban entity. As Avari emphasises, "It did not, however, come to an abrupt end within a particular period of decades or during a particular century." The collapse was gradual, multi-causal, and did not erase Harappan culture entirely — rather, it ended the specifically urban phase while village-level and regional cultures continued.

The Mature Harappan urban phase, which had flourished for approximately 700 years (c. 2600–1900 BCE), represented the high point of a civilisation anchored in the Indus river system and its tributaries. The five great first-tier cities — Harappa, Mohenjo Daro, Dholavira, Rakhigari, and Ganveriwala — had each covered between 80 and 100 hectares and supported sophisticated urban populations. By 1700 BCE, Mohenjo Daro had become, in Avari's phrase, "a ghost town"; thirty-seven centuries would pass before its former grandeur was revealed.

## Causal Analysis

### Theories — the Aryan Invasion (rejected)

Until quite recently the dominant explanation was that the Indus cities were destroyed by the Aryans entering India from Iran and Afghanistan through the Bolan and Khyber passes. This theory rested on two sets of evidence:

1. The Rig-Veda (the premier Vedic text) references the Aryan god Indra destroying a hostile people called the Dasas or Dasyus who lived in fortified places called *pur*.
2. The discovery of skeletons of men, women and children from the lower city of Mohenjo Daro, interpreted as victims of a "last massacre."

The archaeologist most associated with this interpretation was Sir Mortimer Wheeler, who concluded that "on circumstantial evidence, Indra stands accused." Most literary scholars and archaeologists today dismiss Wheeler's theory as too simplistic. The Rig-Vedic *pur* "was nothing like an Indus city; it was just a structure of flimsy ramparts and stockades." If Indra had destroyed the great Indus cities, it would be extraordinary that the Rig-Veda makes no mention of the characteristic features of Harappan urbanism — "the well-laid-out streets, houses, wells, drains, granaries and seals." The skeletons from Mohenjo Daro's lower city are considered wholly insufficient evidence of an organised attack on such a large system. Wheeler later admitted the validity of other reasons for the Harappan decline while still claiming a massacre occurred.

### Multi-Causal Synthesis (current consensus)

"Scholars now consider a combination of natural and socio-economic factors to be the most likely reason for the decline of the Indus cities" (Avari). The primary factors identified are:

**Geological/tectonic:** The Indus region is believed to have experienced severe tectonic disturbances brought about by earthquakes at the beginning of the second millennium BCE. These upheavals affected the normal course of the Indus and its tributaries and helped dry up the nearby Ghaggar–Hakra river. Modern satellite imagery confirms that dramatic shifts in river courses might have created great floods, cutting off food-producing areas from the cities themselves. Large quantities of silt layers in the upper levels of Mohenjo Daro — "many feet above the river course" — attest to those floods.

**Climatic:** Research into rainfall patterns shows that around 2500 BCE there was a great rise in rainfall, but by the beginning of the second millennium BCE it had dropped markedly. Declining rainfall would have damaged food production and contributed to depopulation of the cities.

**Environmental degradation (deforestation):** The burning of charcoal in the brick-baking kilns that produced the millions of standardised bricks of Harappan urbanism caused extreme deforestation and loss of trees. This further contributed to the unpredictability of rainfall. A civilisation built on fired brick had, in a sense, consumed the forests that sustained its water cycle.

**Socio-economic cascade:** With rivers shifting courses, rainfall declining and food failing to arrive from the countryside, a slow but inevitable collapse of the Indus system followed. As Avari describes the sequence: "The Indus cities no longer had surplus produce for trade, and the ensuing loss of revenue would have affected all classes of people. The city authorities became powerless to prevent civil unrest or brigandage, and groups of people were continually leaving the cities for their own safety and survival. As the cities became poorer, their services declined dramatically. The drains and sewers of Mohenjo Daro, kept in good and clean working order for centuries, clogged up with waste and excreta, resulting in disease and pestilence."

## Consequence Analysis

The collapse of the Harappan Civilisation was "really a collapse of its urban features." Its culture did not cease to exist wholesale. The sophisticated urban lifestyle ended, but folk culture continued at village level. Several beliefs, rituals, simple crafts, and rural features survived and developed into proto-historic cultures in surrounding regions.

Two important migrations resulted, caused particularly by the accelerated drying up of the Ghaggar–Hakra river during the later phase:
1. Migration toward the Ganga–Yamuna doab
2. Migration toward Gujarat, leading toward the Deccan

Beyond these large inter-regional movements, local migrations from urban settlements into rural areas also occurred.

**Post-Harappan regional cultures** that archaeologists have identified from this dispersal include:
- **Ochre-Coloured Pottery Culture** in the Ganges plain
- **Jhukar Culture** in Sind
- **Cemetery H Culture** in Punjab
- **Banas Culture** in Mewar, Rajasthan
- **Malwa Culture** in Madhya Pradesh

These cultures evolved as part of the Chalcolithic/Bronze Age and did not regress to Stone Age skill levels. "Some of these cultures survived for many centuries, thus continuing many of the non-urban Harappan traditions."

The peoples of these cultures eventually came into contact with Aryan migrants, who began entering India from around 1700 BCE onwards. "A new Indo-Aryan civilisation would come to shape India's history for the next thousand years and more."

## Historiography

**Source quality:** The collapse is known entirely from archaeology, since the Harappan script remains undeciphered. There are no contemporary written accounts. The main primary evidence includes stratigraphic data from the urban sites, silt layer analysis at Mohenjo Daro, satellite imagery of former river courses, and the geographical distribution of post-Harappan regional pottery cultures.

**Scholarly debates:** The dominant debate has been the Aryan invasion theory versus natural/environmental causation. Wheeler's "Indra stands accused" framing dominated mid-twentieth-century scholarship but has been largely rejected. The current consensus, endorsed by Avari and attributed to Coningham (2005) and others, emphasises a "combination of factors." There remains debate about the relative weight of tectonic, climatic, environmental, and internal political factors; and about whether the migration of Aryan groups was a cause, a consequence, or simply an unrelated contemporary development.

**Recent revisionism (last 30 years):** Satellite imagery confirming dramatic shifts in river courses has strengthened the hydrological explanation. Research into rainfall patterns (Kochhar 2000) has quantified the rainfall decline. The role of deforestation from brick-burning kilns has been more clearly articulated. These developments have moved scholarship firmly toward multi-causal environmental and systemic collapse models and away from external invasion theories.

**Relationship to Aryan migration debate:** The question of whether Aryans caused, accelerated, or simply followed the Harappan collapse is intertwined with the [[indo-european-origins]] controversy. Avari notes that the Aryan invasion theory will be discussed further in Chapter 4 of his work. The two phenomena (Harappan decline c. 1900–1700 BCE; Aryan entry from c. 1700 BCE) appear to be sequential rather than simultaneous, further undermining the invasion thesis.

**Collection coverage:** Avari (2007) gives substantive treatment in Chapter 3. The Cambridge World History and earlier Thapar treatment (1966) are also ingested. The 1966 Thapar account retained more of the invasion model; subsequent scholarship has moved strongly toward the multi-causal environmental view.
