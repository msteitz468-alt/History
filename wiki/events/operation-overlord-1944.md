# Operation Overlord (Normandy Campaign, 6 June – late August 1944)

The largest amphibious invasion in history and the campaign that opened the Western Front in Europe. From the initial D-Day landings on five beaches through the bitter battle for Caen, the controversial armored operations, the US breakout at Cobra, and the encirclement at Falaise, Overlord established the conditions for the liberation of France and the final advance into Germany.

**Full narrative and broader context:** See [[events/world-war-ii-1939-1945]].

## Narrative

Allied forces under SHAEF (Eisenhower) landed on 6 June 1944 on the beaches of Normandy after years of planning and debate over the Second Front. British and Canadian forces (Second Army under Dempsey, overall land command Montgomery) faced the most difficult objectives around Caen. American forces landed at Utah and the bloodily contested Omaha. Airborne operations preceded the seaborne assault.

The campaign quickly became a struggle against the bocage terrain and determined German defense. Caen, a D-Day objective, was not secured until mid-July after costly operations. Operation Goodwood (British) and Cobra (American) finally produced the breakout. The subsequent pursuit and the Battle of the Falaise Pocket destroyed much of the German Seventh Army and Fifth Panzer Army, though the gap was not fully closed.

The campaign cost the Allies approximately 225,000 casualties; German losses exceeded 200,000 (plus ~200,000 captured in the pocket and after).

## Causal Analysis

The decision for Normandy was the result of long Allied strategic compromise: US insistence on direct cross-Channel attack versus British preference for peripheral Mediterranean operations. Once launched, the plan's success depended on:

- Naval and air supremacy enabling the landings and supply build-up (Mulberry harbors critical after storm damage).
- Deception (Fortitude) fixing German reserves in the Pas de Calais.
- The ability to expand the lodgment before German panzer reserves could mass for a decisive counterattack (prevented in part by Rommel's absence and Hitler's reserve release delays).

Montgomery's "master plan" (as presented postwar) emphasized fixing German armor on the British eastern flank to enable American breakout in the west. D'Este's analysis (and contemporary documents) shows the plan evolved under pressure and that Caen's early capture was considered essential for both the shield function and British maneuver room.

## Consequence Analysis

- Strategic: Opened the second front; tied down and destroyed irreplaceable German formations in the West.
- Operational: Demonstrated both the power and the friction of large-scale coalition combined-arms operations in difficult terrain. Exposed British infantry manpower crisis; highlighted value (and limits) of heavy bomber support for ground operations.
- Command: Strained inter-Allied relations (Montgomery vs. Eisenhower/Bradley over pace and claims); produced lasting historiographical controversy over "the master plan" and the Falaise gap.
- Long-term: Set the stage for the advance to the Rhine and the final defeat of Nazi Germany in the West.

## Actors

Primary Allied commanders as listed in frontmatter. German defense initially under Rommel (who favored forward defense) and Rundstedt (more traditional mobile reserve doctrine); both constrained by Hitler.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960): the German high command's Normandy

German command was split on where the invasion would fall: Rundstedt and Rommel expected Pas-de-Calais, where the 15th Army grew from 10 to 15 infantry divisions by spring 1944, while Hitler's own intuition pointed to Normandy from March 1944 ("Watch Normandy!"), prompting some reinforcement between the Seine and Loire — but the bulk of German strength stayed north of the Seine because of Allied deception (Fortitude). On the night of 5–6 June, Rundstedt's HQ noted unusually heavy coded BBC messages to the French Resistance and radar jamming between Cherbourg and Le Havre, and the 15th Army was alerted at 10pm — but Rundstedt did not alert the 7th Army, whose sector the landings actually hit; 7th Army's own commander, General Friedrich Dollmann, was away at a map exercise in Rennes, having ordered a "temporary relaxation of the standing alert" that day. First landing reports reached 7th Army at 1:11am; general alarm sounded at 1:30am. Rommel himself was at his home in Herrlingen, Germany, having departed on 5 June believing invasion "not imminent"; General Speidel telephoned him at 6am on the 6th, and he did not reach Army Group B headquarters until late afternoon.

Hitler's order, logged in the Seventh Army war diary at 16:55 hrs on 6 June, demanded the beachhead be "annihilated by the evening of June 6" — General Pemsel's blunt reply: "That would be impossible." Hitler had reserved to himself personal authorization of any panzer-division movement in the West and did not wake from an afternoon sleep to grant it until 3pm that day, a delay Speidel and Shirer both identify as decisive.

At the 17 June meeting at Margival (an unused invasion-of-Britain bunker near Soissons), Speidel's eyewitness account describes Hitler as "pale and sleepless, playing nervously with his glasses"; Rommel bluntly told him the struggle was "hopeless" against Allied air, sea, and land superiority and proposed withdrawal beyond range of Allied naval guns. Hitler refused, touting the V-1 (launched the previous day) as war-winning and promising "masses of jet fighters." An errant V-1 landed on the bunker roof after the meeting; though no one was hurt, it so unsettled Hitler that he fled to Berchtesgaden and never made his planned 19 June visit to Rommel's headquarters. At a second Obersalzberg meeting on 29 June, Hitler again dismissed Rundstedt and Rommel's appeals to face reality, lapsing into what Speidel called "fantastic digressions" about miracle weapons; Rundstedt was replaced as C-in-C West by Kluge on 1 July, precipitated partly by his blunt retort to Keitel: "Make peace, you fools. What else can you do?"

Rommel's 15 July letter to Hitler included the handwritten postscript: "I must beg you to draw the proper conclusions without delay. I feel it my duty as Commander in Chief of the Army Group to state this clearly." He told Speidel: "I have given him his last chance. If he does not take it, we will act." Two days later his staff car was strafed by Allied fighters near the front, critically wounding him and removing him from the conspiracy's active plans — Speidel judged he had by then "made up his mind irrevocably to do his part in ridding Germany of Hitler's rule (though still opposing his assassination)." For Rommel's subsequent forced suicide and the wider July conspiracy, see [[events/july-plot-1944]] and [[actors/german-resistance-to-nazism]].
