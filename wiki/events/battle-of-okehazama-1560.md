## Narrative

In 1560 Imagawa Yoshimoto, lord of Suruga, Tōtōmi, and Mikawa, led a large army estimated at around 25,000 men westward through Owari province with the stated intention of marching on the capital at Kyoto. The Matsudaira forces under the young Matsudaira Motoyasu (later [[tokugawa-ieyasu]]) served in the Imagawa van and took part in the assault on Oda border forts.

[[oda-nobunaga]], lord of Owari, left his castle of Kiyosu with only a handful of companions and a couple of hundred men, chanting a verse from the Noh play *Atsumori*: "Man's life is fifty years. In the Universe what is it but dream and illusion? Is there any who is born and does not die?" His force grew to over two thousand as he advanced. He deposited a prayer for victory at the Shinto Shrine of Atsuta — notably denouncing Yoshimoto as a destroyer of Shinto shrines — and proceeded toward the enemy position.

Yoshimoto had encamped at a place called Dengaku-hazama (also rendered Oke-hazama from the nearest hamlet), where he was receiving the severed heads of captured enemies in formal ceremony, "calling for some Noh choruses to be chanted the while, and declaring that neither God nor Devil dare meet his armies" (Sadler). His men were feasting and the watch was negligent.

Nobunaga ostentatiously positioned troops with dummy formations at Zenshoji near Narumi to fix the enemy's attention, then took three thousand men on a rapid flanking march to drop down on Yoshimoto's camp from the rear of the surrounding hills. A sudden violent thunderstorm screened his advance, and his force rushed down the slope as the storm broke, throwing the Imagawa camp into confusion. Yoshimoto, thinking the commotion was a quarrel among his own troops, shouted orders before realizing his position. He drew his sword, cut through a spearshaft with a single blow, wounding his attacker, but was then killed by a second opponent who took his head. "His army immediately melted away with more than two thousand five hundred casualties" (Sadler).

When news of Yoshimoto's defeat reached Matsudaira Motoyasu, he withdrew his forces cautiously to Mikawa after confirming the report, taking up quarters at the Daijuji temple outside Okazaki. The Imagawa garrison in Okazaki castle shortly evacuated, and Motoyasu occupied it on the twenty-third day of the fifth month of 1560.

## Causal Analysis

The immediate cause was Imagawa Yoshimoto's decision to rest his troops at an exposed position without adequate security precautions, apparently confident that no opponent would dare a frontal challenge to his large force. The enabling cause was the sudden thunderstorm that masked Nobunaga's approach. The deeper causal context was Nobunaga's recognition that his only viable option against an overwhelming force was a surprise assault on the headquarters, combined with his characteristic psychological disregard for conventional military decorum — "he doesn't look a bit as if he was just going into a fight" (Sadler, quoting Atsuta shrine priests watching him depart).

## Consequence Analysis

The consequences at the immediate scale were the destruction of Imagawa's army and the end of his power in Owari. At the consequential scale, the battle was "one of the decisive actions of Japanese history, in that it placed Nobunaga in the front rank of military leaders, and placed the acquisition of supreme power within his grasp" (Sadler).

For Ieyasu specifically, the battle was transformative: the destruction of his overlord gave him the opportunity to occupy Okazaki and establish himself as an independent lord in Mikawa for the first time. The alliance he concluded with Nobunaga in 1561 — unusual in a period when no feudal chiefs had trusted each other for over two hundred years — defined the strategic framework of his next two decades.

The successor Imagawa Ujizane is described as "devoted to Japanese verse and football, and even more given up to drink and dalliance, and without any ideas about retrieving his position" (Sadler), ensuring the Imagawa house would not recover its former power.

## Historiography

Sadler's account emphasizes the dramatic personal qualities of Nobunaga — his sang-froid, his irreverence, his willingness to act against all military convention. The source is the *Mikawa Go-Fudoki* and chronicle tradition compiled in the Tokugawa period, which had strong reasons to celebrate both Nobunaga (as Ieyasu's ally) and Ieyasu's adroit response to the battle. The tactical details (thunderstorm timing, dummy troops) are consistent across chronicle sources but cannot be independently verified. The 25,000-man figure for the Imagawa army should be treated as an approximation.
