## Narrative

The Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 was the last and largest of a series of samurai uprisings against the Meiji government, led by Saigō Takamori — the most celebrated warrior-statesman of the Restoration era. It was crushed by Japan's newly created conscript army, decisively ending the political and military primacy of the samurai class.

Saigō had been one of the three pre-eminent leaders of the Meiji Restoration alongside Kidō Kōin and [[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]]. He broke with the government in 1873 in the dispute over whether Japan should send a punitive expedition to Korea. After his resignation, he returned to his native Satsuma domain in Kyūshū, where he ran private military academies (shigakkō) that attracted thousands of former samurai deeply resentful of Meiji reforms — particularly the abolition of samurai stipends and the 1873 conscription law that opened military service to commoners.

The rebellion broke out in early 1877 when Satsuma samurai, fearing that government arms stored in their domain were about to be confiscated, launched a pre-emptive assault. Saigō placed himself at the head of the insurgency. The fighting was prolonged and intense, with government forces — largely conscript soldiers — sustaining heavy casualties before prevailing. Saigō died in the final engagement, either killed in battle or by suicide; tradition holds the latter. The government spent enormous sums to suppress the revolt, flooding the economy with non-convertible paper currency that generated severe inflation throughout the following years.

As Keene's account of the post-rebellion period makes clear, the financial aftershock of 1877 remained a dominant preoccupation of the Meiji cabinet into the early 1880s: "The first point was to redeem a considerable part of the paper money that had been printed to pay the expenses of the 1877 Satsuma Rebellion. The flood of banknotes had resulted in a loss of confidence in their value, and now a silver 1-yen coin was worth 1 yen, 43 sen in paper money."

## Causal Analysis

The rebellion had structural and proximate causes. Structurally, it arose from the displacement of the entire samurai class: loss of hereditary stipends, the replacement of domain armies by a national conscript force, the right to carry swords revoked, and old social hierarchies overturned. These changes concentrated particular resentment in Satsuma, which had been the most powerful domain and whose warriors had been the backbone of the Restoration itself.

The proximate cause was the 1873 political crisis over Korea. Saigō had advocated a bellicose approach to Korea, partly as a means of providing an outlet for disaffected samurai. When the caretaker government rejected his proposal upon the return of the Iwakura Mission, he and several key colleagues resigned. This concentrated former-samurai opposition around Saigō personally.

The rebellion thus represents: `caused_by: [[meiji-restoration-1868]]` in that it was the social convulsion produced by the Restoration's rapid dismantling of feudal structures; `contributed_to` by the inflation and dislocation of the early Meiji period; and `preceded_by` the Saga Rebellion of 1874 and other smaller samurai uprisings.

## Consequence Analysis

The defeat of the Satsuma Rebellion had transformative consequences:

1. **End of the samurai as a fighting force.** The conscript army's victory demonstrated definitively that professional warriors could be outfought by peasant soldiers. The samurai class was thereafter politically disarmed.

2. **Redirection of opposition to civilian channels.** Itagaki Taisuke, who had been a supporter of Saigō, drew the lesson explicitly: "The defeat of Saigō's forces by the government army made it clear that it was useless to attempt to oppose the government by military means; therefore, the Risshi-sha's message would be spread by speeches and newspaper articles, not swords." This accelerated the [[freedom-and-popular-rights-movement|Freedom and Popular Rights Movement]].

3. **Fiscal crisis.** The paper money issued to fund the war caused rampant inflation, destabilizing government finances and provoking a major political debate over whether to float a foreign loan, a debate that occupied the cabinet through 1880.

4. **Removal of the Meiji government's most dangerous critic.** With Saigō dead, the locus of opposition shifted from armed rebellion to parliamentary agitation — a shift the oligarchs could manage more easily.

5. **Posthumous rehabilitation.** In 1889, on the day the Meiji Constitution was promulgated, Saigō Takamori was posthumously promoted to the senior third rank, restoring his honor in the eyes of the state he had fought against.

## Actors

- **[[saigo-takamori|Saigō Takamori]]** — leader of the rebellion; previously the most celebrated general of the Restoration
- **[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]]** — head of the government forces; the rebellion was framed as lèse-majesté against imperial authority
- **[[itagaki-taisuke|Itagaki Taisuke]]** — former ally of Saigō who redirected his political energies to civilian rights agitation after the defeat
- **[[okubo-toshimichi|Ōkubo Toshimichi]]** — interior minister who directed the government's suppression of the rebellion; assassinated in 1878
- **Yamagata Aritomo** — the military organizer of the conscript army that defeated Saigō

## Historiography

Keene's treatment of the Satsuma Rebellion as background context (the rebellion itself fell in the range covered by earlier sections of the biography) emphasizes its fiscal and political aftermath rather than the military campaign. The rebellion appears throughout chapters 33–35 as the primary cause of the inflation crisis of 1879–1881, as the event that redirected samurai opposition into civilian channels, and as the defining shadow over Saigō's posthumous reputation.

The rebellion occupies a central place in Japanese national memory, with Saigō as a tragic hero figure — a man of premodern virtue destroyed by the modernizing state he had helped create. His rehabilitation in 1889 reflects both the government's desire for reconciliation on the day of constitutional promulgation and the genuine popular sympathy his memory commanded.
