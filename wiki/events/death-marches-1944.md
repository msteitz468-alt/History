# The Death Marches (1944–1945)

As Allied armies closed on the Reich, the SS evacuated concentration and extermination camps — most infamously [[auschwitz|Auschwitz]] in January 1945 — driving hundreds of thousands of starving prisoners on foot and in open rail cars toward camps in the interior, shooting stragglers en route. A quarter of a million or more died in this final phase of [[the-holocaust|the Holocaust]]. Documented for the wiki from [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans, *The Third Reich at War* (2008)]].

## Narrative

Per [[evans-third-reich-at-war-2008|Evans (2008)]]. ~700,000 prisoners remained in the camp system (662+ subcamps) at the start of 1945.

**Auschwitz.** The Birkenau gas chambers were closed on Himmler's order in November 1944 — after ~40,000 killings in October alone — and dismantled to destroy evidence. On 19 January 1945, 58,000 prisoners were marched west; SS guards shot stragglers where they fell (~15,000 died en route). Höss's own account describes columns "struggling through deep snow . . . no food" and corpses every few hundred yards (his claim to have shot a guard he caught killing a prisoner for sport is self-serving postwar testimony, flagged as such). The Sonderkommando witnesses melted into the columns and survived; Mengele absconded with his research files. The Red Army entered on **27 January 1945**: ~7,000 survivors, 600 corpses in the open, and storerooms holding 837,000 women's coats and dresses and 7.7 tonnes of human hair.

**The marches.** Jews were singled out on the roads (SS pulling Jewish prisoners from the Adler Works column to shoot; ~5,000 Stutthof prisoners, mostly Jewish women, shot on the beach at **Palmnicken** with 200–300 survivors); the Helmbrechts women's march wandered 195 miles, killing stragglers to the last days; Flossenbürg columns doubled back on themselves for up to 250 miles. At **Gardelegen** (April 1945) ~1,000 Dora prisoners were penned in a barn which police and Hitler Youth soaked with petrol and burned, the bodies still alight when US troops arrived. At Celle, townspeople and adolescents joined the hunt for prisoners scattered by a bombing raid. In Lübeck Bay on **3 May 1945**, RAF fighter-bombers, mistaking them for troop transports, sank the prison ships *Thielbek* (all but 50 of 2,800 dead) and *Cap Arcona* (~4,250 dead) — Gauleiter Kaufmann had loaded 14,000 Neuengamme prisoners aboard rather than let them loose in Hamburg; 400 of the 500 SS men aboard survived.

**The destination camps.** Buchenwald swelled to 100,000 (~14,000 deaths January–April 1945); Mauthausen's evacuee influx killed 45,000 from October 1944. **Bergen-Belsen**, converted from an "exchange" camp into a dumping ground for the unfit under Josef Kramer (ex-Birkenau), reached 60,000 people on sanitation built for 2,000 — "excrement lay on the barrack floors up to a metre thick," typhus raging, over 200 documented cases of cannibalism; ~35,000 died there in the first three and a half months of 1945, among them **Anne Frank** (typhus, March 1945). The British liberated it on 15 April but could not save another 14,000. At **Ohrdruf** (5 April 1945), the first camp Americans reached, Bradley — "More than 3,200 naked, emaciated bodies had been flung into shallow graves" — was physically sick; Eisenhower ordered every soldier in the area to tour it.

**Toll:** an estimated 200,000–350,000 prisoners died in the marches and destination camps — "up to half of the prisoners who were held in the camp system in January 1945 . . . were dead four months later."

## Historiography

Evans argues the marches were "not, as has sometimes been claimed, simply the last chapter of the 'Final Solution'": non-Jewish inmates, state prisoners, and forced laborers died in them too, and they are best understood as "the last act in the brutal and violent history of the Third Reich's system of repression in general" — though Jews were demonstrably singled out for killing along the routes. (Blatman's *The Death Marches* is the standard monograph; Evans's framing is one position in a live interpretive question about Holocaust periodization.)

## Wachsmann, *KL* (2015)

Wachsmann's account (from the history of the [[nazi-concentration-camps|KL
system]]) reinforces and refines Evans. He shows that the evacuations began
**earlier than 1945**: an orderly, low-death first wave in the west (Herzogenbusch,
Natzweiler, autumn 1944) contrasted with lethal, chaotic evacuations in the east
already in 1944 (Majdanek, the Baltic camps Riga/Kovno/Vaivara, with last-minute
massacres at Klooga and Lagedi). The great treks of January–February 1945 forced
>150,000 out of Auschwitz, Gross-Rosen, and Stutthof; ~1 in 4 Auschwitz prisoners
died. He documents the largest march massacres — **Palmnicken** (~3,000 Stutthof
women machine-gunned on the Baltic shore) and **Lieberose** — but stresses that
murder was **not** the SS default: the sick were as often simply left behind, and
Himmler wanted Jews kept alive as hostages. On the periodization question,
Wachsmann agrees with Evans against the "last chapter of the Final Solution"
reading: there were **no genocidal orders from above** during the evacuations,
Jews (a third to a half of marchers) were not treated fundamentally differently
from other prisoners, and "the main purpose of the KL evacuations was not the
murder of Jews or other prisoners" — mass death was an inevitable result, not the
aim. Yet he insists the transports remain **part of the camps' history**, run by
Camp SS men accustomed to killing stragglers, not a separate phase. The worst
disaster came at the very end: the sinking of the *Cap Arcona* and *Thielbek* in
Neustadt Bay (3 May 1945), >7,000 dead. Source:
[[wachsmann-kl-concentration-camps-2015]].
