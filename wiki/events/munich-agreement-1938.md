# Munich Agreement (1938)

The four-power settlement (Germany, Britain, France, Italy — Czechoslovakia
excluded) ceding the Sudetenland to Germany, signed 30 September 1938 at the
climax of the Czech crisis. The apogee of appeasement: Chamberlain's "peace for
our time." For Hitler it was a diplomatic triumph experienced as a defeat — he
had wanted his war against Czechoslovakia in 1938 and was maneuvered into a
bloodless partial gain, which he liquidated by seizing Prague in March 1939
([[occupation-of-czecho-slovakia-1939]]), destroying appeasement's premises.

## Narrative (Evans, 2005)

Hitler told his generals on 28 May 1938 he was "utterly determined that
Czechoslovakia should disappear from the map" — the first aggression that
could not be dressed as Versailles revision. Goebbels ran an invented
atrocity campaign through the summer; Chamberlain flew (his first flight) to
Berchtesgaden in mid-September, conceded boundary revision, and was met at
Bad Godesberg (22 September) with escalated demands. War loomed for 1
October. The exit came from [[goring-hermann|Göring]] — appalled at war over
an issue already conceded, he brokered the conference behind Hitler's back
(the draft agreement written via Weizsäcker, concealed from
[[ribbentrop-joachim-von|Ribbentrop]]) — and from
[[mussolini-benito|Mussolini]], whose postponement request gave Hitler a
face-saving ladder. Hitler signed on 29 September feeling "cheated of the
war"; the affair strengthened Ribbentrop and Himmler at Göring's and the
army's expense.

**German popular mood** is central to Evans's account: the prolonged
"war psychosis" of summer 1938 (bank runs, border flight, spiking Special
Court prosecutions) contrasted with every earlier crisis; Shirer described
Berliners' silence at the 27 September military parade as "the most striking
demonstration against war I've ever seen." Relief at Munich was enormous —
evidence, for Evans, that the drive to war came from the top, not from
popular demand, and (per Hitler's own remarkable 10 November 1938 admission
to the press that years of peace propaganda had left Germans dangerously
pacifist) that even the regime knew it.

**Aftermath in the Sudetenland:** ~8,000 ethnic German and 2,000 Czech
anti-Nazis arrested; ~225,000 fled in waves; the Sudeten Jewish population
fell from 22,000 to under 2,000 by May 1939 as the Nuremberg Laws arrived.
Poland took Teschen (2 October); Hungary gained by the Vienna Award
(2 November); Slovak autonomy (7 October) pre-loaded the state's destruction
in March 1939.

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: the manufactured Sudeten crisis (Henlein's party instructed to
  make unfulfillable demands); enabled by Anglo-French unpreparedness and
  memory of 1914.
- contributed_to: [[causes-of-the-second-world-war]] — both as delay
  (rearmament breathing space, contested) and as confirmation to Hitler that
  the West would not fight.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: [[occupation-of-czecho-slovakia-1939]] — Munich left rump
  Czecho-Slovakia defenceless.

## Historiography

The appeasement debate ("Guilty Men" vs. Taylor-era revisionism vs.
post-revisionist accounts, e.g. Watt, *How War Came*) is one of the largest in
modern diplomatic history; Evans stresses German popular relief at peace —
war anxiety in September 1938 was acute inside Germany.
