# Munich Agreement (1938)

The four-power settlement (Germany, Britain, France, Italy — Czechoslovakia
excluded) ceding the Sudetenland to Germany, signed 30 September 1938 at the
climax of the Czech crisis. The apogee of appeasement: Chamberlain's "peace for
our time." For Hitler it was a diplomatic triumph experienced as a defeat — he
had wanted his war against Czechoslovakia in 1938 and was maneuvered into a
bloodless partial gain, which he liquidated by seizing Prague in March 1939
([[occupation-of-czecho-slovakia-1939]]), destroying appeasement's premises.

## Narrative (Evans, 2005)

Hitler told his generals on 28 May 1938 he was "utterly determined that
Czechoslovakia should disappear from the map" — the first aggression that
could not be dressed as Versailles revision. Goebbels ran an invented
atrocity campaign through the summer; Chamberlain flew (his first flight) to
Berchtesgaden in mid-September, conceded boundary revision, and was met at
Bad Godesberg (22 September) with escalated demands. War loomed for 1
October. The exit came from [[goring-hermann|Göring]] — appalled at war over
an issue already conceded, he brokered the conference behind Hitler's back
(the draft agreement written via Weizsäcker, concealed from
[[ribbentrop-joachim-von|Ribbentrop]]) — and from
[[mussolini-benito|Mussolini]], whose postponement request gave Hitler a
face-saving ladder. Hitler signed on 29 September feeling "cheated of the
war"; the affair strengthened Ribbentrop and Himmler at Göring's and the
army's expense.

**German popular mood** is central to Evans's account: the prolonged
"war psychosis" of summer 1938 (bank runs, border flight, spiking Special
Court prosecutions) contrasted with every earlier crisis; Shirer described
Berliners' silence at the 27 September military parade as "the most striking
demonstration against war I've ever seen." Relief at Munich was enormous —
evidence, for Evans, that the drive to war came from the top, not from
popular demand, and (per Hitler's own remarkable 10 November 1938 admission
to the press that years of peace propaganda had left Germans dangerously
pacifist) that even the regime knew it.

**Aftermath in the Sudetenland:** ~8,000 ethnic German and 2,000 Czech
anti-Nazis arrested; ~225,000 fled in waves; the Sudeten Jewish population
fell from 22,000 to under 2,000 by May 1939 as the Nuremberg Laws arrived.
Poland took Teschen (2 October); Hungary gained by the Vienna Award
(2 November); Slovak autonomy (7 October) pre-loaded the state's destruction
in March 1939.

## Chronology and Terms (Paxton and Hessler)

Chamberlain flew to Germany three times in fourteen days — Berchtesgaden
(September 15), Godesberg (September 22), and Munich (September 29, 1938) —
his first-ever flights, undertaken at age 69. The crisis had been building
since the Karlsbad Program of April 1938, in which Konrad Henlein's Sudeten
German Party demanded internal autonomy, reparations, and ideological
liberty for the Sudeten Germans; at a Berchtesgaden meeting shortly after
the Anschluss, Hitler privately instructed Henlein to "always demand so much
that we can never be satisfied."

A war scare on May 20–21, 1938 saw Czechoslovak mobilization in response to
false reports of a German troop buildup, French and Soviet public
reaffirmation of their treaty commitments, and a warning from Lord Halifax
that Britain might not stand aside. Hitler's military order of May 30,
1938 stated his "unalterable intention to smash Czechoslovakia by military
action in the near future," pending "adequate political justification" —
this predates and is causally prior to the September crisis, meaning Hitler
had committed to force in principle months before the negotiated settlement,
which is central to reading Munich as a delay rather than a genuine
solution.

At Godesberg, Hitler's reply to Chamberlain's return with Czech acceptance
of the Berchtesgaden terms was *"Das geht nicht mehr"* ("that's no longer
enough") — he now demanded immediate transfer of the Sudetenland within
three days, plus immediate troop entry. At Munich, the Sudetenland
(population over 50% German) was transferred immediately; plebiscites were
promised elsewhere but never held; 2,825,000 Germans and 800,000 Czechs were
ultimately forcibly transferred to Germany. Czechoslovakia and the USSR were
excluded from the four-power conference (Britain, France, Germany, Italy —
Mussolini proposed the format); Beneš held out until 5:00 PM on September 21
before yielding to Anglo-French pressure.

Within six months, Poland and Hungary took Czech border territories with
their own nationals (Poland took Teschen), and Hitler broke the agreement
outright, occupying Prague on March 15, 1939 and creating the Protectorate
of Bohemia-Moravia alongside an "independent" Slovakia — see
[[occupation-of-czecho-slovakia-1939]].

Paxton and Hessler's historiographical judgment is blunt: "the Munich
settlement has had no defenders, on either moral or pragmatic grounds." But
they treat the *alternative* — preventive war alongside the USSR, contingent
on Soviet forces being allowed to cross Polish or Romanian territory (which
Poland refused), and dependent on Stalin's purge-weakened officer corps and
untested intentions — as a genuinely live counterfactual rather than a clean
"lost opportunity." `contributed_to: [[appeasement]]` — Munich is
appeasement's high-water application, not identical to the concept as a
whole. Source: [[paxton-europe-twentieth-century-2011]].

## Historiography

The appeasement debate ("Guilty Men" vs. Taylor-era revisionism vs.
post-revisionist accounts, e.g. Watt, *How War Came*) is one of the largest in
modern diplomatic history; Evans stresses German popular relief at peace —
war anxiety in September 1938 was acute inside Germany.

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer supplies the documentary premeditation chain Paxton and Hessler's account (above) also stresses: Case Green was first drawn up 24 June 1937 by Blomberg; Hitler ordered it updated after the Anschluss at a 21 April 1938 meeting with Keitel, recorded by aide Major Rudolf Schmundt, favoring "lightning action based on an incident (for example, the murder of the German minister...)" over a "strategic attack out of the blue." The directive of 20 May 1938 (Keitel to Hitler) originally read "It is not my intention to smash Czechoslovakia by military action in the immediate future without provocation"; the revised directive Hitler signed 30 May 1938 changed the opening to "It is my unalterable decision to smash Czechoslovakia by military action in the near future," with Keitel's covering letter fixing execution "by October 1, 1938, at the latest" — Shirer treats this wording change as his proof-text for premeditation (Shirer 1960).

The "May Crisis" (20–23 May 1938) is covered in more documentary depth than currently on this page: rumors of German troop concentration triggered a Czech partial mobilization; Britain, France, and the USSR displayed unusual firmness; Hitler, humiliated, had the Foreign Office deny aggressive intent on 23 May. Captured German documents (Jodl/OKW telegrams of 21 May) show no actual troop concentration beyond peacetime maneuvers — the "crisis" was based on false intelligence, which Shirer treats as a proximate trigger for the harder 30 May directive.

At the 28 May 1938 Chancellery conference (Goering, Keitel, Brauchitsch, Beck, Raeder, Ribbentrop, Neurath), Hitler declared "It is my unshakable will that Czechoslovakia shall be wiped off the map!" and ordered military preparations for attack "by October 2," with immediate mobilization of 96 divisions.

[SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer states his own authorial judgment plainly: "It was this writer's impression in Berlin from that moment until the end that had Chamberlain frankly told Hitler that Britain would do what it ultimately did in the face of Nazi aggression, the Fuehrer would never have embarked on the adventures which brought on the Second World War... This was the well-meaning Prime Minister's fatal mistake."

[EYEWITNESS] At Bad Godesberg, Shirer, breakfasting on the terrace of the Hotel Dreesen (22 September), observed Hitler pass by with a nervous tic and "ugly, black patches under his eyes"; a German editor companion muttered "Teppichfresser!" ("carpet eater") — the whispered nickname circulating among party hacks for Hitler's reported habit of hurling himself to the floor and chewing carpet edges during rages that week. At the Godesberg meeting Hitler rejected the Berchtesgaden-agreed terms on arrival, demanding immediate military occupation with evacuation to begin 8am, 26 September, complete by 28 September; Chamberlain protested "But this is nothing less than an ultimatum!" — Hitler: "Nothing of the sort... It is not a Diktat at all. Look, the document is headed by the word 'Memorandum.'" Shirer watched their farewell from a vantage point 25 feet away in a porter's booth used as a temporary broadcast studio, noting apparent cordiality despite the day's clash.

[EYEWITNESS] At the 26 September 1938 Sportpalast speech, Shirer broadcast from a balcony above Hitler and recorded in his diary that night: "For the first time in all the years I've observed him he seemed tonight to have completely lost control of himself... he leaped to his feet... pounding down on the table, and yelled with all the power in his mighty lungs: 'Ja!'" Goebbels had shouted "One thing is sure: 1918 will never be repeated!" moments before. Hitler declared he would have the Sudetenland by 1 October regardless: "We want no Czechs!" Two days later Hitler told Sir Horace Wilson "If France and England strike, let them do so! It's a matter of complete indifference to me. Today is Tuesday; by next Monday we shall be at war."

[EYEWITNESS] Shirer's 27 September 1938 diary account of Berliners' response to the "war psychosis" parade (already partly quoted on this page via Evans) is given in fuller context: a motorized division marched through Berlin at dusk to whip up war fever; Berliners "ducked into the subways, refused to look on"; fewer than 200 people watched Hitler review troops from the Chancellery balcony. Shirer's diary: "It has been the most striking demonstration against war I've ever seen... What I've seen tonight almost rekindles a little faith in the German people. They are dead set against war." He records hard data on the military balance the same evening: the German military attaché in Paris warned Berlin that French partial mobilization amounted almost to total mobilization, projecting 65 French divisions on the German frontier within six days against barely a dozen German divisions (half reserve, of doubtful value); the German military attaché in Prague estimated total Czech call-up at 1,000,000, field army 800,000.

Shirer gives the "Black Wednesday" mediation sequence in more detail: Mussolini's intercepted phone call to Attolico (28 September, noon) — "Ask immediately for an interview with the Chancellor... tell him I favor accepting the suggestion... Hurry!" — and Hitler's reply, "Tell the Duce that I accept his proposal." [SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer disputes the standard credit given to Mussolini for "saving the peace": "surely this is being overgenerous... Chamberlain, not Mussolini, made Munich possible," since German generals treated Italian military strength as negligible. He also documents that the "Italian proposals" tabled at Munich by Mussolini as his own compromise were in fact drafted the day before in the German Foreign Office (Goering, Neurath, Weizsäcker, behind Ribbentrop's back), translated into French by Dr. Schmidt, and telephoned to Rome for Mussolini to present as his own — unknown to Chamberlain "I believe, to his death."

Two Czech representatives (Dr. Vojtech Mastny, Dr. Hubert Masarik) were kept waiting in an adjoining room at Munich from 2pm to 7pm on 29 September with no access to the negotiations; Masarik's official report to Prague, quoted verbatim by Shirer: "The atmosphere was oppressive; sentence was about to be passed... Mr. Chamberlain was yawning continuously, without making any effort to conceal his yawns." [EYEWITNESS] Shirer's diary entry after the signing: Daladier "looked a completely beaten and broken man... he stumbled out the door in silence" when asked if he was satisfied, contrasted with Hitler's "light of victory" and Chamberlain's "yawns and air of pleasant sleepiness." The morning after, Chamberlain sought a second private meeting with Hitler (sole witness: Dr. Schmidt) and produced the Anglo-German Declaration ("peace in our time" paper); Hitler signed "with a certain reluctance... only to please Chamberlain," per Schmidt — and, unknown to Chamberlain, Hitler and Mussolini had already agreed at this same Munich meeting that they would eventually fight "side by side" against Britain (Shirer 1960).

Postwar Nuremberg testimony of German generals (Keitel, Manstein, Jodl), cited at length by Shirer, is a major addition to the historiography already on this page: Keitel testified "we lacked the means for an attack which involved the piercing of the frontier fortifications"; Manstein: "neither our western border nor our Polish frontier could really have been effectively defended by us... had Czechoslovakia defended herself, we would have been held up by her fortifications"; Jodl, with 5 fighting plus 7 reserve divisions in the west, judged holding against 100 French divisions "militarily impossible." Shirer's own summary: "Germany was in no position to go to war on October 1, 1938... Had she done so, she would have been quickly and easily defeated, and that would have been the end of Hitler and the Third Reich" — this generals'-testimony evidence base is the strongest documentary support in the wiki for the "Munich as lost opportunity" position already flagged as contested by Paxton and Hessler above; the two views should stand side by side rather than be adjudicated in wiki voice. [SHIRER-THESIS] Shirer also directly rebuts the "Munich bought time for rearmament" argument via Churchill's own verdict: "The year's breathing space said to be 'gained' by Munich left Britain and France in a much worse position compared to Hitler's Germany than they had been at the Munich crisis."

Hard territorial/economic data (final settlement, 20 November 1938): Czechoslovakia ceded 11,000 sq mi containing 2,800,000 Sudeten Germans and 800,000 Czechs (figures the wiki's Evans-sourced material above gives similarly), plus all Czech fortifications, rated second only to the Maginot Line in Europe; Czechoslovakia lost 66% of coal, 80% lignite, 86% chemicals, 80% cement, 80% textiles, 70% iron/steel, 70% electric power, 40% timber. Poland took ~650 sq mi around Teschen (228,000 population, 133,000 Czechs) at Foreign Minister Józef Beck's insistence; Hungary got 7,500 sq mi (500,000 Magyars, 272,000 Slovaks) awarded 2 November by Ribbentrop/Ciano (Shirer 1960).

Hitler's own regret, per Schacht's account: on returning to Berlin after Munich Hitler exclaimed to his SS entourage, "That fellow [Chamberlain] has spoiled my entry into Prague!" — evidence, per Shirer, that full military conquest rather than merely the Sudetenland had been his real objective all along, directly supporting this page's framing of Munich as "a diplomatic triumph experienced as a defeat." A German diplomat reported to Berlin four days after Munich (3 October 1938) that Stalin would "reconsider her foreign policy" and become "more positive" toward Germany after being excluded from the settlement — the first documentary hint, per Shirer, of what would become the [[molotov-ribbentrop-pact-1939|Nazi-Soviet turn]].
