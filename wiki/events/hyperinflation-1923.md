# German Hyperinflation of 1923

The catastrophic collapse of the German currency, culminating in late 1923 when
the mark fell to trillions per dollar, wiping out cash savings and fixed
incomes and searing itself into German collective memory.

## Narrative

Sourced from Evans ([[evans-coming-of-third-reich-2003]]). Reich deficit
financing began in 1916, premised on annexationist expectations of recouping
war costs from the defeated — reversed when Germany itself lost. The dollar
went from ~4 marks pre-war to 8 (Dec 1918), 47 (end 1919), 263 (Nov 1921), 493
(July 1922). When the [[ruhr-occupation-crisis-1923|Ruhr occupation]] and
government-financed passive resistance hit in January 1923, collapse became
free fall: 17,000 marks per dollar (January) → 353,000 (July) → 98.86 million
(September) → 25.26 billion (October) → 4.2 trillion (December). Money "lost
its meaning almost completely": municipalities printed one-sided emergency
notes; wages were collected in wheelbarrows and spent within hours; a Daily
Mail correspondent watched a gramophone rise from 5 million marks at 10 a.m.
to 12 million by 3 p.m. Rye bread went from 163 marks (3 January) to 233
billion (19 November). At the peak over 90% of average family spending went on
food; malnutrition drove tuberculosis deaths up. [[klemperer-victor|Victor
Klemperer]]'s diary supplies the lived texture: nearly a million marks of
professorial salary by May yet unpaid gas bills; "Germany is collapsing in an
eerie, step-by-step manner... How long will we still have something to eat?"

Stabilization came with Stresemann's fulfilment policy, the gold-pegged
Rentenmark (15 November 1923), Hjalmar Schacht's appointment to the Reichsbank
(22 December), and the [[dawes-plan-1924|Dawes Plan]].

## Causal Analysis

- caused_by: war deficit finance from 1916 and lost industrial regions
  (1919 output 42% of 1913); [[treaty-of-versailles-1919]] gold-denominated
  reparations exporting scarce revenue.
- caused_by: [[ruhr-occupation-crisis-1923]] — the trigger tipping chronic
  inflation into hyperinflation via money-printed passive resistance
  (feedback loop: the arrears causing the occupation were themselves products
  of currency decay).
- Political trap (Evans): raising taxes read as taxing Germans to pay the
  Allies, so governments agitated for rescheduling instead.

## Consequence Analysis

- produced: [[dawes-plan-1924]] and the Stresemann-era stabilization.
- Uneven ruin (Evans revises the "destroyed the middle class" thesis):
  bond-holders and fixed-income creditors lost everything; mortgage debtors
  acquired property "for virtually nothing." But "popular memory conflated
  the effects of the inflation, the hyperinflation and the stabilization into
  a single economic catastrophe in which virtually every group in German
  society was a loser" (attributed) — contributed_to
  [[weimar-democracy-and-its-collapse]].
- Crime as subsistence: theft convictions 115,000 (1913) → 365,000 (1923)
  while wounding convictions *fell* from 113,000 to 35,000 — disorder was
  about food, not violence (Evans).
- Cultural-political residue: fed Weimar's fascination with criminals,
  gamblers, and swindlers (Dr Mabuse, Felix Krull) and, per Evans, "lent new
  power to stock fantasy-images of evil... the speculator and, fatefully, the
  financially manipulative Jew" — contributed_to [[german-antisemitism]].
  On 6 November 1923 a Berlin mob looted a Jewish-immigrant district shouting
  "Beat the Jews to death!"; an eyewitness reporter judged it "inflamed racial
  hatred, not hunger, that is driving them to loot."
- Post-stabilization deflation: Stinnes's empire collapsed; consolidation
  produced United Steelworks (1924) and I.G. Farben (1924) — Europe's largest
  corporation, fourth in the world.

## Historiography

Evans calls it "the greatest hyperinflation ever" — prices a billion times
prewar, against 14,000× in Austria, 23,000× in Hungary, 2.5 million× in
Poland. He disputes the claim that industrialists deliberately drove the
inflation for gain ("the gains they made... were not as spectacular as has
often been alleged"). His central interpretive move is the memory point:
objective losses were uneven, but the remembered catastrophe was universal —
which is what mattered politically in 1929–33.
