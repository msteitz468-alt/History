## Narrative

The Dawes Plan of 1924 was the international financial framework that stabilized the [[weimar-republic]] after the hyperinflation crisis of 1923. Named after the American banker Charles G. Dawes (who chaired the commission), it restructured German reparations payments on a graduated scale and organized a large American loan to stabilize the German currency and balance of payments. Source: [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18.

The crisis the Plan addressed was triggered by the French occupation of the Ruhr in January 1923 (after Germany failed to make timber and coal reparations deliveries). The Weimar government financed passive resistance by printing money; hyperinflation destroyed savings, wiped out middle-class wealth, and brought the interconnected system of production and exchange to the edge of collapse. By autumn 1923, the logic of self-preservation reasserted itself: "Too many people both inside and outside Germany had too much to lose to allow a complete collapse of Germany and the disruption of its economic engine."

## Architecture

The Dawes Plan's architecture had three interlocking elements:
1. **American private credit**: Wall Street opened approximately $3 billion in long-term credit to Germany over the following years, in the form of both long-term loans and shorter-term capital movements. American banks took major stakes in German industrial firms; GM bought Opel; Ford established manufacturing in Cologne.
2. **Reparations restructuring**: Payments were calibrated to German capacity and backed by specific German assets (including the Reichsbahn, which was mortgaged to reparations creditors).
3. **Return to the gold standard**: Germany returned to convertibility under the new Rentenmark/Reichsmark, stabilizing prices and exchange rates.

## Stresemann's Strategic Logic

Gustav Stresemann, as Foreign Minister, embraced this architecture not merely as economic necessity but as a deliberate foreign policy strategy. His reasoning: "Having failed in 1917 to stop the extension of American economic power to Europe by means of the U Boat, Stresemann now welcomed American capital as the foundation for a post-military security strategy." Germany's indebtedness to Wall Street would make America into Germany's protector. As the American stake grew, Washington would have an interest in seeing the most onerous reparations obligations lifted. "The lasting ties of economic interdependence would substitute for the dangerous system of military alliances" ([[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

This was the strategic architecture that produced the Locarno Treaties (1925) and Germany's admission to the League of Nations (1926).

## Economic Performance and Fragility

The Dawes framework enabled expansion of the welfare state (unemployment insurance, 1927), industrial recovery, and integration of German corporations into global market-sharing agreements (IG Farben, Siemens, AEG with American counterparts). The balance of payments showed persistent trade deficits offset by capital inflows — visible in retrospect as structural fragility (Table 18.1, [[smith-oxford-modern-german-history-2011]], ch. 18).

The Plan's weakness was its dependence on continued American capital flows. When the cyclical downturn began in 1928 and American lending dried up after the Wall Street Crash (October 1929), the entire architecture collapsed. The Dawes Plan was superseded by the Young Plan (1929), which further reduced reparations — but by then the Depression was already undermining the political framework that Stresemann had built.
