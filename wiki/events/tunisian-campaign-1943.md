# Tunisian Campaign 1943

The final phase of the Allied conquest of North Africa, culminating in the surrender of Axis forces (primarily German Fifth Panzer Army and remnants of Rommel's Afrika Korps / Italian First Army) on May 13, 1943. The campaign tested the maturing "novice" Allied logistics system established after Torch and directly enabled follow-on operations.

## Narrative

After Torch landings (Nov 8, 1942), the Allies failed to seize Tunis before Axis reinforcement. By mid-November the British First Army was 50 miles from Tunis but stalled by "the combination of resource constraints, weather, and increasing Axis strength." Limited port throughput, transportation shortages, a single rail line, and rains forced Eisenhower to pause. "Eisenhower decided to wait out the winter and provide time to build his force."

In January 1943 the British Eighth Army (pursuing from El Alamein) linked up from the east. Rommel commanded ~100k Axis (74k German) but at reduced strength (half authorized; one-third equipment operational). Germans had shorter LOCs but inferior air and supply. Allies held a 250-mile front with three corps.

Rommel struck west at Kasserine Pass (Feb 14-22, 1943) with 21st and 10th Panzer Divisions. US service units destroyed or abandoned supplies (e.g., 55k gallons fuel at Feriana ignited). II Corps fell back to Tébessa. Rommel gained Kasserine but "delays allowed II Corps and the British to reinforce"; by Feb 22 "German supplies were running low" with "only a few days’ worth of food and ammunition remaining and little reserve fuel." Rommel withdrew. US losses: 183 tanks, 194 half-tracks, 208 artillery pieces, 512 trucks. Critically, "even though the American forces lost more armored vehicles than the Germans had attacked with in total, these losses were quickly replaced. The American logistical system was finding its stride."

After Kasserine, Hitler unified German command under Rommel (Army Group Afrika) but recalled him March 10 for health reasons; von Arnim took over.

April reorganization: British shifted forces; US II Corps (now under Omar Bradley after Fredendall) moved 140 miles north to northern Tunisia (Apr 14-18) in a complete supply line shift. Eastern BS supported via negotiated rail allocations (250 tons/day deals with British). Stockage goals: 21 days supplies + 6 units of fire for II Corps.

**Sustainment innovations in April:** Eastern BS pipelines (Philippeville–Ouled Rahmoun: 500 tons fuel/day = 200 truck equivalents; Bône–Souk el Arba; combined 1,200 tpd, freeing 480 trucks). April fuel to US forces: >7.8 million gallons. Sabotage of exposed pipelines stopped by contracting French (with fines passed to Arab caliphs); "Almost overnight the shooting and vandalism stopped."

Allied attack opened Apr 19-20 (British Eighth Army). British First Army main effort Apr 22. II Corps attacked east Apr 23 toward Mateur/Bizerte through rough Djebel terrain (some mule transport for final miles). Axis fuel shortages immobilized tanks; limited air/ferry deliveries (May 4: 1,130 t ammo + 180 t fuel) too little. Mateur fell May 3; Bizerte (II Corps) and Tunis (British First) May 8-9. Southern flank collapsed May 12; final surrender May 13. Afrika Korps last message: "Ammunition shot off. Arms and Equipment destroyed. ... D. A. K. has fought itself to the condition where it can fight no more."

Allied forces responsible for ~275,000 Axis POWs. US forces in theater grew from 128,567 (Nov 1942) to 395,461 (Apr 1943).

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Torch logistics shortfalls (truck/shipping limits) that prevented rapid exploitation to Tunis in Nov-Dec 1942 (see [[operation-torch-1942]]). 

`contributed_to:` Kasserine German offensive (Feb 1943) exploiting US inexperience and forward supply dumps. Axis air/sea interdiction failures and lack of unified sustainment command (noted by Kesselring and post-war German accounts).

`enabled:` April Allied buildup via base sections, pipelines, truck regiments (46th QM controlling 6,000+ troops), and rail coordination.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Complete Axis expulsion from Africa; massive POW haul; secure base for Husky. "The surrender of the Axis forces in Tunisia signaled an opportunity to reset Allied forces, plan for the next operation, and discuss the lessons of the past six months."

`enabled:` [[operation-husky-1943]] (Sicily invasion <2 months later using forces/airfields already in theater; supplies already moving).

`contributed_to:` Maturation of US/ Allied sustainment: "Enabling this victory was the establishment of a functioning communications zone." Base sections proved flexible; SOS responded to crises (e.g., new truck units post-Kasserine).

The campaign showed "the battle is fought and decided by the quartermasters before the shooting begins" (Rommel). Logistics, not just combat power, determined the outcome.

## Actors

- [[eisenhower-dwight-d]]: Overall Allied commander; paused for winter buildup; reorganized for final offensive.
- [[patton-george-s]]: Assumed II Corps command post-Kasserine; restored discipline.
- [[larkin-thomas]]: SOS NATOUSA commander; oversaw base section support to II Corps and Eastern BS.
- Other key service leaders: Brig. Gen. Arthur Pence (Eastern BS); Maj. Gen. Humfrey Gale (British); Everett Hughes (US deputy theater commander).
- Axis: Rommel (initial), von Arnim (final), Kesselring (higher command).

## Historiography

Dworak (Range 01) emphasizes sustainment as decisive: Allied interdiction of Axis sea LOCs (230 ships lost Mar-May); rapid US replacement capacity vs. German/Italian collapse (fuel <50 tpd for Rommel at points). German post-war accounts stress supply breakdown, lack of combined command, failure to take Malta, and inability to interdict Allied shipping. The "novice" phase ended with victory; lessons on balanced forces, pipelines, rail/port throughput, and coalition sustainment directly informed later Mediterranean ops.
