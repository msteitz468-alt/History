# Foundation of Rome and the Regal Period

## Status of the "Foundation"

The traditional date of **753 BCE** (Varronian chronology, the standard used
throughout [[walbank-cah-v7-2-1989|CAH VII.2]]) was fixed by antiquarians of the
late Republic (Atticus, Varro) working backward from the consular list to place
the monarchy's end c. 509–506 BCE and then adding ~244 years of seven kings with
suspiciously regular ~35-year reign averages. **This date is not independently
attested** and archaeology does not confirm a discrete foundation event: the
Forum, Palatine, and other hills show traces of occupation from at least the
tenth century BCE and the Forum necropolis was in use through the ninth and early
eighth centuries. The *Regia* (royal shrine-dwelling) shows phases from c. 630 BCE.

What the evidence does support is a **process** — the gradual formation of an urban
center from a cluster of villages on the hills above the Tiber crossing, completed
structurally with the paving of the Forum (c. 635–575 BCE) and the first
monumental temples (Sant' Omobono, the Capitoline temple). The traditional "founding"
date is best understood as the retrospective **legitimation date** adopted by the
Roman tradition, which required a precise origin for the city's augural
constitution.

## The Band-Chief Context

The regal period's most historically grounded feature is the **band-chief
phenomenon**: non-hereditary kings drawn from the mobile aristocracy of central
Italy, confirmed by inauguration rites and the senate but wielding primarily
military authority. See [[roman-kings]] for analysis.

Key episodes with some archaeological or documentary support:
- The **Tarquins** (Priscus, Servius Tullius, Superbus): Etruscan dynasts who
  transformed Rome into a monumental city with town-planning, the Capitoline
  temple, and a reformed army. The François Tomb (Vulci) and the *Lapis
  Satricanus* provide contemporary epigraphic confirmation of figures connected
  to this era.
- The **Pyrgi dedication** (bilingual Etruscan-Phoenician, c. 500 BCE) confirms
  the Etruscan–Carthaginian alliance in the western Mediterranean, the context
  of the **First Treaty of Rome with Carthage** (Polybius III.22, which Polybius
  dates to the first year of the Republic, 509 BCE, though this date is disputed).
- The **Servian agger**: the earth rampart enclosing the Quirinal-Viminal-Esquiline
  may predate the stone "Servian Wall" (definitively fourth century); a fragment
  of Attic pottery c. 490–470 BCE from the earthwork is insufficient to date
  the whole to the fifth century.
- **Porsenna's sack of Rome** (c. 508 BCE): the embarrassing counter-tradition
  that Porsenna actually *took* Rome (Tacitus; Pliny, *HN* xxxiv.139) is almost
  certainly true and was suppressed by the main narrative (which instead
  celebrated Horatius, Scaevola, and Cloelia). The Romans were ultimately
  liberated by the Latin League and Aristodemus of Cumae, not by their own
  resistance.

## Structural Significance

The regal period laid the foundations for the Republican order in three ways:

1. **Army and assembly**: The *centuriate organization* (whatever its sixth-century
   form) combined military organization with political participation, creating the
   basis for a citizen-army state in which property determined military and
   eventually political weight.

2. **Clan structure**: The *gens* system (personal name + clan name) spread through
   all social classes from c. 700 BCE. The aristocracy solidified as a **patriciate**
   by monopolizing priesthoods and senate access during the monarchy; the Republic
   inherited this structured inequality and spent two centuries (the "Struggle of
   the Orders") dismantling it.

3. **Latin hegemony**: At the transition to the Republic, Rome already claimed
   hegemony over the Latin cities (confirmed by the Carthaginian treaty's terms).
   The loss of this hegemony and its gradual recovery (culminating in the Latin
   War of 340–338 BCE) structures the early-Republican period.

## Causal Analysis

The formation of Rome as a city-state was caused primarily by the convergence of:
- Geographic advantage (Tiber crossing, salt trade, access routes)
- The general urbanizing process of [[latial-culture|Latial Culture]] and its
  Etruscan parallel
- Etruscan dynastic influence (the Tarquins as the catalysts of monumental urbanism)
- Greek and Phoenician trade contact via the Tiber emporia (sanctuaries at
  Sant' Omobono, Gravisca)

The specific institutional form — non-hereditary kingship, aristocratic senate,
curiate assembly, centuriate army — emerged from the interaction of these forces
and the particular instability of a frontier city constantly exposed to ambitious
outsiders.

## Historiography

See [[early-roman-source-criticism]] for the systematic discussion. The single
most important methodological point for this event is that **the literary tradition
for the regal period was almost entirely created in the late Republic** (the
annalists of c. 150–50 BCE) and must be treated as hypothesis-generation rather
than evidence. The archaeology of Latium is the more reliable guide to the
structural conditions; the literary tradition preserves some genuine institutional
memory (the senate, the *comitia curiata*, the kingship ritual) and some genuine
family claims (the Claudian migration, the Fabian band at the Cremera) within a
framework of systematic invention.

The "deliberately pluralist" editorial approach of CAH VII.2 — Torelli
(archaeological), Momigliano (literary/comparative), Drummond (institutional/
constitutional) — explicitly acknowledged that **no single account of the regal
period can be definitive**.
