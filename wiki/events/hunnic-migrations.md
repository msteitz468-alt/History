# Hunnic Migrations and the "Great Wandering of Peoples"

The arrival of the Huns in eastern Europe c. 370 CE triggered a chain reaction of migrations and invasions that profoundly reshaped the Roman world and created the political map of early medieval Europe. Under leaders like Attila, the Huns dominated much of central Europe for a generation before their power collapsed after Attila's death (453).

## Causal and Consequence Analysis
Not a single "invasion" but a cascade: Huns displace Goths and Alans → Goths enter Roman territory (376) → Visigothic revolt and sack of Rome (410) → Vandals cross to Africa (429) → Attila's campaigns (447–452) → final deposition of Romulus Augustulus (476).

## Historiography

### From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

Mitchell treats Ammianus’s Hun excursus as heavily classical/Herodotean stereotyping and cautions that Romans had little reliable information; Huns played only a **minor role for twenty years** and became a sustained threat only in the **second quarter of the fifth century**. In the 370s they likely presented high nuisance value rather than an existential threat; for Tervingi/Greuthungi crossings of **376** one should stress **attractions of life in the Roman provinces**, especially as economic conditions north of the Danube had worsened since the **369** frontier agreement — more than pure Hunnic “push.”

Mid-fifth-century Mediterranean politics (440–480) as an elaborate **balance of power** among the western court at Rome, Huns in Illyricum, the eastern emperor at Constantinople, and Vandals at Carthage — both Gaiseric and Attila near marriage alliances with Valentinian’s house and both extracting supra-regional diplomacy from military supremacy.
