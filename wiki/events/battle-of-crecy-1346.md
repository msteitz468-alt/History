# Battle of Crécy (26 August 1346)

## Narrative

The first great land battle of the [[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years' War]] and the European
debut of the English **longbow-and-dismounted** system. At the climax of Edward III's 1346
*chevauchée* through Normandy, the English king turned at bay on a low ridge between Crécy and
Wadicourt and stood against the pursuing army of Philip VI. Edward fielded, per the
[[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]], "an army of 14,000–15,000,
including 8,000 foot, of whom the majority were longbowmen." The French were substantially more
numerous but, in Anne Curry's words, "amorphous and poorly coordinated between the different types
of troops." Hired Genoese crossbowmen were outshot and broke; successive uphill charges of the
French chivalry were shattered by flanking longbow fire and the dismounted English men-at-arms.
Among the French dead was the **blind King John of Bohemia**. The victory let Edward march on and
take **Calais** after a year-long siege.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[hundred-years-war-1337]]` — Crécy was an episode of the wider Anglo-French war and
Edward III's claim to the French crown. It was produced more proximately by Edward's decision to
**stand on prepared ground** rather than be run down on the march, and by the English military
system — a paid, contract-raised army with income-graded recruitment that put massed longbowmen in
the field. France's defeat is attributed by the volume to its **coordination failure**, not its
numbers.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced: capture of Calais (1347)` — a permanent English bridgehead in France for two centuries.
`enabled:` the repetition of the same tactical system at Poitiers (1356) and
[[battle-of-agincourt-1415|Agincourt]] (1415). Crécy is a marker event of the **infantry/missile
revolution** that historians (Clifford Rogers) place at the heart of late-medieval military change.
