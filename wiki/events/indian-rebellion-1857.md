# The Indian Rebellion of 1857 (the Mutiny)

The **Rebellion of 1857–58** — the "Mutiny," the "Great Rebellion," India's "First War of
Independence" in nationalist memory — was the convulsion that ended **[[english-east-india-company|Company]]
rule** and brought India under the British **Crown**. Documented for the wiki through Percival Spear's
[[spear-history-of-india-v2-1965|*A History of India*, Vol. II]] (1965), whose interpretation is
itself a notable historiographical position.

## Narrative

A century of expansion was "properly rounded off" by the revolt. Beneath an apparent tranquillity,
**Dalhousie's** aggressive [[british-raj|westernizing]] and his **annexations** (the **doctrine of
lapse**; the annexation of **Oudh** for misgovernment, 1856, widely felt as a breach of faith) had
alarmed the whole upper class. The flashpoint came through the **army**: the Brahmin-heavy Bengal
sepoys, resentful of overseas service, were issued the new **Enfield cartridges greased with cow and
pig fat** — unclean to Hindu and Muslim alike. The conviction "ran like wildfire that there was a plot
against the old cultures." The Mutiny began at **Meerut (10 May 1857)** and the seizure of **Delhi**
the next day (the aged Mughal **Bahadur Shah** proclaimed figurehead); it raged through the sieges of
**Kanpur and Lucknow** and the central-Indian campaign of **Tantia Topi and the Rani of Jhansi**,
ending with the fall of Gwalior (20 June 1858). British reinforcements (freed by the end of the
Crimean War) settled an outcome "never, in the long term, in doubt." It was fought with **great
ferocity and savage reprisals on both sides**.

## Spear's interpretation

Spear argues it was **neither a purely military mutiny nor a clever conspiracy nor a nationalist war**.
The military rising had genuine religious motives; the wider revolts were **backward-looking** —
Delhi a rallying point for **Muslim revivalism**, Nana Sahib for **Maratha revivalism**, agrarian
grievances bringing out the Bihar and Oudh landlords — while "the peasants were notably passive" and
**"all the new westernized class were on the British side."** "Nowhere can be found any sign of
forward-looking action towards a united India." His verdict: a **"last convulsive movement of protest
against the coming of the west on the part of traditional India"** — fragmentary in form, nostalgic in
climate. (This benign, anti-nationalist reading is exactly what later nationalist and subaltern
historiography contests, reclaiming 1857 as a proto-national uprising.)

## Consequences

- **The end of the Company:** in **1858** the East India Company was abolished and India placed under
  the **British Crown** (later the Raj proper; Victoria Empress of India, 1877).
- **Post-Mutiny caution:** government grew "more sensitive to Indian opinion, particularly the
  established classes who could control the people," and warier of headlong westernizing — "public
  works rather than public morals" became the reformer's guiding star.
- **The army was recast** (the three presidency armies kept separate; the Bengal army rebuilt) into
  the form that survived to independence.
- **The deepest result was a silent shift in Indian opinion:** the country "turned away from the
  guardian prophets of past glories," lost confidence in the traditional rajas and zamindars, and
  began to fasten its hopes on the **new English-educated class** — evidenced by the surge of English
  education in the next generation, now driven from the Indian side. The road to the
  [[indian-national-congress|national movement]] runs from here.

## Delhi 1857 and the last Mughal (Dalrymple)

[[dalrymple-the-last-mughal|William Dalrymple's *The Last Mughal*]] (2006) retells the revolt from
**Delhi** and the court of the last emperor **[[bahadur-shah-zafar|Bahadur Shah Zafar]]**, using
the Persian/Urdu **"Mutiny Papers"** — the rebel city's own records — to recover the uprising from
the Indian side. The mutinous Meerut sepoys made the aged, powerless poet-emperor their figurehead
in May 1857; the **legitimacy of the Mughal crown** was what briefly bound Hindu and Muslim rebels,
sepoys and *jihadi* preachers, together. Dalrymple's interpretive theses (recorded here as
**positions**, not settled fact): the revolt had a serious **religious dimension** (the *ghazis*
and "Wahhabi"-influenced clerics preaching jihad alongside material grievances), and it was a
reaction to the increasingly **aggressive, evangelising, racially exclusive** late Company. The
British **recapture of Delhi (September 1857)** brought a savage sack, mass executions, the shooting
of the Mughal princes, and Zafar's trial and **exile to Rangoon** — the deliberate extinction of
the dynasty of [[babur|Babur]]. (Set beside Spear's interpretation above.)
