# Saar Plebiscite (1935)

## Narrative

On 13 January 1935, the population of the Saar territory (roughly half a million voters) voted in the plebiscite mandated by the Versailles Treaty. Just under 91 per cent chose return to Germany. The result was a major propaganda triumph for Hitler that dispelled lingering doubts about the genuineness of his popular backing and provided momentum for the next revisionist steps.

The Versailles Treaty had removed the Saarland from Germany, placed it under League of Nations control for fifteen years, and granted France rights to its resources. After fifteen years the inhabitants were to choose return to Germany, union with France, or the status quo. Resentment at the 1919 treatment ran high among the largely German-speaking population.

The German government prepared the ground carefully. As the date approached, Goebbels unleashed a massive propaganda barrage directed at the Saar and raising consciousness at home. Berlin was confident of victory. The French ambassador noted Hitler would not have been surprised by French attempts to forestall by seizure or adjournment.

The Saar was overwhelmingly Catholic with a large industrial working-class population — groups that had shown least enthusiasm for Nazism inside Germany. Opponents hoped for a substantial anti-Nazi vote given repression of the Left and Church tensions in the Reich. However, Catholic authorities in the Saar put their weight behind return to Germany. Many Saar Catholics looked to Hitler as the bulwark against Bolshevism. On the Left, party loyalties had already eroded massively. Nazi propaganda contrasted return to Germany with continued unemployment, French economic exploitation, and lack of political voice. Intimidation (as in the 'time of struggle' in the Reich) played a role. Nationalist emotion and material self-interest aligned for the vast majority.

When votes were counted, just under 91 per cent had chosen dictatorship. At least two-thirds of former supporters of both left-wing parties had backed return. Any doubts about Hitler's genuine backing were dispelled.

Hitler exploited the triumph while making conciliatory noises abroad: 'Germany will of its own accord never break the peace'; in Saarbrücken (1 March, day of formal incorporation) he expressed hope that 'relations between Germany and France had improved once and for all'. His private view was that the advantage must be exploited immediately.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` Versailles plebiscite mechanism; overwhelming German-speaking population and smouldering resentment; effective German propaganda and preparatory work.

`contributed_to:` Catholic Church positioning against Bolshevism; erosion of left-wing loyalties; economic grievances under French-linked status quo.

`preceded_by:` Nazi consolidation inside Germany; ongoing rearmament in secret.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` Massive boost to Hitler's prestige and demonstrated popular support; propaganda windfall used to maximum effect.

`enabled:` Strengthened position for conscription announcement (16 March 1935) and subsequent moves; indirect impetus to rearmament demands.

`concurrent_with:` Early foreign-policy caution and rearmament buildup.

The result was treated as a genuine expression of will (despite pressures) and removed a major point of friction with France on paper, while freeing Hitler to pursue the next revision.

## Actors

- Primary: Hitler (exploitation and framing); Goebbels (propaganda); Saar Catholic authorities and voters.
- Secondary: French government; League administration; former left parties in Saar.

## Historiography

Kershaw presents the plebiscite as a 'rich propaganda gift' that fell into Hitler's lap and was milked ruthlessly. Notes the irony that a population that might have been expected to resist Nazism delivered an overwhelming vote for return. Emphasizes the combination of nationalist emotion, material calculation, Church influence, and intimidation. The episode is used to illustrate how foreign-policy 'triumphs' (even low-risk ones) accrued directly to Hitler's personal myth and prepared the ground for bolder steps. Detailed on preparation, social composition of the Saar, and Hitler's public vs. private reactions.

**Sources from range:** Contemporary diplomatic reports (François-Poncet), Goebbels propaganda context, voting analysis, Hitler's Saarbrücken and interview statements.

## From Evans, *The Third Reich in Power* (2005)

Additions (ingested 2026-07-02): Goebbels's apparatus blanketed the Saar
(80,000 posters, 1,500 meetings, cheap People's Receivers), with SS units
escalating pre-vote terror against opponents; ~91% of votes cast chose
Germany (13 January 1935). Aftermath: Prussian "carpet-baggers," a Gestapo
HQ in the old union building, the Nuremberg Laws applied to Saar Jews from
September 1935, and unfulfilled economic promises until the 1938 rearmament
boom. The success emboldened the 16 March 1935 conscription/Luftwaffe
announcement. Source: [[evans-third-reich-in-power-2005]]

## From Shirer, *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich* (1960)

Shirer gives the vote as 477,000 to 48,000 for return to Germany (13 January 1935); he notes Hitler used the occasion to renounce further claims on Alsace-Lorraine, a conciliatory gesture toward France consistent with the wiki's account of his "no further territorial demands" messaging (Shirer 1960).
