# The Norman Conquest of Southern Italy and Sicily (c. 1016–1091)

## Narrative

Across the eleventh century, bands of **Norman** adventurers transformed themselves
from hired mercenaries into the rulers of all southern Italy and Sicily.
[[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]] (G. A. Loud, Ch. 4b) describes the starting point: in
1000 the *Mezzogiorno* was divided into **three zones** — Byzantine **Apulia and
Calabria**, Arab **Sicily** (under the Kalbid emirs), and the **Lombard
principalities** of Capua, Salerno, and Benevento, with the small Byzantine-leaning
duchies of Naples, Gaeta, and Amalfi on the coast — a fragmentation compounded by
mixed Greek, Lombard, and Muslim populations of doubtful loyalty.

Normans first appear as mercenaries (drawn south by pilgrimage and the revolts against
Byzantium, such as Melus of Bari's risings of 1009–18). They hired themselves to every
side, won lands, and then took power for themselves. The decisive family was the
**Hautevilles**: the sons of Tancred of Hauteville carved out Apulia, and **Robert
Guiscard** ("the Cunning") made himself **Duke of Apulia, Calabria, and Sicily**. A
turning point was the **papal alliance**: at the **Synod of Melfi (1059)** Pope
Nicholas II invested Guiscard with his conquests as a **papal vassal** — the reform
papacy, having broken with the empire, embracing the Normans as protectors (the source
of the *milites sancti Petri* who would help defend Gregory VII). Guiscard expelled the
Byzantines, taking **Bari (1071)**, their last Italian stronghold, and even campaigned
against Byzantium across the Adriatic. His younger brother **Roger I** conquered
**Muslim Sicily** in a thirty-year campaign (Messina 1061 – Noto 1091).

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the political and religious **fragmentation** of southern Italy, which
left no single power able to resist determined newcomers.
`caused_by:` the **recruitment of Norman mercenaries** and the ambition of the
Hauteville family, who exploited every conflict to acquire land and lordship.
`contributed_to:` the **papal–Norman alliance** (Melfi, 1059), which legitimised the
conquests and gave the reform papacy a military protector against the empire.

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the unification of the *Mezzogiorno* under Norman rule, ending Byzantine
and Arab dominion in Italy and Sicily.
`produced:` the **[[kingdom-of-sicily|Norman kingdom of Sicily]]** (proclaimed by
Roger II in 1130) — a wealthy, centralised, multicultural Arab-Greek-Latin state (see
Cycle 8 / Loud Ch. 15b).
`enabled:` a durable papal ally and a new great power in the central Mediterranean,
reshaping the politics of Italy, Byzantium, and the [[crusades|Crusades]].

## Historiography

NCMH IV treats the conquest less as a single planned enterprise than as the cumulative
work of competing Norman war-bands gradually consolidated by the Hautevilles — a
process of opportunistic state-building comparable to, but distinct from, the
contemporary [[norman-conquest-of-england-1066|Norman Conquest of England]] by their
cousins.
