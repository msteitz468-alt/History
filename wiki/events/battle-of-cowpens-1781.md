# The Battle of Cowpens (17 January 1781)

The tactical masterpiece of the **[[american-revolution|Revolution]]** — a textbook **double
envelopment** in which **[[morgan-daniel|Daniel Morgan]]** destroyed Banastre Tarleton's corps.

> **Staff-college tactical analysis:** see **[[hubs/warfare/age-of-expansion/american-revolutionary-war/battle-of-cowpens|Cowpens — Tactical Analysis]]** for the full order of battle, the phased conduct of the action, and the principles-of-war assessment.

## Narrative

When [[greene-nathanael|Greene]] boldly divided his army, [[cornwallis-charles|Cornwallis]] sent
Tarleton with ~1,100 men to destroy Morgan's ~700. Morgan turned at Hannah's Cowpens and arrayed a
**three-line defense** — skirmishers, then militia asked only to fire two volleys and withdraw, then
Continentals on the rise behind. Tarleton's impetuous charge spent itself against the first lines;
when the Continentals appeared to pull back and then wheeled into a sudden volley and bayonet
counterattack, while American cavalry and the rallied militia struck both flanks and the rear, the
British were **routed almost entirely**. Congress voted Morgan a medal; Tarleton "was never entrusted
with an independent command again."

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` Greene's **deliberate division of forces** to draw Cornwallis into dividing his own.
- `contributed_to:` **Tarleton's impetuosity** and Morgan's masterly use of his militia's known limits.

## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` the loss of Cornwallis's light troops, which "significantly reduced the forces [he] could
  devote to field operations" and drove him to burn his baggage and chase Greene in the "Race to the
  Dan."
- `enabled:` the attritional path through **[[battle-of-guilford-courthouse-1781|Guilford Courthouse]]**
  that bled Cornwallis toward Virginia and **[[siege-of-yorktown-1781|Yorktown]]**.

## Historiography

Morgan's own after-action report and Continental Congress records document the victory closely; Tarleton's own (self-justifying) memoir gives the British side. Historians have long debated how much of the celebrated "three-line" defense was pre-planned genius versus adaptation to known militia limitations under fire, and how much credit belongs to Morgan alone versus subordinate commanders (Howard, William Washington's cavalry). The battle is a staple of American military history precisely because it functions as a clean textbook tactical case — the "double envelopment," compared here to Cannae — and some historians caution against reading too much doctrinal tidiness into what was also a product of Tarleton's impetuosity and poor scouting.
