# The First Mithridatic War (89–85 BCE)

The war in which **[[mithridates-vi|Mithridates VI]]** overran Roman Asia and Greece,
massacred the resident Italians, and was beaten back by **Sulla** — the bloodiest
collision of Rome and a Hellenistic king. Treated in [[crook-cah-v9-1992|CAH IX]]
Ch. 5 (Hind).

## Narrative

### The conquest of Asia and the Asiatic Vespers (89–88)

Exploiting Rome's absorption in the **[[social-war-91-88bce|Social War]]**, Mithridates
destroyed the armies of Nicomedes of Bithynia and the Roman commissioners (Aquillius,
Cassius, Oppius) and overran the **province of Asia** — Rome's forty-year
administration "collapsed." He posed as **liberator**, remitting taxes and cancelling
debts; the consul **Aquillius** was captured and executed by having molten gold poured
down his throat, "in mockery of the avarice that had brought on the war." Then, in
**88**, came the **"Asiatic Vespers"**: Mithridates' secret order for the simultaneous
massacre of all **Italian residents** (perhaps 80,000) across the Greek cities — many
of which obeyed with enthusiasm born of hatred for the Roman *publicani* and
money-lenders. The act ensured no complicit city could return to Roman allegiance.

### The war in Greece (88–86)

Invited by anti-Roman factions, Mithridates' general **Archelaus** took **Athens**
(under the tyrant Aristion) and **Delos**, and much of Greece went over. **Sulla**,
given the command, crossed in 87 and conducted the brutal **sieges of Athens and
Piraeus** (Athens fell 1 March 86; he plundered the temple treasures of Delphi,
Epidaurus, and Olympia). He then destroyed Mithridates' huge armies in two decisive
battles in Boeotia — **Chaeronea** and **Orchomenus** (86) — neutralising the scythed
chariots and the cavalry with discipline and entrenchment.

### Fimbria, and the Treaty of Dardanus (85)

A rival Roman army sent by Sulla's enemies at Rome (Flaccus, then the usurper
**Fimbria**) campaigned in Asia and nearly trapped Mithridates at Pitane — but
**Lucullus** refused to let his fleet hand the credit to Sulla's foes. With his fleet
and cities deserting, Mithridates accepted the **[[mithridates-vi|Treaty of Dardanus]]
(85)**: he surrendered his conquests, 70–80 ships, and a 2,000-talent indemnity, but
kept Pontus and his Black Sea empire — emerging "so little scathed."

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: Mithridates' expansionism and Roman provocation — but Hind stresses the
deeper cause was the **alienation of the Greek cities** by the rapacity of Roman
governors and *publicani*, which made the Vespers possible; and Rome's
[[social-war-91-88bce|Social-War distraction]], which gave Mithridates his opening.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: **Sulla's crushing settlement of Asia** — the cities billeted, walls pulled
down, and a **20,000-talent** burden (indemnity plus five years' back-tax) that
mortgaged them for a generation, while loyal cities (Rhodes, Ilium, the Carian and
Lycian cities) were rewarded.
enabled: a great surge of **Mediterranean piracy** (the fleets removed, the cities
ruined); the **later Mithridatic Wars** (Murena, Lucullus, Pompey); and, for Sulla, the
fleet, prestige (*Felix*), and money to march on his enemies at Rome — the next
civil war.

## Historiography

**Appian's *Mithridatica*** and **Plutarch's *Sulla*** (each concentrating on a
different siege), with Memnon; figures are inflated. The Vespers' total and date are
debated. See [[mithridates-vi]].
