# Assassination of Abraham Lincoln (14–15 April 1865)

On the evening of 14 April 1865 — Good Friday, five days after Appomattox — John Wilkes Booth shot President Abraham Lincoln at [[places/fords-theatre|Ford's Theatre]] in [[places/washington-dc|Washington, D.C.]]. Lincoln died at the Petersen House the following morning. Coordinated attacks targeted other officials the same night (Seward grievously wounded).

## Narrative

### Kidnap plot and network (Ellsworth 2025)

From mid-1864, [[actors/booth-john-wilkes|John Wilkes Booth]] recruited boyhood friends Sam Arnold and Michael O'Laughlen to seize Lincoln on the Soldiers' Home route and boat him to Richmond (Aug 1864). Separately, Confederate spy Thomas Conrad ran a Richmond-ordered kidnap scheme (Sept–mid-Nov 1864) abandoned when Lincoln took an armed escort. Booth's Montreal stay (18–27 Oct 1864) at St. Lawrence Hall put him in contact with Confederate Canada figures (George N. Sanders, Patrick C. Martin). Ellsworth interprets the evolving plot as carrying Confederate Secret Service "handprints" (Harbin, John Surratt, Powell, Atzerodt)—an interpretive thesis, not proven government direction at trial.

By early 1865 Booth moved weapons caches to Washington; a White House mining scheme appears in Atzerodt's confession; a 17 Mar Campbell Hospital theater abduction failed when Lincoln did not attend—"Nobody talked about kidnapping the President after that."

### Immediate run-up

At the [[events/lincoln-second-inaugural-1865|Second Inaugural]] (4 Mar 1865) Booth stood less than twenty-five feet from Lincoln and later boasted of the chance to kill him. On 11 Apr, after Lincoln's White House speech favoring limited Black suffrage for the "very intelligent" and Black soldiers, Booth urged Lewis Powell to shoot; Powell refused; Booth said it was "the last speech he'll ever give." On 13 Apr (Grand Illumination) Booth, Herold, Atzerodt, and Powell met in Powell's Ninth Street room.

### 14 April 1865 — Ford's Theatre

Good Friday. Lincoln held a long cabinet (Seward absent recovering from a carriage accident; son Frederick sat in). Grant briefed Appomattox; the Grants declined a Ford's invitation; Lincoln invited Maj. Henry Rathbone and Clara Harris. *Our American Cousin* (Laura Keene benefit) began ~8:15; the presidential party arrived ~8:30 to cheers.

Mid–third act, during Harry Hawk's laugh line, a gunshot. A man in black climbed the Presidential Box railing, spur catching bunting, fell to the stage, rose with a dagger, shouted **"Sic semper tyrannis!"** (Virginia's motto), and fled to a waiting horse. Laura Keene and stagehands identified [[actors/booth-john-wilkes|Booth]]. Lincoln was carried to the Petersen House opposite [[places/fords-theatre|Ford's Theatre]] and died the morning of 15 April.

### Same-night multi-target attacks

- **Lewis Powell** assaulted [[actors/seward-william-h|William H. Seward]] at Lafayette Square—attacking Frederick Seward and a nurse, then slashing the bedridden secretary's face and throat; fled thinking Seward dead; arrested three days later.
- **George Atzerodt**, assigned to kill Vice President Andrew Johnson, drank and backed out.
- Herold and Mary Surratt were also arrested in the wider sweep.

### Manhunt and punishment

Booth was hunted twelve days and fatally shot in the neck in a burning Virginia barn (~26 Apr); died aged 26. Military-commission trial sentenced four to death; three to life; Ned Spangler to six years. On **7 July 1865** Powell, Herold, Atzerodt, and Mary Surratt were hanged at the Old Arsenal Prison. Prosecutors, Ellsworth notes, **could not prove** direct Confederate-government behest though they believed it. John Surratt escaped abroad, was later tried in the U.S., hung jury, freed.

### Aftermath

National mourning; Stanton oversaw a 1,600-mile funeral train Washington–Springfield via ten cities. Some Southern voices celebrated; the loyal North draped cities in crepe. Ellsworth's Afterword argues Lost Cause memory later rewrote Booth as a lone madman to scrub Confederate network ties—treat as **historiographical position**, not wiki verdict.

## Causal Analysis

- **caused_by:** [[actors/booth-john-wilkes|Booth]]'s decision and shot — direct agency.
- **contributed_to:** Confederate defeat and fears of Black citizenship; prior kidnap conspiracy infrastructure; weak theater security; political polarization after [[events/us-presidential-election-1864]] and [[events/lincoln-second-inaugural-1865]].
- **preceded_by:** Appomattox (9 April); Lincoln's 11 April speech on limited Black suffrage (reported to have hardened Booth).
- Distinguish sequence from causation: Union victory did not *necessitate* assassination; it shaped Booth's motives and timing.

## Consequence Analysis

- **produced:** Immediate presidential succession of Andrew Johnson; national mourning; trials.
- **enabled:** A Reconstruction path without Lincoln's political skill and "malice toward none" framing — Johnson's conflict with Radicals followed.
- Scale: local act, civilizational consequences for U.S. citizenship politics and national memory.

## Historiography

Debates continue over Confederate government complicity vs. freelancing conspiracy; over Mary Surratt's guilt; and over how much the assassination changed Reconstruction. Ellsworth offers a narrative multi-thread synthesis emphasizing network and contingency. Specialist literature (assassination monographs, secret-service studies) should adjudicate deeper claims. Donald *Lincoln* covers the death from the biographical side.
