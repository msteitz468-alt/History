# The Fourth Crusade and the Sack of Constantinople (1202–1204)

## Narrative

The **Fourth Crusade**, intended for Egypt, was diverted to **Constantinople**, which a
Latin army **sacked in April 1204** — the most notorious episode in the history of
Latin–Byzantine relations and the terminal event of [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(whose Byzantine narrative, Paul Magdalino, Ch. 20, "The Byzantine empire, 1118–1204,"
ends here; the aftermath belongs to [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]).

Unable to pay the **Venetians** for the fleet they had contracted, the crusaders fell
under the influence of Doge **Enrico Dandolo** and were drawn into a **Byzantine
succession dispute**: the pretender Alexios Angelos promised money, troops, and Church
union in return for restoration. The diversion (via the sack of Christian **Zara**, which
earned the crusaders' excommunication) led to Constantinople; when the promised payments
failed and relations broke down, the crusaders stormed and **brutally sacked the city in
1204**, looting its churches and relics and shattering the [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine
empire]].

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the crusade's **crippling debt to Venice** and the leverage it gave Dandolo.
`caused_by:` the **Byzantine succession crisis** and the appeal of Alexios Angelos, which
provided the pretext and the lure.
`contributed_to:` the long **Latin–Byzantine estrangement** built up since the
[[komnenian-restoration|Komnenian era]] (the trade rivalries with the Italians, the
massacre of the Latins in 1182, the failure of Church union) and the **collapse of
Byzantine power** after Manuel I's death (1180).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **Latin Empire of Constantinople** and the partition of Byzantine
territory among the crusaders and Venice.
`produced:` the Byzantine **successor states** — the Empire of **Nicaea** (which would
recover Constantinople in 1261), the Despotate of **Epiros**, and the Empire of
**Trebizond**.
`produced:` a **permanent deepening of the East–West schism**, the bitterness of 1204
poisoning Greek–Latin relations for centuries (see [[eastern-churches]]).

## Significance

The events of 1204 — described by contemporaries as the climax of the Latin assault on
the Greek world — fatally weakened Byzantium, which never fully recovered even after
1261, and stand as the symbolic end of the high-medieval Byzantine empire. The editors
of NCMH IV note the long preparation of "both events" (Manzikert and 1204) and quote the
description of 1204 as "the last of the barbarian invasions of the Roman empire." The
restored but diminished empire belongs to the thirteenth-century world of NCMH V.

## Wickham: "the failure of alternatives"

[[wickham-medieval-europe-2016|Chris Wickham]] makes 1204 the hinge of an explicitly **comparative** argument, naming a whole chapter of *Medieval Europe* "1204: the failure of alternatives." His point is not about the crusade's contingent mechanics (he grants the familiar "crusader greed, Venetian cynicism, imperial ineptness") but about what the destruction of Byzantium *cost the European political repertoire*.

Byzantium, he argues, was the surviving **alternative model** of how a medieval polity could work: a **tax-based state** (it "never abandoned, and never would abandon, a land tax") that funded a **paid, professional army**, a large literate **bureaucracy** running the capital and the fisc, and a **fully literate elite** "in a way that the post-Carolingian west would not match for centuries." Because the state taxed and the aristocracy did not locally dominate the independent tax-paying peasantry, Byzantine elites "could not easily go it alone": opposition took the form of *usurpation*, not the *provincial separatism* and cellular fragmentation that defined the Latin west after the [[feudal-revolution-debate|eleventh-century break]]. Byzantium had been a model for governance from Rus' to Sicily to Hungary.

When Byzantium broke up — Wickham pairs the **late-twelfth-century separatist collapse** with the sack of 1204, treating it as the eastern equivalent of the western eleventh-century break, "even sharper" — "its techniques of government were no longer available to Latin Europe, and had to be reinvented, which took a long time." Its heirs at the old imperial scale were the **Ottomans**, whose Muslim religion made them an unlikely model for Christian Europe. Thus 1204 is, in Wickham's reading, a forfeited *opportunity*: the loss of a working tax-state developmental path, which the west would only slowly rebuild from the thirteenth century onward (see [[politics-of-land]], [[late-middle-ages]]). It is "as large a political event as the similar break-up of the western Roman empire, and is as complex to explain."

## Historiography

The historiography divides between **contingent** and **structural** explanations.
The dominant tradition explains the diversion to Constantinople through the
*accidents* of the expedition — the crusade's crippling debt to **Venice**, the
leverage this gave Doge **Dandolo**, and the opportunistic appeal of the pretender
**Alexios Angelos** — a chain Wickham summarises as "crusader greed, Venetian
cynicism, imperial ineptness." A long-running sub-debate asks whether the diversion
was **premeditated** (a Venetian or Montferrat design) or a cascade of improvised
responses to insolvency; most recent scholarship favours contingency over conspiracy.

[[wickham-medieval-europe-2016|Chris Wickham]] shifts the question from *how* the
crusade reached Constantinople to *what its success destroyed*, treating 1204 as the
terminal point of a **structural** Byzantine breakdown that began with the
late-twelfth-century separatist revolts — "the failure of alternatives" (see above).
He notes that, relative to the comparable break-up of the western Roman empire, the
fall of Byzantium has received *less* analytic attention precisely because the Fourth
Crusade "has seemed fairly straightforward to explain." The NCMH IV editors
([[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004]]) place 1204 in a long preparation reaching back to
Manzikert (1071) and quote the description of the sack as "the last of the barbarian
invasions of the Roman empire." The bitterness of 1204 in the Orthodox tradition —
and the question of how far it permanently poisoned Greek–Latin relations — remains a
live interpretive thread (see [[eastern-churches]]).
