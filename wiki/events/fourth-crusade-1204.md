# The Fourth Crusade and the Sack of Constantinople (1202–1204)

## Narrative

The **Fourth Crusade**, intended for Egypt, was diverted to **Constantinople**, which a
Latin army **sacked in April 1204** — the most notorious episode in the history of
Latin–Byzantine relations and the terminal event of [[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004|NCMH IV]]
(whose Byzantine narrative, Paul Magdalino, Ch. 20, "The Byzantine empire, 1118–1204,"
ends here; the aftermath belongs to [[abulafia-ncmh-v5-2000|NCMH V]]).

Unable to pay the **Venetians** for the fleet they had contracted, the crusaders fell
under the influence of Doge **Enrico Dandolo** and were drawn into a **Byzantine
succession dispute**: the pretender Alexios Angelos promised money, troops, and Church
union in return for restoration. The diversion (via the sack of Christian **Zara**, which
earned the crusaders' excommunication) led to Constantinople; when the promised payments
failed and relations broke down, the crusaders stormed and **brutally sacked the city in
1204**, looting its churches and relics and shattering the [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine
empire]].

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by:` the crusade's **crippling debt to Venice** and the leverage it gave Dandolo.
`caused_by:` the **Byzantine succession crisis** and the appeal of Alexios Angelos, which
provided the pretext and the lure.
`contributed_to:` the long **Latin–Byzantine estrangement** built up since the
[[komnenian-restoration|Komnenian era]] (the trade rivalries with the Italians, the
massacre of the Latins in 1182, the failure of Church union) and the **collapse of
Byzantine power** after Manuel I's death (1180).

## Consequence Analysis

`produced:` the **Latin Empire of Constantinople** and the partition of Byzantine
territory among the crusaders and Venice.
`produced:` the Byzantine **successor states** — the Empire of **Nicaea** (which would
recover Constantinople in 1261), the Despotate of **Epiros**, and the Empire of
**Trebizond**.
`produced:` a **permanent deepening of the East–West schism**, the bitterness of 1204
poisoning Greek–Latin relations for centuries (see [[eastern-churches]]).

## Significance

The events of 1204 — described by contemporaries as the climax of the Latin assault on
the Greek world — fatally weakened Byzantium, which never fully recovered even after
1261, and stand as the symbolic end of the high-medieval Byzantine empire. The editors
of NCMH IV note the long preparation of "both events" (Manzikert and 1204) and quote the
description of 1204 as "the last of the barbarian invasions of the Roman empire." The
restored but diminished empire belongs to the thirteenth-century world of NCMH V.

## Wickham: "the failure of alternatives"

[[wickham-medieval-europe-2016|Chris Wickham]] makes 1204 the hinge of an explicitly **comparative** argument, naming a whole chapter of *Medieval Europe* "1204: the failure of alternatives." His point is not about the crusade's contingent mechanics (he grants the familiar "crusader greed, Venetian cynicism, imperial ineptness") but about what the destruction of Byzantium *cost the European political repertoire*.

Byzantium, he argues, was the surviving **alternative model** of how a medieval polity could work: a **tax-based state** (it "never abandoned, and never would abandon, a land tax") that funded a **paid, professional army**, a large literate **bureaucracy** running the capital and the fisc, and a **fully literate elite** "in a way that the post-Carolingian west would not match for centuries." Because the state taxed and the aristocracy did not locally dominate the independent tax-paying peasantry, Byzantine elites "could not easily go it alone": opposition took the form of *usurpation*, not the *provincial separatism* and cellular fragmentation that defined the Latin west after the [[feudal-revolution-debate|eleventh-century break]]. Byzantium had been a model for governance from Rus' to Sicily to Hungary.

When Byzantium broke up — Wickham pairs the **late-twelfth-century separatist collapse** with the sack of 1204, treating it as the eastern equivalent of the western eleventh-century break, "even sharper" — "its techniques of government were no longer available to Latin Europe, and had to be reinvented, which took a long time." Its heirs at the old imperial scale were the **Ottomans**, whose Muslim religion made them an unlikely model for Christian Europe. Thus 1204 is, in Wickham's reading, a forfeited *opportunity*: the loss of a working tax-state developmental path, which the west would only slowly rebuild from the thirteenth century onward (see [[politics-of-land]], [[late-middle-ages]]). It is "as large a political event as the similar break-up of the western Roman empire, and is as complex to explain."

## From Logan, History of the Church in the Middle Ages (2013)

Logan narrates the crusade as “the tragedy of the crusade called by Pope [[innocent-iii|Innocent III]] in August 1198.” Innocent intended papal control and the recovery of Jerusalem; secret strategy was to seize **Egypt/Alexandria** as hostage for Jerusalem (Richard I among earlier supporters of that approach). Response was slow; Fulk of Neuilly preached; recruitment from the Nov. 1199 tournament of Thibault of Champagne; leaders included Baldwin of Flanders, Louis of Blois, Simon de Montfort et al.

**Treaty of Venice (1201):** transport 4,500 knights + horses, 9,000 squires, 20,000 foot (33,500) for 85,000 marks in four instalments by April 1202; Venice 50 war galleys for half spoils; ~450 vessels, ~14,000 crew. Innocent confirmed May 1201 with apparent condition not to attack Christians. Thibault died young; **Boniface of Montferrat** chosen leader without consulting the pope. Only ~**12,000** crusaders arrived and only **50,000** marks paid — collapse imminent.

**Zara (1202):** deferred payment deal if crusaders took Christian Zara (Hungarian hands; Venetian interest); barons agreed, rank and file ignorant (Robert of Clari). Sailed 8 Nov. 1202; took and pillaged Zara after 16 days. Innocent appalled; excommunication incurred, later lifted **except for Venetians**.

**Constantinople bargain:** Philip of Swabia (married to Irene, daughter of deposed Isaac Angelus) and Boniface proposed restoring young **Alexius**; Alexius would pay the Venetian debt. Innocent forbade the sword against Christians — leaders hid the prohibition; legate excommunicated Venetians; despite Simon de Montfort’s defection, Corfu decision to sail for Constantinople; crusade out of control.

**Capture and sack:** Fleet left Corfu 25 May 1203; sighted Constantinople 23 June; Galata taken, harbour chain broken; walls breached, Alexius III fled; Isaac and Alexius IV co-emperors (1 Aug. 1203 coronation; promise of church union). Unpaid, a coup strangled Alexius IV; crusaders planned a Latin empire (emperor 1/4; rest split Venice/knights); Villehardouin treated the crusade as over. **12 April 1204** walls breached; fire; three-day sack allowed. Logan quotes Runciman: “The sack of Constantinople is unparalleled in history” — rape of nuns, random slaughter, theft of jewels/relics, church violation, art destruction; Santa Sophia stripped, altar smashed, prostitute on the patriarch’s throne; a Cistercian abbot specialized in relic theft for Alsace; Dandolo took the four bronze Hippodrome horses (now St Mark’s) — hubris/greed symbol; central booty pool finally paid Venice.

**Innocent’s response:** To the new Latin emperor he wrote without referencing the attack; to **Boniface** he condemned forsaking the crusader vow, fighting Christians for Constantinople’s riches, sparing neither religion, age, nor sex, fornication/adultery/incest including with consecrated women; blamed Venetians. **Baldwin of Flanders** crowned 16 May 1204; Latin empire lasted to **1261**. Logan: the episode twisted Urban II’s ideal and made the East–West schism permanent; John Paul II apologized in 2001.

## Historiography

The historiography divides between **contingent** and **structural** explanations.
The dominant tradition explains the diversion to Constantinople through the
*accidents* of the expedition — the crusade's crippling debt to **Venice**, the
leverage this gave Doge **Dandolo**, and the opportunistic appeal of the pretender
**Alexios Angelos** — a chain Wickham summarises as "crusader greed, Venetian
cynicism, imperial ineptness." A long-running sub-debate asks whether the diversion
was **premeditated** (a Venetian or Montferrat design) or a cascade of improvised
responses to insolvency; most recent scholarship favours contingency over conspiracy.

[[wickham-medieval-europe-2016|Chris Wickham]] shifts the question from *how* the
crusade reached Constantinople to *what its success destroyed*, treating 1204 as the
terminal point of a **structural** Byzantine breakdown that began with the
late-twelfth-century separatist revolts — "the failure of alternatives" (see above).
He notes that, relative to the comparable break-up of the western Roman empire, the
fall of Byzantium has received *less* analytic attention precisely because the Fourth
Crusade "has seemed fairly straightforward to explain." The NCMH IV editors
([[luscombe-ncmh-v4-2004]]) place 1204 in a long preparation reaching back to
Manzikert (1071) and quote the description of the sack as "the last of the barbarian
invasions of the Roman empire." The bitterness of 1204 in the Orthodox tradition —
and the question of how far it permanently poisoned Greek–Latin relations — remains a
live interpretive thread (see [[eastern-churches]]).

## Islamic perspective (Cobb 2014)

[[cobb-race-for-paradise-2014|Cobb]] notes that the Fourth Crusade was preached (1198) with
**Ayyubid Egypt** as principal target, but “famously never made it to the Islamic Near East”:
diverted to Constantinople in 1204, it toppled the Byzantine emperor and planted a Latin kingdom
on Byzantine soil. **Arabic sources are sparse but informed** — sack, burning, relics and objets
sold including into the Near East. **Abu Shama** dryly remarked that Franks looted treasures and
marbles: “Damascus has seen plenty of that marble.” Sultan **al-ʿAdil** had clear advance warning
of Frankish movements; whether the crusade would have succeeded had it reached Egypt/Syria remains
moot. A **1204 naval raid on Rosetta**, probably connected, met an impotent Ayyubid response —
the Ayyubids had no fleet. For Muslim observers the episode confirmed both Frankish menace and
the unpredictability of where that menace would strike.

## Economic Consequences

David Jacoby's analysis (in [[laiou-mottahedeh-crusades-byzantium-muslim-2001]]) details the economic transformation that followed the Latin conquest — see [[latin-romania-economy]] for the full process page. Key findings:

- The Latin conquest dissolved **Byzantine centralized control over manufacture**, above all silk — the empire's most important industrial sector. No Latin successor authority replicated Byzantine state direction of the silk industry.
- **Confiscation and redistribution** of Byzantine crown land, magnate estates, and Constantinopolitan monastic property was extensive, with Crete triggering a Greek rebellion in 1212 over property seizures.
- The conquered territories were rapidly reintegrated into **Western trade networks**, with Venice structurally dominant: Greek merchants were entirely excluded from Venetian state-galley commerce; the leasing of these ships and transport of precious goods were the exclusive preserve of Venetian citizens.
- Over two centuries, Latin Romania underwent a long-term **deindustrialization**: from a supplier of finished silk textiles to the West, it became primarily a supplier of raw silk, cocoons, and dyestuffs (*kermes*) to expanding Luccan and Venetian industries. Italian silks were imported back in growing volume — reversing an age-old trade flow.
- Jacoby stresses **internal differentiation**: Venetian territories (especially Crete) were more commercially dynamic and credit-driven than feudalized areas (Frankish Morea), where Western feudal arrangements were less suited to commercial exploitation.
- Despite these disruptions, **agrarian continuity** prevailed: the same crops, labor forms, and rural infrastructure persisted under Latin lords who shared Greek lords' interest in preserving the Byzantine countryside.

## From Gibbon, *Decline and Fall* (Chs. LX–LXI; LXVIII coda)

Attributed claims from [[sources/gibbon-decline-and-fall-1776]]; not asserted as modern fact.

- **Schism as precipitant.** Greek–Latin separation after Charlemagne’s Western restoration; “the schism of Constantinople, by alienating her most useful allies, and provoking her most dangerous enemies, has precipitated the decline and fall of the Roman empire in the East.” Immediate cause traced to rivalry of leading prelates (*filioque* and discipline points magnified by bigotry).
- **Diversion of the crusade.** Young Alexius’s offers (end of schism, 200,000 silver marks, Egyptian service or knight-maintenance) divert the host; Dandolo’s “more generous ambition” seeks trade and dominion; mixed baronial motives — public advantage, honor of restoring a monarch, belief that Constantinople must precede Jerusalem — while pious dissenters withdraw after Zara’s stain.
- **Venetian armament.** Fleet scale (120 horse transports, 240 troop transports, 70 storeships, 50 galleys) underwrites the boast that “forty thousand Christian heroes were equal to the conquest of the world.”
- **Nine-century dream broken.** Latins awaken Greeks “from a dream of nine centuries” — the presumption that the capital was impregnable to foreign arms.
- **Sack balance-sheet.** Ordered plunder under common-stock distribution; French residue ~400,000 silver marks ≈ seven times England’s annual revenue; “the gain is never equivalent to the loss”; three fires, melted statues, relics flooding Europe, libraries perishing; Greeks forever weep the ruins.
- **Dual witnesses.** Innocent III accuses pilgrims of open-day lust without regard to age, sex, or profession; Gibbon reduces Nicetas’s blood streams to ~2,000 slain (mostly by returning Latin exiles) and savors comparing Villehardouin and Nicetas as marshal and senator.
- **1204 vs 1453.** Empire only subverted by Latins; religion trampled by Muslim conquerors (cross-link [[fall-of-constantinople-1453]]).
