# Japan-Russia Negotiations over Korea and Manchuria (1903)

The eight months of failed diplomacy between Japan and Russia (August 1903 – February 1904)
that immediately preceded the [[russo-japanese-war-1904|Russo-Japanese War]]. The negotiations
are documented in Donald Keene's [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002|*Emperor of Japan: Meiji
and His World*]] (chapter 53) through the deliberations at the Japanese court, the emperor's
intervention to keep Katsura in office, and the Japanese proposals submitted to St. Petersburg.

## Background

The immediate precipitant was Russia's **failure to complete the withdrawal of its troops from
Manchuria** as required by the 1902 Russo-Chinese agreement. The first phase of evacuation had
occurred; the second phase (due April 1903) did not. Japan interpreted this as a permanent
occupation — the consolidation of Russian hegemony over Manchuria as a stepping-stone to Korea.

On **1 June 1903**, seven Japanese law professors submitted a memorial to Prime Minister Katsura
calling for urgent action. They argued that Japan had already missed three strategic opportunities
(the Triple Intervention in 1895, the German seizure of Kiaochow in 1897, and Russia's post-Boxer
failure to withdraw) and could not afford a fourth. The memorial stated Japan's military
superiority over Russia would last "less than a year at the longest" — once the Trans-Siberian
Railway was fully operational and Russia's Pacific fleet reinforced, the balance would tip. The
seven doctors stopped short of explicitly demanding war but clearly envisioned an ultimatum.

## The Emperor's June 23 Council

On **23 June 1903**, at the request of Prime Minister Katsura and Foreign Minister Komura,
[[emperor-meiji|Emperor Meiji]] summoned nine of Japan's senior statesmen to discuss policy
toward Russia. This council revealed the Japanese government's settled view: Russia must withdraw
from Manchuria; Korea must not fall under Russian influence; the two questions were inseparable
(Japan resisted any diplomatic formula that treated Manchuria as already Russian in exchange for
Korean recognition).

The same week, **General Kuropatkin**, the Russian minister of war, visited Japan as a state
guest and was received by the emperor — decorated with the Order of the Rising Sun, First Class.
The Japanese avoided military discussions but noted that the Russian war minister's private
assessment was that war could be avoided. Kuropatkin conveyed a personal message from the tsar
expressing hope for "increasingly intimate" relations now that the Trans-Siberian Railway was
complete — a message that the Japanese read as threatening rather than reassuring.

## Diplomatic Breakdown

On **28 July 1903** Japan formally requested Russia's willingness to negotiate on Korea and
Manchuria. On **12 August** Foreign Minister Komura's six-point proposal was delivered to
Count Lamsdorf in St. Petersburg:

1. Mutual recognition of Chinese and Korean independence and territorial integrity; Open Door.
2. Recognition of Japan's special interests in Korea and Russia's in Manchurian railway administration.
3. Non-interference in each other's commercial and industrial enterprises (Japan in Korea, Russia in Manchuria).
4. Troops sent to either territory to restore order must be limited to need and promptly withdrawn.
5. Japan's exclusive right to advise and assist Korea (including militarily) recognised.
6. This treaty to supersede all previous Russo-Japanese agreements on Korea.

The Russian response was slow (Lamsdorf claimed to be "busy"), and its content was shaped by
a simultaneous power shift in Russian Far Eastern policy: on **12 August** the tsar established
the Viceroyalty of the Far East under **Alekseev**, concentrating all authority in a hard-liner
who despised negotiated settlements. The moderate Finance Minister Witte was simultaneously
removed. These changes meant that the Russian counterproposals, when they arrived, were far
more restrictive than Komura's draft.

Over the following months, Japan and Russia exchanged four rounds of proposals. The Russians
insisted on keeping Manchuria as a purely Chinese matter (outside the negotiating framework)
while limiting Japan in Korea more narrowly than Komura's draft allowed. Japan insisted that
the Manchurian and Korean questions were inseparable.

## The Emperor's Role

The diplomatic crisis coincided with a domestic political crisis: Katsura, despairing of
managing both the Seiyūkai-dominated Diet and the Russian crisis, submitted his resignation in
June 1903. The emperor refused to accept it, telling Katsura that "at a critical time, when
negotiations were about to begin with the Russians on Korea and Manchuria, he could not permit
the resignation." He then appointed Itō Hirobumi as President of the Privy Council (13 July),
writing personally to Itō: "I have depended on and trusted your service over the years, and I
hope that with your sound advice and faithful assistance we shall successfully accomplish the
task ahead" — described by Keene as "probably the most unambiguous statement of esteem that
the emperor ever gave to any of his subjects." The manoeuvre unified the Japanese government
behind Katsura while drawing Itō's pro-Russian faction into a responsible consultative role.

## Outcome

By February 1904 negotiations had collapsed, with neither side willing to yield on the
Manchuria-Korea linkage. Japan launched its **surprise naval attack on Port Arthur on 8 February
1904**, opening the Russo-Japanese War without a formal declaration of war.
