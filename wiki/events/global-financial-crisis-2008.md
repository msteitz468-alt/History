# Global Financial Crisis (2008)

The **Global Financial Crisis** was the most severe worldwide economic downturn since the
Great Depression, triggered by the collapse of the US subprime mortgage market and the
unwinding of the vast, poorly understood web of mortgage-backed securities and derivatives
built on it. Years of aggressive lending, securitization, and leverage across the US and
global financial system left major institutions dangerously exposed; the failure of the
investment bank Lehman Brothers in September 2008 triggered a full-scale panic and freeze
in global credit markets.

## US Government Response

The [[bush-george-w|Bush administration]], in its final months, pushed through the
Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) to stabilize the banking system, and extended
emergency support to the auto industry — actions in tension with the administration's
stated free-market principles but justified, in Bush's own account, as necessary given
"not ordinary circumstances." The crisis dominated the closing weeks of the 2008
presidential campaign between [[mccain-john|John McCain]] and [[obama-barack|Barack
Obama]], reinforcing a public mood favoring change and contributing to Obama's victory;
the new administration inherited and continued the crisis response into 2009.

## Historiography

Historians and economists continue to debate the relative weight of causes — deregulation
and inadequate oversight, monetary policy, global capital imbalances, and failures of risk
management and ratings agencies all feature prominently in different accounts. The crisis
prompted a substantial regulatory response (Dodd-Frank Act, Basel III internationally) and
remains a central reference point in debates over financial regulation, the moral hazard
of "too big to fail" institutions, and the political legitimacy of large-scale government
intervention in markets.
