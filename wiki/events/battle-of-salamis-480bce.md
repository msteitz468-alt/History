# The Battle of Salamis (480 BCE)

At **Salamis**, in the narrow strait between the island and the Attic coast, the Greek League
fleet destroyed the navy of [[xerxes-i|Xerxes I]] — the decisive sea-battle that **broke the
Persian invasion** and, in the judgment of [[boardman-cah-v4-1988|CAH Vol. IV]] (Ch. 10,
N. G. L. Hammond), saved the independent Greek world. It is the battle Aeschylus, a
participant, dramatized in the **Persae** (472).

## The road to battle

With Thermopylae forced and **Athens evacuated and burned**, the Greek fleet (~370 triremes)
gathered in the Salamis Channel while the Peloponnesians wanted to fall back to the Isthmus.
**[[themistocles|Themistocles]]**, commanding over half the fleet, insisted on fighting in
**narrow water** to neutralize Persian numbers and speed. To force the issue, he sent his
agent **Sicinnus** to Xerxes with a false report that the Greeks were about to flee and could
be caught divided. Xerxes — believing what he wished — sent a **squadron round the island** to
block the western exit and ordered his **main fleet into the strait at night**, while he set a
throne on **Mt Aegaleos** to watch the victory.

## The battle

At dawn the Persian fleet rowed up the narrows in close order (Phoenicians on the right near
the Attic coast, Ionians on the left), tired from a night under oar. The Greeks backed water
to draw them deeper in, then — as the morning **swell** Themistocles expected came up the
channel and rolled the high Persian ships off their bearing — charged with their low, heavy,
bronze-rammed triremes, "ripping the sides and smashing the oars." Crowded together with no
room to manoeuvre, the Persian fleet was wrecked on itself; a fleeing front line crashed into
the advancing rear. The picked Persian troops landed on the islet **Psyttalia** were
annihilated by Aristides' hoplites. The Aeginetans (awarded **first prize for valour**) and
Athenians (second) cut down the ships trying to escape to Phalerum.

## Causal Analysis

caused_by: [[xerxes-i|Xerxes']] over-confidence after the sack of Athens and his belief in
Greek disunity; Themistocles' **deception** and his insistence on the narrows.
contributed_to: the Greeks' **superior ship design and ramming tactics** (honed at Artemisium)
and Themistocles' knowledge of the **local wind and swell**.

## Consequence Analysis

produced: the loss of much of the Persian fleet (Diodorus: 200+ ships, the best crews, the
non-swimming Persian/Median/Sacan marines drowned) against ~40 Greek ships.
enabled: **Xerxes' withdrawal to Asia** (fearing for the Hellespont bridges), leaving
**Mardonius** with a land army that was destroyed at **[[battle-of-plataea-479bce|Plataea]]**
the next year; the **survival of Greek liberty**; and the conversion of Athens into the naval
power that would build the **Delian League and empire**. Hammond rates it, with Plataea, as
"among the most decisive battles in the history of Europe."

## Historiography

The two great sources are **Aeschylus' Persae** (an eyewitness's verse, giving a swift,
panoramic account) and **[[herodotus-reliability|Herodotus]]** (Book VIII, a series of vivid
individual incidents, grudging toward Themistocles). The exact numbers, the geography of
Psyttalia and Xerxes' throne, and the role of Themistocles' bribery-anecdotes are debated;
Hammond reconstructs the tactics from the wind, the ship-types, and the crescent formation.
