## Narrative

In 523 CE the Himyarite king **Joseph** (Dhu Nuwas/Yusuf/Masruq), who had rebelled
against [[aksum|Aksumite]] Christian overlordship the previous year, ordered the massacre
of the Christian population of [[najran]] after a thirteen-month siege conducted by his
agent **Sharahil Yaqbul dhu-Yazan**. The primary contemporary source is Sharahil's own
July 523 dedicatory inscription, discovered at Hima, which records that he "positioned
himself against Najran," took massive plunder, and that the operation left **12,500
Najrani dead**. A separately discovered (2014) inscription records the size of the
Himyarite army itself as 12,000 — a figure far below the 120,000 sometimes cited in
Christian literary accounts, illustrating the wide gulf between the epigraphic and
literary evidentiary traditions for this episode.

Christian literary tradition preserves a richer, more dramatic narrative. The *Book of
the Himyarites* and letters attributed to **Simeon of Beth-Arsham** — the most reliable
of which, "Letter C," survives embedded in Ps.-Zachariah's Chronicle — give a first-hand
account of Joseph boasting of the massacre at Ramla before [[al-mundhir-iii-lakhmid]].
Per Letter C, Joseph swore a false oath of safety to lure the Najrani Christians out of
their defenses, seized their wealth, exhumed and desecrated the body of their dead
bishop, and executed a number of Christians including a figure named **Arethas**, later
the subject of the hagiographic *Martyrdom of Arethas*.

Al-Tabari's later Arab-Islamic version offers a different, more theatrical account:
Joseph offered the Najranis conversion to Judaism or death; those who refused conversion
were burned, put to the sword, or mutilated in a mass trench execution, with a death
toll given as "nearly twenty thousand." A sole survivor, **Daws Dhu Thalaban**, is said
to have escaped on horseback to raise the alarm. Among the dead in this tradition was the
elderly **Abdallah b. Thamir**, credited by legend with introducing Christianity to
Najran.

## Causal Analysis

The massacre followed directly from Joseph's seizure of the Himyarite throne after the
death of the pro-Christian king Madikarib Yafur (521/2) and his subsequent rebellion
against Aksumite suzerainty, including the massacre of the Aksumite garrison and sacking
of the church at Zafar. Najran's large, prominent Christian community — long tied to
[[al-hira]] and, by this point, to northern Arab Christian networks rather than to
Himyar's Judeo-Monotheist establishment — represented both a religious and a political
target for Joseph's assertion of an independent, non-Christian Himyarite state.

## Consequence Analysis

News of the massacre — carried west by Simeon of Beth-Arsham's appeal, or in the
Arab-Islamic tradition by Daws Dhu Thalaban — prompted a renewed Aksumite invasion,
possibly with Roman naval support, which toppled Joseph and restored Christian rule at
Zafar under an Aksumite nominee. An anti-Jewish pogrom followed the reconquest. The
massacre entered a durable Christian hagiographic tradition (the *Martyrdom of Arethas*,
the *Book of the Himyarites*) that shaped western perceptions of South Arabia for
generations, and some scholars (with Bowersock among the skeptics) have proposed links
between the massacre and Quranic sura 85 ("the people of the trench") and sura 105. More
broadly, the episode helped precipitate the diplomatic realignment of the 520s–530s in
which Rome and Persia both sought Arabian and Red Sea proxies — visible in Justinian's
near-contemporaneous elevation of [[al-harith-ibn-jabala]] as super-phylarch.

## Historiography

The Najran massacre is unusually well attested for a 6th-century South Arabian event,
combining a contemporary Sabaic inscription (Sharahil's own account, with its precise
and comparatively modest casualty/army-size figures) with an extensive later Christian
martyrological literature (the *Book of the Himyarites*, Simeon of Beth-Arsham's
letters, the *Martyrdom of Arethas*) and a separate Arab-Islamic historiographical
tradition (al-Tabari) with its own, much larger casualty figures and distinct narrative
details (the trench execution, Daws Dhu Thalaban's escape). The scale discrepancy
between the 12,500 of the epigraphic record and the 120,000 of some Christian accounts
is a textbook case of hagiographic inflation, and the proposed Quranic connections (sura
85/105) remain contested — Bowersock disputes the sura 85 link specifically.
