# Crimean War

The **Crimean War** (1853–1856) was a major conflict fought between the Russian Empire and an alliance composed of the Ottoman Empire, Great Britain, France, and the Kingdom of Sardinia. Prompted by disputes over influence in the Near East, the war culminated in Russia's defeat and the Siege of Sevastopol. The conflict exposed the profound technological, administrative, and economic backwardness of Russian autocracy, forcing the new emperor, Alexander II, to launch the Great Reforms.

## Narrative

The war arose from the collapse of the Concert of Europe over the "Eastern Question"—the political future of the declining Ottoman Empire. In 1852, disputes arose between Catholic France and Orthodox Russia over the custody of the Holy Places in Palestine. Nicholas I used this opportunity to demand a protectorate over all Orthodox Christian subjects in the Ottoman Empire. When the Sultan, backed by Great Britain, refused the demand, Russian troops occupied the Danubian Principalities (Moldavia and Wallachia) in July 1853. The Ottoman Empire declared war in October 1853.

In November 1853, the Russian fleet destroyed the Ottoman fleet at the **Battle of Sinop**. Fearing a total collapse of Turkey and Russian control of Constantinople and the Straits, Great Britain and France entered the war in March 1854, followed later by the Kingdom of Sardinia. 

To bring the war to a decisive end, the Allied forces launched an invasion of the **Crimean Peninsula** in September 1854, targeting Russia's primary Black Sea naval base at **Sevastopol**. The siege of the fortified city lasted for nearly a year (September 1854 to September 1855). Despite heroic defense (highlighted by the surgeon Nicholas Pirogov's medical innovations and the leadership of Admiral Nakhimov), Sevastopol fell to the Allies in September 1855.

Exhausted, financially broken, and threatened with Austrian intervention, Russia sued for peace. Nicholas I had died in February 1855, and his successor, Alexander II, signed the **Treaty of Paris (1856)**. The treaty neutralized the Black Sea (prohibiting Russia from maintaining a navy or fortifications there) and forced Russia to cede southern Bessarabia.

## Causal Analysis

- `caused_by:` **The Eastern Question:** The declining power of the Ottoman Empire created a geopolitical rivalry. Nicholas I sought to expand Russian influence in the Balkans and the Mediterranean, which clashed with British determination to protect trade routes to India and French imperial ambitions.
- `caused_by:` **Diplomatic Miscalculation:** Nicholas I mistakenly believed that Austria and Prussia would support him (due to his intervention in Hungary in 1849) and that Great Britain would not fight. This led to Russia's diplomatic isolation.

Nicholas... negotiated with London and Paris... According to the Treaty of Adrianople that Nicholas concluded with the sultan on September 2 (14) of that year, the Ottomans formally ceded Georgia, confirmed Greek as well as Serbian autonomy and granted substantial concessions in the Danubian principalities... St Petersburg also won important strategic gains, including control of the Danube River’s mouth." "Impressive as they were, Nicholas’s gains belied considerable restraint... The tsarist ascent in Turkey led to considerable alarm in Britain... The Straits Convention of 1 (13) June 1841... forced Russia to backtrack from its demands at Unkiar-Skelessi." "The origins of the Crimean War... remain a source of lively controversy. What is clear is that the conflict began, almost innocuously, over a French attempt in 1850 to extend the Catholic Church’s rights to maintain the Holy Places, sacred sites of Christendom in Ottoman-ruled Palestine. Motivated by President Louis Napoleon’s effort to court domestic political support, the ploy elicited a strong response from Nicholas, who insisted on the prerogatives of the Orthodox Church. Although none of the powers sought war, the tsar’s clumsy diplomacy, the intransigence of the sultan and the machinations of Stratford Canning, Britain’s Russophobe minister to Constantinople, all helped transform a ‘quarrel of monks’ into the first major clash among the powers since Waterloo." "The Crimean War itself was more a diplomatic than a military defeat for Russia... ## Consequence Analysis

- `produced:` **Exposure of Russian Backwardness:** The war revealed that Russia's serf-based economy, lack of railways, corrupt bureaucracy, and obsolete military technology (smoothbore muskets against Allied rifles, wooden sailing ships against steamships) could not compete with industrialized European powers.
- `produced:` **Demoralization and Peace:** The defeat shattered the myth of Russian military invincibility established in 1812 and led to the humiliating Treaty of Paris.
- `produced:` **The Great Reforms:** The defeat convinced the imperial government and Alexander II that the survival of the empire required a comprehensive modernization. It led directly to the **Emancipation of the Serfs (1861)**, as well as the reforms of the judiciary, local government, and the military (universal conscription of 1874).

## Actors

- **Nicholas I:** The Russian emperor whose aggressive diplomacy provoked the war and who died during the conflict.
- **Alexander II:** Succeeded Nicholas I in 1855 and was forced to negotiate the peace and execute the reforms.
- **The Allied Coalition:** Great Britain, France, the Ottoman Empire, and Sardinia, who united to contain Russian expansion.

## Historiography

Western historians traditionally view the Crimean War as a product of diplomatic blunder and Russophobia, focusing on the military mismanagement of both sides (such as the Charge of the Light Brigade). Russian and Soviet historians emphasize the systemic causes, analyzing how the war exposed the internal crisis of the serf-based autocracy. Modern scholars, such as Rieber, highlight the structural military consequences: the war forced Russia to abandon its standing peasant army in favor of a modern reserve system based on universal conscription.
