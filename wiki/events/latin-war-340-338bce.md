# The Latin War

## Narrative

The **Latin War** (341–338 BCE) was the conflict between Rome and a coalition of
Latin cities (joined by some Campanian communities and the Volsci) that had
formed in response to Rome's growing dominance over the alliance. It was the
decisive test of whether the Latin–Roman relationship, formalized in the
[[foedus-cassianum-493bce|foedus Cassianum]] of 493 BCE as a nominal alliance of
equals, could survive Roman expansion.

### Causes

The immediate trigger was the diplomatic reversal of the **First Samnite War**:
Rome had allied with the Campanians against the Samnites in 343, then reversed
course in 341 and renewed the Samnite alliance, leaving the Campanians and
Sidicini to fend for themselves. The Latins and Campanians, feeling betrayed,
formed a joint coalition. The war that began c. 341 had a deeper cause: the
Latins were no longer being treated as equals in the bilateral alliance but as
effective subjects — required to supply troops and land for Roman expansion
without receiving equal political representation.

Livy records specific Latin demands: admission to the Roman citizenship and
the right to supply one consul and half the senate. Cornell notes these demands
are "clearly anachronistic" — they echo the aspirations of Italian insurgents
in the Social War of 91 BCE and cannot have been formulated in this precise form
in 340. But the underlying grievance (subordination without representation) was
genuine.

### Campaigns

The specific details of the campaigns are largely unrecoverable. Livy gives
accounts but his source material is a mixture of annalistic elaboration and rhetorical
invention. The tradition records the campaigns of 340–338 BCE, including:

- **Battle of Veseris** (340 BCE, near Mt. Vesuvius): The most famous engagement,
  remembered for two incidents:
  1. **T. Manlius Torquatus** executed his own son for engaging in single combat
     without orders — a stark display of Roman military discipline
  2. **P. Decius Mus** (consul) performed the *devotio* — dedicating himself and
     the enemy to the gods of the underworld and charging headlong into the opposing
     ranks to win the battle at the cost of his own life. Cornell considers the
     devotio "probably" based in fact; the son's execution is "possibly" historical.

- The Volsci of Privernum were defeated in 341.
- Further Roman victories in 340 ended the fighting temporarily; mopping-up
  operations against Sidicini, Aurunci, and Volsci continued through 339–338.
- **Capture of Pedum** (338 BCE): The last significant Latin stronghold; its fall
  opened the way for the comprehensive settlement.

## The Settlement (338 BCE)

Roman victory was followed by the most creative diplomatic settlement in early
Republican history: the **[[roman-commonwealth-formation-338bce|formation of the
Roman Commonwealth]]**, which dissolved the Latin League and reorganized Latin
and Campanian communities into a hierarchy of statuses. See the dedicated event
page for the full settlement terms.

## Significance

The Latin War was the **structural turning point** of Roman Italian expansion.
Before 338 BCE, Rome operated within the framework of a bilateral alliance with
nominally equal Latin partners. After 338 BCE, Rome operated as the uncontested
hegemon of central Italy, with all the surrounding communities organized in
individual subordinate relationships. The creative settlement gave Rome a template
for incorporating conquered peoples that it would use for the next two centuries
of Italian expansion.

Cornell (and De Sanctis before him) regards the 338 BCE settlement as the **critical
moment** (*momento critico*) of Roman history — the institutional breakthrough that
made the eventual conquest of the Mediterranean possible.

## Historiography

Source quality for the Latin War is poor: Livy's account is mainly from annalists
of the first century BCE writing with full knowledge of the Social War of 91 BCE,
which they used to color the Latin demands retrospectively. The basic outline
(Roman victory, dissolution of the League, creative settlement) is confirmed by
later writers and by the political history of the following century. The specific
military episodes are largely unverifiable.

The Battle of Veseris and the devotio of Decius Mus are particularly contested:
the *devotio* ritual is attested in Roman religion independently, and it would
be unparsimonious to dismiss both the 340 BCE devotio and the 295 BCE devotio
(when P. Decius Mus II repeated his father's sacrifice at Sentinum) as complete
fabrications. The ritualized parallel suggests genuine family tradition, though
elaborated.
