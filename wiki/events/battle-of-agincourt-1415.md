# Battle of Agincourt (25 October 1415)

## Narrative

The most famous English victory of the [[hundred-years-war-1337|Hundred Years' War]] and the high
point of the longbow system. After taking **Harfleur**, Henry V marched a reduced and sickly army
toward Calais and was brought to battle by the much larger French royal army (the king, Charles VI,
being incapacitated; command fell to the Constable **d'Albret** and Marshal **Boucicaut**). Fought
in a **narrow, rain-sodden defile** between the woods of Agincourt and Tramecourt, the battle turned
on terrain and mud: the dismounted French men-at-arms, packed onto a front too narrow for their
numbers, bogged down in the mud under arrow storm and were overwhelmed. The
[[cambridge-history-of-war-v2|Cambridge History of War, Vol. 2]] reads it, through Anne Curry, less
as chivalric miracle than as proof of the **recruited, paid, longbow-based army** and of the
chronic French failure to coordinate its troops. Henry's order to **kill prisoners** at a critical
moment is the battle's enduring controversy.

## Causal Analysis

`caused_by: [[hundred-years-war-1337]]` — the wider Anglo-French war and Henry V's revival of the
English claim. Proximately produced by Henry's decision to march (not re-embark) after Harfleur, and
decided by the **defile, the mud, and the French command's disunity** with the king absent — the
volume's recurring explanation for French battlefield vulnerability.

## Consequence Analysis

`enabled:` Henry V's **conquest of Normandy**; `produced:` the **Treaty of Troyes (1420)**
recognising him as heir to the French throne. Agincourt marks the **apogee** of English fortunes and
of the longbow system, from which the later French recovery (artillery, standing companies) would
eventually reverse the war's verdict by 1453.

## Historiography

The battle is unusually well documented for its bias-riven variety: the anonymous *Gesta Henrici Quinti* (an eyewitness-adjacent English clerical account written within a few years, openly propagandistic in Henry's favour), the Burgundian chronicler Enguerrand de Monstrelet, and Jean de Wavrin (who claimed an uncle present in the French ranks) give partly conflicting troop numbers and sequences. French chivalric numbers for their own army were long inflated in later retellings to explain the disaster; modern prosopographical work using pay records and muster rolls (notably Anne Curry's revisionist studies) has narrowed the gap between the two armies considerably, overturning the traditional picture of an overwhelming French numerical advantage. Henry's order to kill French prisoners mid-battle is reported across the major chronicles but its scale, timing, and justification remain debated. Shakespeare's *Henry V* and nineteenth-century nationalist historiography further overlaid the battle with a mythology of the "band of brothers" that modern military historians treat cautiously against the administrative and tactical record.
