# Japanese Coup in Indochina (Operation Bright Moon, 9 March 1945)

On the evening of 9 March 1945, Japanese forces executed a carefully planned coup (Operation Bright Moon) that destroyed French colonial authority across all of Indochina in a matter of days. It was the single most important event enabling the Viet Minh’s later seizure of power.

## Narrative

Japanese suspicions had grown that the French might switch sides after the liberation of France. After an ultimatum that was rejected, Japanese troops moved simultaneously against French administrative centers, garrisons, and communications. Most French units were taken by surprise. Senior officers were captured; many garrisons were overrun or isolated within hours.

A force of roughly 2,000 under General Marcel Alessandri escaped northward and reached Dien Bien Phu, later joined by General Gabriel Sabattier. Ultimately about 5,700 French and colonial troops (including 2,400 Europeans) straggled into southern China, where they were disarmed by the Chinese. De Gaulle had hoped to maintain at least a token presence in the northwest; that hope was crushed.

## Causal Analysis

**caused_by:** Japanese strategic decision to preempt any French defection or Allied landing by eliminating French military and administrative capacity while it was still possible.

**contributed_to:** The visible, rapid collapse of French power in front of the Vietnamese population destroyed the psychological foundation of colonial rule (small number of Europeans controlling millions through prestige and minimal force).

## Consequence Analysis

The coup created the conditions for the August Revolution. With French authority gone and Japanese forces themselves on the verge of defeat, the Viet Minh faced minimal organized opposition when they moved to seize power after Japan’s surrender. French colonial rule would never fully recover its pre-March 1945 credibility.

## Actors

- Japanese: Matsumoto (diplomat who delivered the ultimatum); 22nd Army units.
- French: Governor-General Decoux; various garrison commanders (most overwhelmed).
- Viet Minh: Positioned to exploit the resulting vacuum.

## Historiography

Logevall presents the coup as the decisive blow from which French imperial authority in Indochina never recovered. French accounts often stress the treachery and the logistical difficulties that prevented a coordinated response. Vietnamese histories view it as the moment when the colonial edifice visibly crumbled, opening the path to independence.
