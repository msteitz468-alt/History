## Narrative

The Jesuit Guaraní missions — the thirty pueblos (reducciones) clustered in three zones between the Río Uruguay, Río Paraná, and southern Paraguay — constituted the most elaborate indigenous–colonial institution in the Americas. Founded beginning in 1610, they brought roughly a century and a half of relative security and demographic growth to a Guaraní population already battered by [[encomienda]] labor, [[bandeirantes]] slave raids, and epidemic disease. They ended abruptly in 1767–68 when Charles III expelled the Society of Jesus from his dominions.

### Origins and Early Growth

When Jesuit fathers Marciel de Lorenzana and Francisco de San Martín founded San Ignacio Guazú in 1610 near the confluence of the Río Paraná and Río Paraguay, they were building on earlier Franciscan experiments. The Guaraní accepted Jesuit overtures "initially for the material benefits, especially plentiful iron tools and food, and for security, but aboriginal religious traditions prevented their accepting Catholic beliefs in the first mission generations." Jesuit proselytizing included coercion: "When some groups resisted Jesuit overtures, the fathers encouraged clients to attack the recalcitrant peoples."

By the 1620s eleven more missions existed in the Guairá region (now Paraná, Brazil) alongside eleven founded in northern Paraguay. The existence of these missions infuriated encomenderos, whose Guaraní labor the missions shielded.

### The Bandeirante Crisis and Guaraní Military Response (1620s–1641)

Brazilian [[bandeirantes]] from São Paulo struck the Guairá missions in the late 1620s, hunting Guaraní "like beasts." In the missions of San Antonio and San Miguel, survivors reported: "the poor Indians ran to take refuge in the church, where they [the Brazilians] killed them like cattle in a slaughter-house." After the devastating raid of 1631, twelve thousand Guaraní fled south under Father Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, abandoning the Guairá and exposing Spaniards of Villa Rica to danger.

The Guaraní response was military reorganization. In the fertile fields and pastures of Misiones, "Guaraní with Jesuit instructors organized an effective militia, with firearms, European weapons, and discipline." By 1646 the Peruvian viceroy authorized mission men to carry firearms. Guaraní mission militias defeated the bandeirante forces at the battle of Mbororé on the Río Uruguay in 1641, effectively ending large-scale Paulista raids on mission territory.

This militarization created a regional power the Spanish crown relied on: mission armies suppressed rebellions in Asunción in the 1640s, 1720s, and 1730s, and helped found Montevideo in 1726. The arrangement also exacerbated settler resentment of Jesuit influence.

### The Thirty Pueblos at Height (1700s)

By the early eighteenth century thirty Guaraní missions, "whose population fluctuated from 80,000 to 120,000, were clustered in three places: east of the Río Uruguay in present-day Rio Grande do Sul; between the Uruguay and the Río Paraná in Misiones, Argentina; and in southern Paraguay between the Paraná and the Río Tebicuary." In 1754, when the population was 101,752, seven eastern reductions had been prosperous for two generations.

Mission governance balanced Jesuit paternalism with Guaraní political structures. A Guaraní corregidor headed each mission's civil hierarchy; hereditary caciques acted as brokers between elites and the people; cabildos handled municipal affairs. An innovative artisan class included "blacksmiths, carpenters, statue makers, gilders, silversmiths, weavers, hat makers, rosary makers, makers of organs, bells, and things of bronze, of spinets, harps, flageolets, bugles and other musical instruments."

Mission life remained coercive. Agricultural labor, previously voluntary, became compulsory: "for the rest," Jesuits held, "it was necessary to beat them one or more times so that they sow and reap the necessary amount." The Guaraní resisted Jesuit prescriptions, preferring their own plots to communal fields, rejecting European plowing techniques, and retaining plural marriages and shamanistic practices underground. Women suspected of improper conduct were cloistered in houses of correction.

The mission economy exported high-quality hides and yerba mate (caamiñí grade) to markets in Buenos Aires, undercutting lay Paraguayan competitors in the yerba trade.

### The Treaty of Madrid and the Guaraní War (1750–1756)

In 1750 Spain and Portugal agreed in the Treaty of Madrid to exchange territory east of the Río Uruguay — where seven prosperous missions stood — for land in the Banda Oriental. Guaraní residents of San Nicolás, Santo Ángel, San Luis, San Lorenzo, San Borja, San Juan Bautista, and San Miguel were ordered to abandon homes, fields, pastures, and livestock and move west.

The Guaraní refused. "Ceding their homes to Brazilians enraged the Guaraní of these missions, whose mortal enemies they had been for a century." Sepe, corregidor of San Miguel, believed that their patron saint Michael had ordered them not to move, and led the initial resistance. He "conducted an effective Guaraní guerrilla effort, taking a Portuguese fort and spreading terror among the Portuguese invaders." After Sepe's death, Nicolás Neenguirú briefly led, but defected.

The war ended disastrously at the battle of Caaibate in February 1756: "the Guaraní suffered 1,400 deaths, the European allies but 3." The Iberian alliance routed a force lacking unified command and coherent strategy. The surviving Guaraní eventually returned to their homes in 1761, after the two crowns nullified the Treaty of Madrid.

Wright and Carneiro da Cunha emphasize that Jesuit blame for the war was politically convenient but false: "The persistence of powerful native leadership, enhanced by the military organization developed during the colonial period, and the strong sense of Guaraní ethnic identity certainly contributed to the articulation of the movement."

### Jesuit Expulsion and Mission Collapse (1767–1810s)

In 1767 Charles III expelled Jesuits from his dominions — driven by regalism, anti-Jesuit politics in Madrid, and royal desire to seize Jesuit wealth. Replacement priests frequently knew no Guaraní; administrators found no treasures and dismantled the economic controls essential to mission functioning. "Mission prosperity vanished. New administrators were thieves." Legal safeguards preventing sale of Guaraní property were removed; Spanish merchants, employers, and land-hungry settlers moved in.

The missions "slowly deteriorated." Skilled artisans, farmers, and herdsmen found employment in the Río de la Plata economy but did not "return to the forest" as many accounts alleged. In the 1780s mission Guaraní represented a quarter of Paraguay's 100,000 people but Indian rights dwindled. Paraguay's Dictator Francia destroyed Candelaria, Loreto, and other missions in the 1820s to create a buffer zone. The Wars of Independence prompted another exodus: thousands of Guaraní joined porteno armies or dispersed into the rural poor. By 1821, of 30,000 Guaraní who had populated the Seven Missions in Jesuit times, only 3,000 remained.

In 1848 Paraguayan President Carlos Antonio López incorporated remaining Indians as citizens, ending legal segregation but stripping communal land and cattle protections.

## Consequence Analysis

The mission system delayed full Guaraní proletarianization by roughly a century. It created an educated, militarily organized Guaraní elite that prosecuted the Guaraní War (the most significant indigenous armed resistance in the Río de la Plata region) and produced the linguistic and cultural substrate of modern Paraguay. However, post-Jesuit collapse stripped the Guaraní of land protections, communal property, and political autonomy in accelerated fashion. The Guaraní language outlasted the missions: "by 1800 the Guaraní of the civil province was a new language" that converged with mission Guaraní after 1768, and in mid-twentieth-century Paraguay Guaraní speakers outnumbered Spanish speakers 92 to 52 percent.

## Actors

- **[[guarani]]** — primary agents and beneficiaries/subjects; drove mission adoption for material and security reasons, resisted Jesuit coercion through non-compliance and flight, organized military resistance in the Guaraní War.
- **Society of Jesus (Jesuits)** — organized the reductions, trained militia, managed the yerba and hide economy, acted as political intermediaries; expelled 1767.
- **[[bandeirantes]]** — Paulista slave raiders whose attacks in the 1620s–1630s triggered mass Guaraní migration south and the militarization of the missions.
- **Sepe (José Tiarayu)** — corregidor of San Miguel, led initial armed resistance to Treaty of Madrid relocation; killed in skirmish before Caaibate.
- **Nicolás Neenguirú** — corregidor of Concepción, briefly commanded Guaraní resistance after Sepe's death; defected to governor of Buenos Aires.
- **payagua** — Guaycuruan riverine peoples who raided mission supply lines and Jesuit shipping on the Río Paraguay.

## Historiography

The missions have attracted two major interpretive traditions. A "Golden Age" or "Jesuit Republic" literature (Caraman, Cunninghame Graham) treats the missions as benevolent utopias destroyed by Bourbon politics. A revisionist tradition (Melia, Carbonell de Masy, Saeger) emphasizes Guaraní agency: Guaraní chose missions for material and security benefits, shaped governance through caciques, resisted Jesuit agricultural prescriptions, and preserved underground religious practice. The Guaraní War has been re-read as indigenous resistance driven by "powerful native leadership" and ethnic identity rather than Jesuit manipulation.

A separate debate concerns the Guaraní population: Jesuit and colonial sources imply 200,000 Guaraní in the Guairá region and 60,000 in Tape in the first half of the seventeenth century, figures confirmed by Bartomeu Melia's demographic work but requiring methodological caution given source biases.
