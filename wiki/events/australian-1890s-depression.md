# Australian 1890s Depression

A severe economic collapse beginning in 1890 that ended three decades of the
Australian colonial long boom, prompted the formation of the labour party movement,
and reshaped the political conditions for federation.

## Narrative

The 1890s opened with "sufficient warning of the coming austerity." The 1880s had
seen "a heated boom in land and mining shares" — most feverishly in Broken Hill
silver, where BHP shares "raced from £174 to over £400 between December 1887 and
February 1888." Colonial banks tightened lending in late 1888. Land and share prices
edged downward in 1889 and by 1891 "entered free-fall."

The international trigger was the **Baring Crisis** of November 1890: the Bank of
London's bailout of the Baring investment house "as a result of bad debts in
Argentina caused British financiers to halt their flow of credit to the Australian
colonies." Simultaneously "a steep decline in world prices for commodities such as
wool, silver and sugar" contracted British investment from 1891. Since the Australian
colonies were "so dependent on the export of commodities and import of capital, they
experienced a much more severe downturn than North America and the old world."

Building societies and land and finance companies "plunged into insolvency in 1891–92."
Worse came in 1893: "of eighteen banks with headquarters on the eastern Australian
mainland, thirteen collapsed between January and May." The Commercial Bank of
Australia and the City of Melbourne Bank — institutions "almost unthinkable" to fail
— went under. Melbourne unemployment reached an estimated 26.5–28.3% in 1893.
Up to 6,000 Melbourne families lost homes to foreclosure. "Harsher still was the
fate of tenants evicted because they were behind on rent."

Not all regions suffered equally. Western Australia was "caught up in a gold rush
between 1892 and 1896." Western Tasmania had a copper-mining boom at Queenstown
in the late 1890s. Queensland's sugar districts, where land values fell earlier
due to falling sugar prices, were hard hit. The departure of thousands of men from
eastern colonies to the Western Australian goldfields at Coolgardie (from 1891)
left "deserted wives and children to fend for themselves."

## Causal Analysis

The depression was the product of a financialised boom meeting external credit
contraction. The structural vulnerability was colonial over-dependence on British
capital: "two-thirds of private capital formation in Melbourne was directed to
residential construction during the 1880s, whereas in Britain the figure was
one-fifth." The Baring Crisis was the exogenous shock that exposed this dependency.
Commodity price falls compounded it. The drought (1895–1903), overlapping the
financial depression, created what contemporaries called the "Federation Drought"
as a second wave of rural distress.

The Great Strikes of 1890–94 (maritime strike 1890, Queensland shearers' strike
1891, Broken Hill miners' strike 1892, Queensland shearers again 1894) interacted
with the economic crisis: they preceded the worst of the financial collapse, and
their defeat — by employers using police and troops — accelerated the turn to
electoral politics and the formation of the Labor Party.

## Consequence Analysis

**Immediate:** Bank collapses, mass unemployment, evictions, and the destruction of
working-class savings. Union membership fell from roughly one in five male wage
earners in 1890 to "closer to one in twenty in 1896."

**Political — Labour Party formation:** The maritime and shearers' strikes' defeat
prompted "the establishment of labour parties in the southern and eastern mainland
colonies between November 1890 and May 1891." In NSW the Labor Electoral League
won "36 out of 141 seats in the lower house to hold the balance of power after the
election of 1891." Queensland formed the world's first labour government (briefly)
in 1899.

**Legislative — 'Civilising capitalism':** Colonial governments in the late 1890s
introduced "a minimum wage, welfare payments for those most in need, courts for
the arbitration of industrial disputes" and — in NSW under Lyne's ministry in
1900 — old-age pensions and compulsory arbitration. Victoria's wages boards
tackled sweated trades from 1896.

**Aboriginal policy:** Aboriginal workers, "crucial to the survival of pastoralists
and pearlers in northern Australia…particularly the case during the Depression,"
were subject to intensified state control. Queensland's Aboriginal Protection and
Restriction of the Sale of Opium Act (1897) became the model for similar
legislation across Australia. The removal of 'half-caste' children from families
"first began to be widely advocated in the 1890s."

**Federation:** The Depression paradoxically contributed to federation. The "uneven
effect around the country would 'bring the six colonies closer in size and status…
making it easier to present the case for Federation as a coming together of equals.'"
The Corowa Plan (1893), which gave the federation movement its democratic momentum,
emerged from a border district most exposed to colonial separation's inconveniences.
The depression also "distracted from grander visions" in 1891–93, delaying the
first round of constitutional ratification.

## Historiography

Ch 9 (Melissa Bellanta, "Rethinking the 1890s") explicitly argues against the
older labour-historian interpretation that treated the 1890s solely as the crucible
of the labour movement. Bellanta insists on the decade's cultural complexity:
consumer spending, spectator sport, bohemianism, first-wave feminism, and popular
entertainment coexisted with "economically grim, politically intense" conditions.
The chapter engages with the controversy: "were [the 1890s] the cradle or graveyard
of hopes for radical social change?" and refuses resolution. The economic causes
are framed through Bellanta's narrative but rest primarily on E.A. Boehm,
*Prosperity and Depression in Australia, 1887–1897* (Oxford, 1971) and Luke
Trainor, *British Imperialism and Australian Nationalism* (CUP, 1994). The
term *fin de siècle* is explicitly rejected as misleading for Australia's context.
