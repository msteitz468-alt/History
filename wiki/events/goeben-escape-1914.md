# The Escape of the Goeben and the Breslau

The escape of the German battlecruiser **Goeben** and light cruiser **Breslau** from the western Mediterranean to the Dardanelles in August 1914 was a pivotal naval and political event that directly facilitated the Ottoman Empire’s entry into the First World War on the side of the Central Powers.

## Narrative

Shortly after 10.30 on the morning of 4 August 1914 Captain Francis Kennedy, commanding the British battle cruisers *Indomitable* and *Indefatigable*, sighted the two German cruisers. Kennedy was sailing westwards, north of Bône and south of Sardinia, under orders to close the exit of the Mediterranean at Gibraltar. However, the course being steered by the German ships was to the east. Earlier that day the Goeben had bombarded Philippeville and the Breslau Bône, both embarkation ports for the XIX French corps en route from North Africa to metropolitan France. Although his ships had done little damage, Rear-Admiral Wilhelm Souchon had broken off the action and was now proceeding to the Straits of Messina. As the four cruisers converged, they kept their guns trained fore and aft. They passed each other at a distance of 8,000 yards without an exchange of either shots or courtesies. The British ultimatum to Germany was not due to expire until midnight.

Kennedy swung his ships round and set off to shadow the Germans. In her trials the Goeben had achieved a speed of 27 knots. But she had been sent out to the Mediterranean precipitately in 1912, during the first Balkan war, and her engines had created problems ever since. She had spent July in dry dock at Pola... In the ensuing chase three out of the Goeben’s twenty-four boilers failed, and, although she occasionally managed 24 knots, her average speed was 22. ... Souchon, on the other hand, was determined to exploit his marginal advantage. His stokers fell unconscious in the effort to raise sufficient steam in the summer heat, and four died from scalding. By nightfall Kennedy had lost sight of the German cruisers.

The combination of leaky boilers and great speeds had depleted the Goeben’s coal supplies. Souchon therefore bunkered at Messina but was only allowed twenty-four hours to do so by the Italians, who had affirmed their neutrality on 2 August. ... On the evening of 6 August, the Goeben and the Breslau... left Messina and steered a course for the Adriatic. They were duly spotted by HMS *Gloucester*... Gloucester stuck close... observed that the entry to the Adriatic was a feint, and reported that their course was now towards Cape Matapan and the Greek Peloponnese.

Although grossly inferior in armament, Gloucester engaged the Goeben on the evening of 6 August. But Troubridge did not follow suit. ... At 3.47 a.m. on the morning of 7 August he turned away. He was in tears as he did so. ... he signalled to Milne that he had abandoned the chase.

Milne still expected Souchon to double back westwards... Thus Souchon enjoyed an uninterrupted sixty hours in the Greek archipelago, completing a leisurely coaling in the early hours of 10 August. ... At 5 p.m. on 10 August his two ships anchored at the entrance to the Dardanelles. ... The subsequent news that the Turks were guiding the Goeben and Breslau into Constantinople came as a complete surprise to him. The Germans’ escape rendered the actions of every British naval commander, with the distinguished exception of Kelly of the Gloucester, not prudent but incompetent.

Souchon’s orders up until 2 August were exactly those reflected in Entente dispositions—he was to operate in the western Mediterranean or break through to the Atlantic. But on that day Turkey and Germany formed a secret alliance. Tirpitz and Pohl... agreed that Souchon should go to Constantinople; Souchon had received these orders even before the attacks on Philippeville and Bône.

## Causal Analysis

The escape succeeded due to a combination of German determination and British/Entente command, intelligence, and policy failures:

- Goeben’s marginal speed advantage exploited despite mechanical defects; British reluctance to push *Indomitable* (short of stokers) to full effort.
- Troubridge’s interpretation of Churchill’s “superior force” order as applying to the Goeben itself (rather than Austro-Hungarian/Italian fleets) and his respect for modern naval gunnery range led to prudent withdrawal.
- Milne’s respect for Italian neutrality, dilatory bunkering in Malta, and Admiralty confusion over Austrian hostilities prevented effective pursuit east of Sicily.
- “Most crucial of all, the Admiralty did nothing to correct the fundamental assumption that the Germans would be aiming for the western Mediterranean and not the eastern.”
- Secret alliance of 2 August redirected Souchon eastwards; Austrian fleet under Haus refused to sortie south in support due to incomplete mobilization and contradictory orders.

Britain’s longer-term imperial calculation (Turkish naval strength blocking Russian Black Sea access) and desire to restrict the war to Europe contributed to complacency; conspiracy theories of deliberate facilitation are not supported, but the failure to correct assumptions was critical.

## Consequence Analysis

- Immediate: Goeben and Breslau neutralized for Mediterranean operations (did not reenter until 1918); combined with Austrian/Italian fleets no longer a threat to Entente.
- Political: “The voyage of the Goeben and the Breslau was an opportunity to widen the war, not restrict it.” Directly enabled Ottoman entry (see [[events/turkey-entry-1914]]).
- Naval command: Exposed British Mediterranean Fleet leadership (Milne, Troubridge); Admiralty wireless direction criticized for imprecision.
- Strategic: Symbol of German success in widening conflict to Asia/Africa; delivered modern warships to Turkey, shifting Black Sea balance and blocking Russian imports via Dardanelles.
- Rationalization adopted by Entente: ships “taken out of the Mediterranean.”

## Historiography

Grounded exclusively in Hew Strachan, *The First World War, Volume I: To Arms* (2001), Ch. 8. Strachan emphasizes contingency, command error over conspiracy, and the strategic divergence (Britain restrict vs Germany widen the war). Notes decrypts of Berlin-Souchon traffic known but not acted upon effectively. The episode is presented as emblematic of opening uncertainty (“who was at war with whom”) and the failure of joint Franco-British Mediterranean planning.
