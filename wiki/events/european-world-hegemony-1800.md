## Narrative

The establishment of European world hegemony in the generation of approximately 1776–1815 is, in Hodgson's analysis, the third of three "more or less simultaneous" world-historical events of this period — alongside the Industrial Revolution and the French Revolution — and in global terms arguably the most consequential.

Hodgson uses "hegemony" carefully, distinguishing it from direct rule: Europeans "did not immediately dominate all lands in the world, but they immediately dominated all interstate relations — political or commercial or even intellectual." European merchants were "the leading foreign merchants in the ports of all the world"; European consular representatives "the leading foreign political figures to be reckoned with." This hegemony was exercised "even before they had imposed their conditions effectively within all regions."

The event was not purely a byproduct of European internal developments but "was partly parallel in its internal mechanism to the European events themselves": as the Napoleonic crisis forced Europeans to develop their potentialities to the maximum, the same pressure registered as a readjustment crisis in Muslim lands where the eighteenth-century Occidental presence had been most serious. The French Revolution's eruption in Europe paradoxically gave some Muslim reformers (Selim III) a temporary respite from European pressure to attempt internal reforms — precisely the respite that allowed the *Nizam-e Jadid* of 1792.

By the Congress of Vienna (1815), the European powers were "in a position to issue in earnest such a decree as the pope had once issued by way of anticipation" (the papal division of the world between Spain and Portugal, which Hodgson dismisses as "a mere gesture"). Throughout the nineteenth century, "affairs of remote parts of the world were, in fact, settled among the Powers."

## Causal Analysis

**Primary cause: the [[great-western-transmutation]].** The hegemony rested on a "decisively higher level of social power" than was to be found elsewhere — not merely military, but economic, intellectual, and organizational. "European enterprises, such as firms or churches or, of course, governments, could muster a degree of power, intellectual, economic and social, which was of a different order from what could be mustered among even the most wealthy or vigorous peoples in the rest of the world."

**Contributing cause: Napoleonic Wars as catalyst.** The wars forced European powers to maximize their military and economic capacities and drew them into deeper involvement in internal Islamicate affairs. The opium trade, whose "decisive growth" Hodgson dates to the Napoleonic years, serves as an indicator of how the Far East too was being drawn into the new order even before its full implications were recognized.

**Contributing cause: prior commercial penetration.** European commercial presence in Islamicate lands had been growing through the eighteenth century, progressively displacing local luxury industries. By the generation of 1789, this had already altered the internal balance of forces in Muslim countries such that the existing order had become untenable — the hegemony was the overt political expression of an economic subordination already underway.

## Consequence Analysis

The hegemony institutionalized the [[complementary-dependence|complementary dependent]] order. Politically, "all peoples had to adjust their governments to a modern European international political order." Economically, they had to "adjust their economies — a harder task — to the competition of technically industrialized Europe." Intellectually, they had to "adjust their mental outlook to the challenge of modern science as studied in Europe."

Even formally "independent" areas could retain local autonomy "only to the extent that they provided European merchants, European missionaries, even European tourists, with a certain minimum of that type of international 'law and order' to which they had become accustomed in Europe" — for otherwise, "the European powers would feel forced to intervene; and wherever one of them concentrated its efforts it would almost infallibly succeed."

The long-term consequence was the institutionalization of the "development gap": a global order divided between technicalized and non-technicalized societies, "inescapably bound up with each other, yet... bafflingly hard to comprehend each other." Kipling's "East is East and West is West and never the twain shall meet" expressed the cultural dimension of this structural fact.

## Actors

Key actors in the Islamicate world's encounter with hegemony:

- **[[ottoman-empire|Selim III]]** (r. 1789–1807): launched the *Nizam-e Jadid* (1792); deposed by Janissary-*ulama* coalition (1807); his reforms nonetheless opened the path for Mahmud II.
- **Mahmud II** (r. 1808–39): progressively implemented the transformation Selim envisaged; destroyed the Janissaries (1826); the generation of 1789's full Ottoman consequences unfold under his reign.
- **Memed-Ali** (1769–1848): Albanian Ottoman commander who mastered Egypt (1805), launched the most ambitious autonomous Muslim response to the Transmutation; "a selfish, illiterate genius" in Hodgson's phrase.
- **Warren Hastings / Cornwallis**: the two governors-general whose reforms (1772–93) established the administrative and legal framework for British India.
- **Wellesley** (governor-general 1798–1805): established British military pre-eminence throughout India.

## Historiography

Hodgson insists the decisive period is 1776–1815, not 1500 (the conventional "modern" starting point). Dating from 1500 "obscures crucial distinctions" by treating the first Portuguese-Iberian expansion as equivalent in world-historical significance to the Transmutation itself. What mattered was not the "new outreach of the West" (already present in the sixteenth century) but "the new level of social process in the world, which happened to have emerged in the West."

He is equally critical of any "civilizational decline" narrative for Islamic history. The collapse of Islamicate power in this generation reflects "not... inherent defects in Islamicate culture, whether religious obscurantism or political incapacity" but the structural features of the Transmutation's irruptive impact: its acceleration of historical pace beyond the capacity of agrarianate-level diffusion to absorb.
