# The "Edict of Milan"

The so-called **"Edict of Milan"** (313) was the joint declaration by [[constantine-the-great|Constantine]]
and **Licinius** granting **religious toleration** to Christians and restoring their confiscated
property, ending the [[great-persecution-303|Great Persecution]]. Treated in [[bowman-cah-v12-2005|CAH
XII]] Ch. 4 (**Averil Cameron**).

## Narrative

In the winter of 312–13 Constantine and Licinius met at **Milan**, cementing their alliance with the
marriage of Licinius to Constantine's sister Constantia. The resulting policy — preserved as a letter
in both Lactantius and Eusebius — granted Christians (and all cults) **freedom of worship** and
ordered the **restoration of church property**. Cameron stresses that the document is **misleadingly
named**: it was a **joint** declaration (not Constantine's alone), and it built directly on the
**edict of toleration issued by Galerius in 311** that had already called off the persecution.

## Causal Analysis

The measure followed Constantine's victory at the [[battle-of-the-milvian-bridge-312|Milvian Bridge]]
and the precedent of Galerius' deathbed edict of 311. It reflected both Constantine's growing
Christian commitment and the simple political recognition — after a decade of futile persecution —
that the church could not be eradicated.

## Consequence Analysis

Toleration secured, the church entered into open and state-favored existence in the territories of the
two emperors. But the settlement was **not yet empire-wide**: **Maximinus** renewed persecution in the
East in 312, and only Licinius' defeat of Maximinus in 313 (and, ultimately, Constantine's defeat of
Licinius in 324) extended toleration everywhere. The edict is the legal hinge of the
[[christianization-of-the-roman-empire|Christianization of the empire]].

## Significance

Conventionally treated as the charter of Christian liberty, the "Edict of Milan" is better understood
(per Cameron) as **one step in a sequence** — Galerius 311, Milan 313, Constantine's eastern settlement
324 — by which the Roman state moved from persecution to patronage of the church.

## Historiography

### From Mitchell, Later Roman Empire (2015)

The compact at Milan in **313** proclaimed religious toleration for all but particularly favored Christians by definitively ending persecution, restoring Christian property, and compensating at state expense those who had obtained confiscated Christian possessions. After Constantine’s conversion and joint rule with Licinius (313–324), Licinius’s letter to Asia Minor, Syria, and Egypt guaranteed restoration of privileges/property (Lactantius *DMP* 48; Eusebius *HE* 10.5.2–14). Central policy aim was **freedom of worship empire-wide**. Licinius’s monotheistic army prayer before Maximinus battle was generalized monotheism acceptable to Christians, not necessarily full Christian allegiance. Licinius later restricted assemblies/synods and clerical tax privileges as relations with Constantine deteriorated — Eusebius frames as renewed persecution, Mitchell as security/fiscal measures without Great Persecution-level polarization. Constantine’s 324 victory letters combine Christian triumphalism with emphatic plea for religious tolerance to eastern provincials (*VC* 2.56; 2.60): no forced conversion; temples of “falsehood” may remain.
