## Narrative

The Battle of Sekigahara, fought on the fifteenth day of the ninth month of Keichō 5 (October 21, 1600 by the Western calendar), was the decisive engagement of the Sekigahara campaign and the pivotal event that established Tokugawa Ieyasu's supremacy over Japan. The battle was brief — roughly four hours — but its consequences reshaped the entire political geography of the country.

### Prelude: The Breakdown After Hideyoshi

When [[toyotomi-hideyoshi|Toyotomi Hideyoshi]] died in 1598, his chief anxiety had been the security of his young son Hideyori. He established a board of Five Regents (Tairo) — Tokugawa Ieyasu, Ukita Hideie, Maeda Toshiie, Mori Terumoto, and Uesugi Kagekatsu — along with five Bugyo (commissioners) including [[ishida-mitsunari|Ishida Mitsunari]], to govern until Hideyori came of age.

Tensions surfaced almost immediately. Ieyasu violated Hideyoshi's express prohibition on political marriages by arranging several alliances for his sons and daughters among powerful daimyo, including marrying his sixth son Tadateru to the daughter of Date Masamune. When Ishida complained and the other regents demanded Ieyasu's resignation, the matter was smoothed over through the mediation of Hosokawa Tadaoki and Maeda Toshinaga, who recognized Ieyasu as too powerful to challenge directly.

After Maeda Toshiie died in 1599 — the one Regent capable of checking Ieyasu — seven generals who despised Ishida attempted to assassinate him. Ishida escaped disguised as a woman in a palanquin and, in a remarkable move, sought refuge with Ieyasu at Fushimi. Ieyasu protected him and had him escorted home to Sawayama castle. Sadler notes that Ieyasu's advisor Honda Masanobu had counseled letting Ishida have "as much rope as possible," recognizing him as the best figure to crystallize the opposition while keeping the question of Toyotomi loyalty formally separate.

### Formation of the Coalitions

By 1600 two hostile blocs had hardened. The **Eastern Army** under Ieyasu included Fukushima Masanori, Hosokawa Tadaoki, Kuroda Nagamasa, Ikeda Terumasa, Kato Yoshiaki, Ii Naomasa, Honda Tadakatsu, Asano Yoshinaga, and others — many of them veterans who personally detested Ishida from the Korean campaigns. The **Western Army** nominally led by Mori Terumoto but operationally directed by Ishida Mitsunari from Ogaki included Ukita Hideie, Konishi Yukinaga, Shimazu Yoshihiro, Otani Yoshitsugu, Kobayakawa Hideaki (Matsuo Hill), and Kikkawa Hiroie (Nangu Hill) — though, critically, several of the latter had already opened secret communications with Ieyasu.

Ieyasu's pretext for mobilizing eastward was the Uesugi Kagekatsu affair: Uesugi had been fortifying his Aizu domain with some eighty thousand men working day and night, had refused repeated summons to explain himself to Ieyasu, and his chief councilor Naoe Kanetsugu had written provocative letters dismissing the construction as routine repairs. When Ieyasu marched east in June 1600 — unhurriedly, flying hawks and admiring scenery as he went — Ishida declared war in the west. At a council at Koyama, Ieyasu's eastern allies — including Fukushima, Kuroda, Hosokawa, and Kato — pledged support despite their families being held as hostages by Ishida in Osaka.

The council decided the western theater was primary. Forces loyal to Ieyasu (Date and Mogami) contained Uesugi in the north; Hideyasu held Utsunomiya with twenty thousand men; and Ieyasu turned his main striking force westward.

### The Preliminary Moves

The first strategic objective was the castle of Kiyosu in Owari, which controlled the junction between the Tokaido and Nakasendo roads. Its loyal garrison under "Devil Gemba" (Osaki Gemba) held it against Western overtures, and the Eastern vanguard under Fukushima and Ikeda secured it before Ishida could seize it. Ieyasu himself later credited this loyalty as decisive for the campaign's outcome.

Western-held fortresses at Inuyama, Gifu, and Takegahana fell in rapid succession. Gifu, held by Oda Hidenobu (Nobunaga's grandson), was taken after a fierce assault in which Fukushima and Ikeda nearly came to blows over precedence — Fukushima challenged Ikeda to a duel the evening before the final attack, settled by allocating them different gates.

Meanwhile Ieyasu's old companion Torii Mototada held Fushimi castle — "the last castle Hideyoshi had built" — with eighteen hundred men against assault by the full Western army including Shimazu, Kobayakawa, and Ukita. The garrison held for almost a fortnight, killing three thousand attackers before the castle fell through treachery: a Tokugawa agent holding the Matsu Maru was blackmailed by threats against his family and set fire to his own tower. Mototada led five counterattacks against overwhelming numbers, was reduced to ten men, and killed himself after calling out his attacker's name so the man could respectfully receive his head. His and the other commanders' heads were subsequently displayed at Osaka's Kyobashi bridgehead on a ceremonial stand of white wood as a mark of respect.

Ieyasu's son Hidetada, marching along the Nakasendo with thirty-eight thousand men, delayed himself fatally by stopping to reduce the castle of Ueda, held by Sanada Masayuki. He arrived after the battle was decided. Ieyasu was furious but later pardoned him after Honda intercession, remarking that "these battles are like games of Go."

### The Battle

On the night of October 20, leaving a garrison of seven thousand five hundred at Ogaki, the Western army divisions under Ishida, Shimazu, Konishi, and Ukita made a ten-mile night march along a narrow, steep, cold, and rainy road to take up positions at Sekigahara. Kobayakawa Hideaki's eight thousand men occupied Matsuo Hill on the right flank; Kikkawa Hiroie held Nangu Hill with Mori's forces to the southwest. Ishida personally rode to Matsuo Hill before dawn to instruct Kobayakawa's councilor Hiraoka Yorikatsu to charge down when he lit a signal fire.

**Western dispositions**: Ukita Hideie and Konishi Yukinaga held the center astride the main roads; Shimazu Toyohisa in front and Yoshihiro behind anchored the left; Ishida with his strategist Shima Sakon commanded the right. Otani Yoshitsugu, an experienced general rendered half-blind by leprosy but carried in a palanquin, held a key position facing the Nakasendo alongside Hiratsuka, Toda, Wakizaka, and Ogawa.

**Eastern dispositions**: The Eastern army arrived along the Nakasendo by daybreak and made contact in the mist.

The battle opened around eight o'clock. Ii Naomasa struck Ukita; Fukushima followed close behind. Hosokawa Tadaoki, Kuroda Nagamasa, Kato, and Takenaka attacked Ishida. The Western infantry initially drove back Ii and Fukushima, and the battle "swayed backward and forward without much advantage to either side." Shima Sakon led a forward sortie but was wounded by matchlock fire from Kato and Togawa and had to retire. Terazawa then drove in Konishi's van onto his main force, threw it into disorder, and swung against Ukita's flank.

Through all of this, Shimazu Yoshihiro refused to engage, ignoring repeated requests from Ishida — even when Ishida rode over personally — on the grounds that each commander should fight his own battle.

The crisis came when Ishida lit the signal fire for Kobayakawa to descend. Nothing happened. Otani and Konishi both sent messengers to Matsuo Hill; still nothing. Ieyasu, growing anxious, "bit his fingers hard as he sat on horseback" — his habitual gesture when things looked critical — and ordered his matchlock men to open fire on Kobayakawa's position. The threat worked: Kobayakawa attacked Otani below.

Otani was prepared for this. He repulsed the first attack with his shock troops, then a second. But Ieyasu used Kobayakawa's movement as the signal to order a general attack all along the line. Then Wakizaka, Ogawa, and Kuchigi — who had been placed on Otani's flank by Otani himself against exactly this contingency — also turned against him, joining Kobayakawa in a third assault. Simultaneously, Todo, Kyogoku, and Oda Yuraku attacked Hiratsuka and Toda in the flank. Struck on three sides, Otani's force collapsed. When his men could no longer hold, Otani ordered his retainer Yuasa Gosuke to kill him and hide his head, which was done; Gosuke then killed himself.

Knowledge that they had been betrayed "spread to the other armies and affected their fighting spirit." Konishi's divisions began breaking with cries of "Treachery!" Ukita's force disintegrated; Ukita Hideie himself wished to fight Kobayakawa hand to hand but was overruled by his captains and fled. Of the Western army's substantial forces on Nangu Hill, Kikkawa Hiroie — who had already secretly agreed with Ieyasu to do nothing — simply halted, preventing Nagatsuka, Ankokuji, and Chosokabe from moving. The remnants of Shimazu's fleeing force eventually reached them and reported the battle lost; they then retreated without striking a blow.

Only Shimazu Yoshihiro remained. When most of his men were killed and his brother Toyohisa had fallen, Yoshihiro led the surviving eighty or so in a fighting withdrawal through the Eastern lines, one of his men wounding Ii Naomasa in the left arm at fifteen yards when Ii pressed the pursuit. The Satsuma men reached Osaka by the southwest road and commandeered ships home to Kagoshima.

Around two o'clock Ieyasu saw the victory secured. He removed his brown crêpe silk hood and fastened on a helmet called "White Lining" — giving rise to the Japanese proverb "After victory tighten your helmet" — and proceeded to the ceremonial viewing of heads (*kubi no jikken shiki*). He commended Kuroda Nagamasa first of all: "Today's victory is entirely due to your loyalty and care." Honda Tadakatsu and Fukushima were praised next, and Ieyasu personally bound Ii Naomasa's wounded arm with a bandage from his own writing case.

### Aftermath of the Battle

**Pursuit and execution**: Ishida fled into the mountains around Mount Ibuki, was weakened by three days of eating husks and exposure, contracted dysentery, and was captured in rags. Konishi was apprehended even more quickly; he refused to commit suicide as a Christian. Ankokuji Eikei's hiding place was revealed by a ronin with a grudge. All three were exhibited in Osaka and Sakai as "disturbers of the peace" and then beheaded in the riverbed at Kyoto. Ishida's castle of Sawayama surrendered two days after the battle; his brother and family burned to death in the keep. In the entire castle Ieyasu's men found no money — consistent with Ishida's maxim that "a retainer should spend all he gets from his lord."

**The redistribution**: Ieyasu rearranged domain holdings on a massive scale. Roughly 90 smaller lords lost everything. The greatest losses fell on Mori (reduced from 1,205,000 koku to 369,000, losing his western provinces) and Uesugi (transferred from Aizu at 1,200,000 to Yonezawa at 300,000). Satake was moved from Hitachi to Akita at reduced revenue. Of the "treacherous" Western commanders, Kobayakawa was rewarded with 574,000 koku in Bizen; Kikkawa escaped punishment but Mori's overall holdings were slashed. Shimazu, the most formidable Western fighter but geographically protected, was allowed to retain his revenues (605,000 koku). Eastern allies were rewarded: Kuroda received Chikuzen at 523,000; Fukushima, Hiroshima at 498,000; Kato Kiyomasa increased to 520,000 at Kumamoto; Hosokawa received Buzen at 369,000. Ieyasu's key *fudai* retainers (Ii, Honda, Sakai, Sakakibara) received smaller but strategically placed fiefs controlling roads and passes rather than large revenues — deliberately, as part of controlling the administration without creating rival wealth centers.

In 1602 Ieyasu married his son Hidetada's daughter to Hideyori to maintain a nominal connection to the Toyotomi heir while keeping close surveillance. In 1603 he was formally proclaimed Shogun by the Emperor, establishing the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]].

## Causal Analysis

The proximate cause of the battle was the breakdown of the Toyotomi regency: Hideyoshi's administrative structure (Five Regents + Five Bugyo + three Mediators) lacked any mechanism for resolving the fundamental ambition conflict between Ieyasu and Ishida. The enabling cause was the death of Maeda Toshiie in 1599 — the only figure with sufficient prestige and forces to balance Ieyasu.

Structurally, the campaign illustrates the logic of the [[sengoku-period|Sengoku period]] in its terminal phase: political marriage networks, hostage-holding, and pre-battle defection negotiations proved more decisive than battle-field valor. Ieyasu's systematic work through Kuroda Nagamasa and Hosokawa to neutralize potential Western supporters (especially persuading Maeda Toshinaga not to resist) preceded the military campaign and largely determined its outcome before the first shot was fired.

The Western army's internal contradictions — Shimazu's refusal to cooperate, Kikkawa and Mori's passive betrayal on Nangu Hill, Kobayakawa's pre-negotiated defection — meant that the numerically large Western force was never coherent. Sadler quotes the historian Tokutomi's assessment: "There was no real Commander-in-Chief" on the Western side, while "the very name of Ieyasu seems to have struck one section of it with consternation, while that of Ishida, on the other hand, was so detested by the Easterners that it excited them to fury." Ishida's personal unpopularity — accumulated through the Korean War incidents, his perceived arrogance, and his bureaucratic manner — was a double liability: it weakened Western cohesion while energizing Eastern fighters.

## Consequence Analysis

The battle's immediate consequences were political rather than merely military. The domain redistribution was transformative: it broke the accumulated power of the great western daimyo (Mori, Uesugi, Chosokabe), rewarded the eastern daimyo coalition, and placed Ieyasu's *fudai* retainers in strategic chokepoints throughout the country. Within three years Ieyasu held the Shogunate, the first holder of that office since Ashikaga Yoshiaki (deposed 1575, died 1597).

The longer consequence was the consolidation of the [[tokugawa-shogunate|Tokugawa Shogunate]] as the dominant political institution of Japan for the next 265 years. The domain system (*han*) crystallized out of Sekigahara's redistribution; the distinction between *fudai* (inside vassals) and *tozama* (outside lords) — the latter including the punished western families — became structural to Tokugawa governance.

## Historiography

Sadler's 1937 account draws primarily on Japanese chronicle sources and later Tokugawa-period narratives, and reflects a strong pro-Ieyasu interpretive framework common to pre-war Japanese historiography. The battle is treated as a triumph of strategic patience and political calculation over reckless adventurism. The characterization of Ishida as personally incompetent as a military commander but effective as a bureaucrat aligns with the dominant early twentieth-century Japanese scholarly consensus.

The extent to which pre-battle negotiations — especially with Kobayakawa, Kikkawa, and Mori — predetermined the outcome is a recognized scholarly question. Sadler presents Ieyasu's intelligence network and political maneuvering as the decisive factor, with actual battlefield fighting secondary; this reading is broadly supported by modern Japanese historiography (e.g., Tokutomi's analysis cited in the text). Whether Kobayakawa's defection was genuinely uncertain until Ieyasu fired on him, or was a pre-arranged signal, is contested in the sources.

The Portuguese and other European observers who were present in Japan during this period provide some external perspective. The Dutch ship *Liefde* arrived in April 1600 with its pilot William Adams, who became an advisor to Ieyasu; Sadler notes that the ship's cargo of 500 matchlocks, 5,000 cannon balls, and gunpowder was essentially confiscated by Ieyasu, providing a welcome arms supplement ahead of the campaign.
