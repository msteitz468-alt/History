# Alexander's Invasion of India

## Narrative

Alexander III of Macedon reached the western banks of the River Indus in the spring of
**327 BCE**, having marched through the heart of Iran and across the eastern Persian
satrapies of Drangiana, Arachosia, Bactria, and Sogdiana. His reputation for victories
and cruelties preceded him. The background was the complete collapse of Achaemenid Persian
power: Alexander had defeated Darius III at Gaugamela in 331 BCE, burned Persepolis in
330 BCE, and the last Achaemenid had fled to Bactria where he was murdered. Northwest
India had been part of the Persian empire — the Gandhara satrapy, Sind, and the Indus
basin had contributed legendary revenues to Darius I and his successors.

The **first group Alexander met on South Asian soil** were the Assakenois, who lived on
the borders of the North West Frontier and Kashmir. They resisted but were overcome.
**Gandhara** fell next, its capital Pushkalavati taken. At this point, Alexander received
a significant political gift: the king of **[[taxila|Taxila]]**, Ambhi, whose forces
controlled the lands between the Indus and Jhelum rivers, chose to collaborate. Avari
notes that "Taxila was an ancient city where Vedic culture had flourished for centuries,
and to a certain extent Ambhi's collaboration made sense." His reward was confirmation of
his kingdom within the Alexandrine world empire.

The pivotal confrontation came with **Porus**, the Paurava king who ruled between the
rivers Jhelum (the classical Hydaspes) and Chenab. Porus "has been portrayed as a figure
of heroic grandeur and a worthy opponent of Alexander" — but Avari is blunt that the
portrait is legendary: "The Porus of history nowhere actually matched Alexander in might
and power. He did fight bravely, but his army was massacred and he was decisively beaten."
Alexander rewarded Porus with an extension of territory beyond the Chenab to the Ravi,
"with the strategic aim of protecting his own larger stake in the Indus basin."

Alexander pressed further east to the **River Beas** (which joins the Sutlej to form a
major tributary of the Indus system). This was the turning point. "Reaching the Beas was
the high point of Alexander's military adventure; but however further east he wished to go,
both he and his men realised that it would be a journey without end in the vast Indian
landscape that spread before them." In **July 326 BCE**, his armies refused to go further.
Alexander turned back up the Jhelum and sailed south toward the Arabian Sea.

The return journey was neither triumphal nor easy. The **Mallavas** (hardy frontier
people) offered ferocious resistance. More remarkably, **brahman clans** who "considered
it a part of their dharma or religious duty to fight him" also resisted — Plutarch records
that Alexander had many of them hanged. After reaching the mouth of the Indus, Alexander
devised a three-pronged exit:
- General **Craterus** to return through Arachosia.
- Admiral **Nearchus** to lead the fleet through the Arabian Sea toward the Perso-Arabian
  Gulf.
- Alexander himself to march through the scorching desert of Baluchistan.

Alexander never reached Macedonia, dying in Babylon in **323 BCE**.

## Causal Analysis

The invasion was `caused_by` Alexander's destruction of the Achaemenid Empire, which
had itself controlled northwest India (the Gandhara and Hindush satrapies) since Darius I.
The eastern satrapies lay directly on Alexander's route of conquest. It was also
`contributed_to` by Alexander's personal ambition — his desire to surpass Cyrus and
Darius by reaching the edge of the inhabited world.

The collaboration of Ambhi and the resistance of Porus were both `caused_by` local
political calculations within the competitive landscape of the northwest Indian polities.
The brahman resistance was `caused_by` the dharmic obligation to protect the sacred order
against foreign intrusion.

## Consequence Analysis

The **immediate consequences** were regional: Macedonian control of northwest India's
river valleys for a brief period, and the imposition of Macedonian garrisons and governors.
This control was inherently unstable — the Indian landscape and populations were too vast
and culturally self-sufficient to be lastingly changed. Avari quotes Vincent Smith's
verdict: "India was not Hellenised... and soon forgot the passing of the Macedonian storm."

The **long-term consequences** were more significant. The settlement of Macedonian soldiers
in the northwest over the succeeding decades generated a series of **Indo-Greek kingdoms**
whose cultural impact on Indian art, religion, and trade was lasting. The Gandharan
Buddhist art tradition, with its Hellenistic-influenced sculpture, is perhaps the most
visible consequence. Intellectually, "the intellectual worlds of both Greece and India
also came to know each other better" — Alexander himself "became fond of a naked Indian
ascetic, Kalanos (Kalyana, or 'lucky'), whose wisdom and eccentricity were both instructive
and amusing for him and his soldiers."

The invasion also `enabled` the **[[mauryan-empire|Mauryan Empire]]** indirectly: the
disruption of northwest Indian politics created conditions that [[chandragupta-maurya|
Chandragupta Maurya]] exploited when he overthrew the Nanda dynasty in 321 BCE and began
building the first pan-Indian empire.

Crucially, Alexander's invasion provides one of the earliest **fixed calendar anchors**
for ancient Indian history. The dates of Alexander's campaign are known with precision
from Greek sources, and they allow historians to establish the chronology of the Nanda
dynasty (which was on the throne during Alexander's campaign) and hence to work backward
and forward to date Bimbisara, Ajatashatru, and the pre-Mauryan sequence.

## Actors

**Alexander III of Macedon** (356–323 BCE): Macedonian king who destroyed the Achaemenid
Empire and briefly extended his reach into northwest India. For the Persian Zoroastrians
he was "the accursed"; for Indians he was remembered as "a ruthless aggressor who came
and went, allegedly leaving no lasting imprint on their imagination"; for Europeans he was
"the heroic ideal." Avari remarks: "All three assessments are seemingly correct and yet
false."

**Ambhi of Taxila**: Collaborated with Alexander, receiving confirmation of his kingdom
in return. His collaboration made sense given the competitive politics of the northwest
— Porus was his rival.

**Porus (Paurava king)**: Ruled between the Jhelum and Chenab rivers. Fought Alexander
at the Battle of the Hydaspes but was defeated. Legend endows him with heroic grandeur;
Avari treats this as romanticization. Received extensions of territory as Alexander's
subordinate.

## Historiography

The primary sources for Alexander's Indian campaign are Greek: Arrian's *Anabasis*,
Plutarch's *Life of Alexander*, Strabo's *Geography*, and the accounts of Nearchus
(surviving in fragments). Avari quotes two contrasting passages from Strabo and Plutarch
on Alexander's conduct at Taxila to illustrate the ambiguity: Strabo emphasizes his
generosity; Plutarch records his treachery in massacring Indian mercenaries after a peace
treaty and hanging many brahman philosophers. The Indian sources are largely silent on the
campaign — a significant datum in itself, suggesting that the invasion, momentous from
the Greek perspective, was absorbed into the background noise of northwest Indian politics.

The question of Alexander's long-term impact on India remains debated. The minimal-impact
view (dominant for much of the twentieth century) has been partially revised by scholars
who emphasize the Indo-Greek kingdoms' cultural contribution; but the consensus that India
was not lastingly Hellenized in any deep sense holds firm.
