## Narrative

The battle of Ipsus, fought in 301 BCE on a Phrygian plain, was the decisive
engagement of the [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323|Wars of the Successors]]. The coalition
of [[seleucus-i-nicator|Seleucus]], [[lysimachus]], [[cassander]], and
[[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy]] (the last absent from the field, opportunistically
seizing Coele-Syria instead) confronted the one man still pursuing the reunification
of Alexander's empire, [[antigonus-i-monophthalmus|Antigonus I Monophthalmus]], and
his son [[demetrius-i-poliorcetes|Demetrius Poliorcetes]]. The allies gambled by
abandoning Macedon to an offensive into Asia Minor, forcing Antigonus to recall
Demetrius from Greece.

The battle was decided by the **roughly 400–500 war-elephants** Seleucus had obtained
from the Mauryan [[mauryan-empire|Chandragupta]] (in exchange for ceding the
easternmost provinces). Demetrius' victorious cavalry charge carried too far and was
then screened off from the field by the elephant line, isolating Antigonus' infantry.
The aged Antigonus — over eighty — was killed where he stood. Demetrius escaped with a
fragment of his forces and his fleet.

## Consequence Analysis

Ipsus is the hinge of the period. With Antigonus dead, **the unitary idea died too**:
no later figure pursued the reconstruction of Alexander's empire with serious,
sustained intent. The spoils were partitioned —

- **Lysimachus**: Asia Minor west of the Taurus, founding [[lysimachus|his Anatolian
  kingdom]];
- **Seleucus**: Syria — but [[ptolemy-i-soter|Ptolemy]] had pre-emptively occupied the
  southern half (Coele-Syria) as far as the Eleutherus, refusing to yield it; Seleucus
  let it pass "for now" while reserving his claim. This is the **origin of the
  [[syrian-wars|Syrian Wars]]** that recur through the third century.
- **Cassander**: a free hand in Greece and Macedon;
- **Demetrius**: no kingdom but a fleet and scattered strongpoints — from which he
  would later seize Macedon itself.

Seleucus, now master of the north Syrian *Seleucis*, founded the four cities of the
**Syrian tetrapolis** — Antioch-on-the-Orontes, Seleuceia-in-Pieria, Laodicea, Apamea
— the new heart of his kingdom.

## Historiography

Known mainly through Plutarch's *Demetrius* and Diodorus/Justin (ultimately
[[hieronymus-of-cardia|Hieronymus]]); Seleucus' elephants appear on his coinage. Will
treats Ipsus as the symbolic close of the age of Alexander's would-be heirs and the
true beginning of the territorial-state system. See [[wars-of-the-diadochi-323]],
[[hellenistic-monarchy]].
