# Annexation of the Ryūkyū Kingdom (1875–1879)

The absorption of the Ryūkyū kingdom into the Japanese state — accomplished in stages between 1875 and 1879 — converted the islands' centuries-old dual subordination to both China and Japan into outright Japanese sovereignty. The kingdom was abolished, its king forcibly removed to Tokyo, and its territory designated **Okinawa Prefecture** by imperial decree on March 11, 1879. It was the first territorial annexation of a foreign monarchy by the modern Japanese state, and it established legal and diplomatic precedents used again after the annexation of Korea in 1910.

## Background: Dual Subordination

The Ryūkyū kingdom occupied a peculiar diplomatic position. For centuries it had paid tribute to both the Chinese emperor (through the tribute system) and the Satsuma domain (which had conquered the islands in 1609 but allowed the nominal kingdom to persist for trade advantages). When the Meiji state abolished the domains and incorporated Satsuma, it inherited this ambiguous relationship. The Ryūkyūans' formulation — "Japan is our father and China our mother" — perfectly expressed their situation as a small kingdom between two large powers.

The Japanese, from the outset of the Meiji era, regarded this arrangement as incompatible with modern sovereignty. A kingdom could not serve two emperors any more than "a wife serving two husbands." The [[taiwan-expedition-1874|Taiwan Expedition of 1874]] had already entangled the Ryūkyū question with Chinese sovereignty: China's payment of an indemnity for the killing of Ryūkyū fishermen had implicitly recognized them as Japanese subjects.

## The Squeeze, 1875–1878

From 1875, Japan applied systematic pressure on the Ryūkyū kingdom:

- **July 1875**: Japan ordered King Shō Tai to discontinue tributary relations with China — to stop sending envoys, stop congratulating Chinese emperors on accession, and stop using the Chinese reign-name.
- **1876–1878**: Ryūkyū officials stationed in Tokyo were ordered home; the kingdom's semi-autonomous status was steadily eroded.

The king resisted passively — submitting petitions, citing the difficulty of his position, appealing simultaneously to Japan, China, and Western powers (American, French, and Dutch diplomats all received Ryūkyūan petitions). "No fewer than fourteen petitions were submitted to the Japanese government asking to return to the old form of dual allegiance to both Japan and China" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

In December 1878, Interior Minister **[[ito-hirobumi|Itō Hirobumi]]** decided to end the delay and abolish the domain. He dispatched **Matsuda Michiyuki** (1839–1882), the Interior Ministry's chief secretary, to carry out the operation.

## Matsuda's Mission, 1879

Matsuda arrived in Naha in January 1879 with a clear brief: compel the king to comply or remove him by force. His visit to Shuri Castle on January 26 began with a reading of Prime Minister Sanjō Sanetomi's ultimatum enumerating the kingdom's violations of Japanese orders, followed by a private threat of "extreme measures."

The king's response was dignified but helpless. He explained that if he refused tribute to China, his domain would certainly be punished. His small kingdom, caught between two strong countries, was helpless. He begged for sympathy. Keene's account is notably empathetic: "Shō Tai had never been a commanding figure, but there is something inescapably moving in the spectacle of a king cringing before an official whose sense of mission — disposal of the Ryūkyū problem — left no room for pity."

On **March 11, 1879**, the emperor issued a command abolishing the Ryūkyū domain and directing the king and his heirs to move to Tokyo. Matsuda returned with 160 police and 500 infantry troops. The king, pleading illness, refused to see Matsuda but left the palace on March 11 — "the seat of Ryūkyū authority for 500 years where he had lived his entire life."

A protracted negotiation followed over when the king would travel to Tokyo. The Japanese feared that delay would allow time for Chinese intervention. The crown prince Shō Ten was eventually sent ahead in April 1879 as a kind of advance hostage. The king himself arrived in Yokohama on June 9, 1879, accompanied by his second son and forty retainers. He was received in audience by Emperor Meiji on June 17 and given the junior third rank in the Japanese peerage. Matsuda was decorated for his services.

## Grant's Failed Mediation

During his visit to Japan in the summer of 1879, former U.S. President **Ulysses S. Grant** attempted to mediate the Ryūkyū dispute. He had been asked by China's Prince Kung to raise the question when in Japan, and he did so — at a two-hour private meeting with Emperor Meiji on August 10, 1879, he urged that "Japan in a spirit of magnanimity and justice should make concessions to China." The emperor's response was noncommittal: "As regarding Loo Chu, Itō etc. are authorized to talk with you and will do so shortly."

Grant later wrote to both Iwakura Tomomi and Prince Kung proposing direct negotiations. Direct negotiations did eventually begin in August 1880, and a settlement was tentatively agreed — but China then changed its mind. The Sino-Japanese War of 1894–95 ended any remaining possibility of the question being revisited. Japanese sovereignty over Okinawa was never again contested until the post-World War II U.S. occupation (1945–1972).

## Okinawan Resistance

The absorption of the kingdom did not produce cultural integration. **Sasamori Gisuke**, a mainland Japanese traveler who visited Okinawa in 1893, recorded his observation that the Okinawans still paid conspicuous respect to the deposed royal family — turning out formally along the road when Shō Ten passed, while refusing to attend a banquet hosted by a Japanese imperial prince. "There was not a single instance of an Okinawan marrying a person from 'the other prefectures,' nor was there a single person from 'the other prefectures' who had taken up permanent residence in Okinawa." He added that "the natives' feeling is one of strong attachment to the restoration of the old regime" (Keene, [[keene-emperor-of-japan-meiji-2002]]).

Keene's editorial comment is worth noting: "The annexation of Okinawa hardly figures in histories of Japan, and Shō Tai rates only a brief entry in biographical dictionaries. He was in no sense an important political figure even while a king, and his last thirty years were spent in obscurity. But somehow, even now, there is something affecting about the downfall of a kinglet, deposed by a major country testing its strength at the start of its modern age."

## Significance

The annexation of the Ryūkyū kingdom was Japan's first successful territorial acquisition as a modern imperial state. It demonstrated the template that would be used again: diplomatic pressure, legal argumentation based on prior concessions, the threat and use of force, removal of the ruling family to the Japanese capital, and conversion of the territory into a prefecture with nominal equality but actual subordination. The parallel with Korea after 1910 was explicit — Keene notes that "a precedent for dealing with deposed monarchs, followed after the annexation of Korea, had been established."
