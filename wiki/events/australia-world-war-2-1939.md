# Australia in World War 2

Australia's involvement in World War 2 (1939–45) transformed the nation more profoundly than any event since Federation. Over 1 million Australians served in a population of barely 7 million. The war shifted strategic alignment from Britain to the United States, produced the most ambitious expansion of Commonwealth government powers in Australian history, and laid the institutional foundations for the post-war social settlement.

## Narrative

### Entry and the European War (1939–41)

On 3 September 1939 Prime Minister Robert Menzies announced on national radio his "melancholy duty" to inform Australians they were at war with Germany — invoking British imperial tradition rather than independent decision (Australia had not yet ratified the Statute of Westminster). "The entry into a second world war was greeted with little of the imperialist enthusiasm so evident among Australians in 1914."

Australia was "as ill prepared for war as it had been in 1914." The standing army numbered only 4,000. Defence strategy remained tied to the British naval base at Singapore. Menzies raised the **6th Division of the Second AIF** under Lieutenant-General Sir Thomas Blamey and despatched naval and air forces to the European theatre — notably through the **Empire Air Training Scheme**, under which ~37,000 Australians trained and at least 6,500 were killed over Europe.

In North Africa, the 6th Division fought its first major land battle at Bardia (January 1941), captured Tobruk from the Italians, and pursued the retreating Italians to Benghazi — "in just two months the Allies defeated an Italian army of ten divisions, and over 130,000 Italian soldiers were taken prisoner." The German Afrika Korps then reversed these gains; by April 1941 the **9th Division** (with a brigade of the 7th) was besieged at Tobruk until October, calling themselves the **"Rats of Tobruk."**

The Australian commitment to the **defence of Greece** proved disastrous. Menzies "agreed reluctantly despite concerns about the military risks" to Churchill's plan, even though Blamey "was even summarily consulted" only after British planning was well advanced. Forty per cent of Australian forces sent to Greece were killed, wounded, or captured. The 7th Division then fought in Syria and Lebanon (June 1941).

### Political Transition

Menzies' leadership was undermined by his visit to London (January–May 1941). He returned to find his position untenable and resigned in August 1941 in favour of Arthur Fadden (Country Party). Two independent MPs then switched support to Labor; on 7 October 1941 **John Curtin** was sworn as Prime Minister.

### War in the Pacific (1941–45)

Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941; simultaneously landed in the Philippines, Hong Kong, and Malaya. On 10 December, *HMS Prince of Wales* and *HMS Repulse* were sunk off the Malay coast, shattering the Singapore strategy. Churchill and Roosevelt had already confirmed a "beat Hitler first" strategy in early 1941 — "the Pacific theatre, while absolutely crucial from Australia's perspective, was a secondary concern for Churchill and US president Franklin Roosevelt."

Curtin's New Year message (Melbourne *Herald*, 27 December 1941): "without any inhibitions of any kind, I make it quite clear that Australia looks to America, free of any pangs as to our traditional links or kinship with the United Kingdom." Curtin and Churchill exchanged "a flurry of increasingly acrimonious cables," Curtin warning that loss of Singapore would be "an inexcusable betrayal." **Singapore fell on 15 February 1942** — more than 15,000 members of the 8th Division were among 80,000 Allied prisoners. "A crucial axiom of imperial defence … was shattered."

**General Douglas MacArthur** was appointed Supreme Commander, South-West Pacific Area. The Curtin–MacArthur partnership rested on mutual dependence: MacArthur proposed "You take care of the rear, and I will handle the front."

By the end of March 1942 Japan had conquered Malaya and the Netherlands East Indies; Rabaul and coastal New Guinea towns were lost. The bombing of Darwin on **19 February 1942** killed around 250 people; throughout 1942–43 Japan launched **97 air raids** against towns and military bases in northern Australia. "Contrary to a persistent legend, Japan did not intend a full-scale invasion; its purpose was to isolate Australia and render it ineffective as an Allied base."

The **Battle of the Coral Sea** (May 1942) turned back a Japanese fleet heading for Port Moresby. The **Battle of Midway** (June 1942) decisively shifted Pacific air and naval control to the US. Japan then attempted to reach Port Moresby overland across Papua — leading to the [[kokoda-campaign-1942]].

After 1942 the tide turned. The **9th Division** remained in North Africa and contributed to the Allied victory at **El Alamein** (October 1942). MacArthur's forces (with Australians) drove Japanese from Salamaua and Lae (1943–44). Australian forces were increasingly "mopping up" entrenched Japanese garrisons of limited strategic importance, though they launched assaults in Borneo in the last Allied Pacific campaign. Curtin "was determined that Australia should maintain a military presence to the end of the war as a means of strengthening the nation's voice in the peace negotiations."

Japan surrendered on 15 August 1945; formalised aboard *USS Missouri* on 2 September 1945, with **Blamey signing on behalf of Australia**.

### Home Front

The war transformed Australian domestic life:
- **Manpower controls**: all Australians over 16 registered; prevented from leaving essential industries; Manpower Directorate raided hotels and racecourses for idle workers
- **Rationing**: clothing first (1942), then tea, sugar, butter, meat; pub opening hours reduced; race meetings restricted
- **Income tax centralisation**: Commonwealth took over from States in 1942 (permanent)
- **Women's employment**: Women's Employment Board (1942) awarded women in metal/munitions/aircraft 90 per cent of male wage; 855,000 women in paid work by 1944, 25 per cent of total workforce
- **Women's services**: WAAAF (27,000 members), AWAS, WRANS
- **American presence**: ~1 million Americans passed through Australia 1942–45; peaked at 119,000 in September 1943; MacArthur in Melbourne from March 1942, Brisbane from July 1942; 12–15,000 Australian women became "war brides"
- **Enemy internment**: Dunera Jewish refugees released; Japanese, German, and Italian internees (25,000 total POWs); Cowra breakout 1944

### Casualties and Prisoners

Approximately 34,000 Australians killed — considerably lower than in World War 1. Of 31,000 POWs, 9,000 held in Europe and 22,000 in Asia-Pacific; a third of the latter died in captivity. Changi prison and the Burma–Thai Railway (7,000 of 9,500 Australian prisoners survived) became central to war memory. The Australian War Crimes Act (October 1945) led to ~300 trials of Japanese war criminals.

## Causal Analysis

**Immediate causes** (temporal, not causal): German invasion of Poland; Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor. **Structural causes**: failure of collective security and appeasement in Europe; Japanese militarism and imperial expansion in Asia-Pacific. **Australian specific**: imperial defence commitments that automaticated entry; inadequate defence preparation despite early warning signs from 1937.

## Consequence Analysis

The war permanently expanded Commonwealth government: from 11 departments pre-war to twice as many by 1943; income tax centralised; Commonwealth Bank effectively made central bank; welfare state foundations laid. "The conflict also reshaped government through a permanent expansion of the role of the Commonwealth."

Strategic realignment from Britain to the United States was the defining geopolitical consequence — formalised in the MacArthur partnership and consolidated in postwar ANZUS arrangements (outside this chapter's scope).

## Historiography

Kate Darian-Smith (chapter 4, [[bashford-macintyre-cha-v2-2013]]) is the chapter's author. The "great betrayal" thesis (David Day, 1988) — that Britain deliberately abandoned Australia — is noted but implicitly situated within the wider strategic context of "beat Hitler first." The marginalisation of Australian forces by MacArthur in 1943–44 is noted as a source of discontent. Paul Hasluck's official history (*The Government and the People*, 1952) and David Horner's strategic studies are key scholarly references. The chapter acknowledges the myth of national unity ("despite the emphasis on an 'All In' war effort … differences of class, gender and generation were sharply delineated").
