## Narrative

The Pacific War arrived in the Islands "like a thunderclap" on 7 December 1941 when Japan's air strike against Pearl Harbor initiated simultaneous offensives across the Pacific and South-East Asia. For most Islanders, however, the war had begun as a distant reverberation months earlier: in September 1940 settlers in Noumea and Tahiti had staged coups against pro-Vichy governors and installed Free French administrations, and a few hundred Polynesians, *demis* and Kanaks from the French colonies arrived in the Middle East in mid-1941 as the first Islanders to fight in World War II.

Japan's blitzkrieg in the first six months was breathtaking. The Gilbert Islands at Butaritari fell within days of Pearl Harbor; Guam fell on 10 December; Rabaul was taken by more than 5,000 troops on 23 January 1942; by early March, Lae and Salamaua on mainland New Guinea were under Japanese control. Japan's Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere now encompassed most of South-East Asia, the Gilbert Islands, the Solomons, Australian New Guinea, and parts of Papua. Japan was poised to take Port Moresby by sea—a thrust that, if successful, would have given them the whole island—when US carriers turned them back at the Battle of the Coral Sea in May 1942. The Battle of Midway in June 1942 marked the high-water mark of Japanese expansion.

The Allied counter-offensive was relentless: island-hopping north-west toward Japan, bypassing heavily fortified Japanese bases and cutting their supply lines. By early 1944 the Americans were retaking Japanese-mandated Micronesia beginning with Majuro Atoll in the Marshalls. Bloody battles were fought at Tarawa and Butaritari in the Gilberts, Saipan, Tinian and Guam in the Marianas, and finally at Peleliu in Palau—"the most effective Japanese defence in Micronesia"—where more than 10,000 Japanese died fighting for an island 10 kilometres by 3. Japan surrendered in September 1945. At least 300,000 Japanese had landed in New Guinea and the Solomons; only 127,000 survived.

### Evacuations

The first to flee were European colonisers, whose retreat stripped the veneer of white invincibility. At Kieta on Bougainville Australians decamped at the first sight of a Japanese aircraft, "leaving the town to be looted by villagers." Missionaries in the Solomons "jostled to board the last steamer out of Tulagi in February 1942, abandoning converts and unpaid labourers—who did not forget the betrayal." Ten families of Samoan, Fijian, Tongan and Rotuman missionaries were abandoned by the European Methodists in Papua.

Village populations fled too. The harbour at Port Moresby was "full of canoes, sailing and some rowing with people crying and calling." The people of Nauru were forcibly evacuated by the Japanese to Chuuk Atoll (then Truk), where more than one in three died. One hundred and sixty Islanders remained on Banaba (Ocean Island) in mid-1943; only two survived—Japanese troops massacred the rest in August 1945. The entire Chamorro population of Guam—some 20,000 people—was force-marched to internment camps in July 1944; about 700 Guamanians were executed during the occupation, some by beheading.

### Islanders in the Armed Forces

More than 3,500 Papuans and New Guineans fought in the Pacific Islands Regiment, formed October 1944. Another 3,137 Papua New Guinean police saw action, and 955 served as medical orderlies. More than 2,000 Fijians served in the Solomon Islands campaign; 680 Solomon Islanders enlisted in the Solomon Islands Defence Force. The Tongan Defence Force numbered 2,700 at peak strength. The Tahitian company of the French Battalion of the Pacific—some 300 volunteers—fought in North Africa, Italy, and Provence.

Pre-war settler opinion had been that "natives" would "run at the first sound of bombardment . . . and could not be trusted with firearms." This was comprehensively disproved. The Pacific Islands Regiment "accounted for the deaths of 2,209 Japanese for the loss of just 63 Australians and Papua New Guineans." On Bougainville in the final year of the war, "Japanese soldiers came to fear Bougainvilleans above all." A man from Mambuk village in the east Sepik taught the Australians guerrilla tactics: *"Your European way of fighting should be thrown out, and you must follow the native way . . . Put black paint on the face, or cover it up with mud. Surround the place to be attacked at five o'clock in the morning, then at six o'clock, attack."*

In Fiji, recruiting ran along chiefly lines: the paramount chief (*Roko*) in each province asked the chiefs beneath him, and recruits appeared "each eager to uphold the honour of his people." By August 1943, 6,371 Fijians were in the Fiji Military Forces. Fijian commandos saw action at Guadalcanal, New Georgia, and Vella Lavella, earning praise from American and New Zealand commanders for bravery and jungle skill. The war, however, divided Fiji ethnically: only one Indian—a doctor—served in the Solomons campaign, while only 331 joined the Fiji Labour Corps out of a target of 1,000. Fijian resentment of Indo-Fijian non-participation became "a crucible of Fiji's later political problems."

Despite their contributions, Papua New Guinean soldiers were paid 4d per day with no pensions or compensation, wore distinguishing marks on their laplaps instead of stripes on their arms, and were subjected to corporal punishment. On three separate occasions they "battered down detention centres to liberate fellow soldiers from imprisonment." The commander of the 1st Army in New Guinea signalled that the soldiers "are natural experts in jungle warfare and few Australians ever reach their individual standards." Discharged with "portraits of King George VI or kept on during 1946 to serve as cheap stevedores for the Army, the men of the Pacific Islands Regiment remembered the war with both pride and bitterness."

### Islanders as Labourers

Micronesians were the first labour conscripts, compelled under Japan's Military Manpower Mobilisation Law of 1939 to build airstrips and naval bases. Under martial law from 1941 they could be ordered to work anywhere; *Nan'yō-chō*, the Japanese South Seas mandate, "ended as a forced labour camp." Of 20 Pohnpei men who landed in New Guinea in 1942, 17 died at Buna.

In Papua New Guinea, 37,000 labourers worked for the Australians in mid-1944. The Australian New Guinea Administrative Unit (Angau) was instructed to meet military requirements "even if a temporary sacrifice of native interests is involved." The conscripted carriers on the Kokoda Track—the "Fuzzy-Wuzzy Angels" of Australian wartime legend—bore 40-pound packs across precipitous Owen Stanley ridges, carrying rations to the front and wounded soldiers back. Their conditions were grim: *"Overwork, overloading . . . exposure, cold and underfeeding were the common lot."* Carriers who deserted were hunted by Angau and punished: caned, subjected to pack-drill at a running pace for six hours, or kept in the sun all day. "Carriers who refused to lift loads could expect a punch on the ear."

### The Question of Loyalty

Islander loyalty was situational and rational. In Lae, "the Japanese proved less oppressive than the returning Australians. The Japanese wanted people to stay in the village growing food, whereas the Australians wanted them to carry." A *luluai* (village headman) near Lae explained: *"We thought the Japanese could beat you when you left these places, so we went their way. Afterwards when you bombed and bombed we were doubtful, so we made up our mind to sit in the middle but when you hunt them from these places we will know that you are the stronger."*

Japanese propaganda stressed racial equality, promising that Japan and New Guineans were "brothers who would eat together, and in future Japan would bring factories with all the goods people wanted." Allied propaganda dropped 23 million leaflets, warning collaborators while promising the inevitability of Allied victory.

As Japanese fortunes declined, isolated and starving soldiers turned on Islander populations—stealing pigs, food, and in the Sepik, committing cannibalism. "If a man would not quickly provide yams, taro or saksak [sago] on demand, the soldiers would hang him upside down by a rope around his legs in the centre of the village." Villagers switched sides as the military balance shifted; the Australians conducted witch-hunts against "collaborators," publicly hanging some. One accused man's defence encapsulated the colonial reality: *"The government had deserted us . . . We have been like wives first to the Germans, then to the Australians and finally to the Japanese. We had to submit to the Japanese."*

### Islanders and the New Foreigners

The American military presence transformed the Pacific's social landscape. By mid-1942 approximately 3,500 Americans were at Bora Bora, 8,000 in American Samoa, 5,000 in Western Samoa, 8,700 in Tonga, and 22,000 in New Caledonia. "By the end of the war a million Americans had passed through Manus in the Admiralty Islands." The Seabees (naval construction battalions) transformed landscapes within weeks: half the coconut palms of Nanumea in Tuvalu were destroyed for an airfield occupying one-sixth of the atoll.

The American way struck Islanders with the force of revelation. Described by Islanders as people "both wealthy and generous, unlike the Japanese or any of the colonial Europeans," Americans gave food freely, paid high wages, and—crucially—appeared to treat Black and white servicemen equally. Solomon Islands leader Jonathan Fifi'i recalled: *"no difference between white and black. They had the same jobs, they sat down together, they were equal."* Black American servicemen "put the subservient position of Melanesians into sharp relief and undermined the legitimacy of the colonial order." In Tonga, an American officer had an entire house built for his Tongan girlfriend using military labour.

Colonial authorities struggled to maintain the social code: the Army forbade "sharing taps with Papuans and New Guineans, stopping jeeps to give them a lift, allowing them to address soldiers by Christian names" and "frolicking together in the water." The sharing of food was the central symbolic rupture: *"The test of colour feelings is whether a man will eat with another or not. That is the Melanesian test. No Government official or trader will allow Melanesians to eat with him or even drink a cup of tea with him, for the sake of British prestige."*

## Causal Analysis

The Pacific War reached the Islands as a direct consequence of Japan's strategic expansion (caused_by: japan-imperial-expansion) and the attack on Pearl Harbor (caused_by: [[attack-on-pearl-harbor-1941]]). It was preceded_by the colonial system that had shaped Islander societies and created the social tensions the war would rupture. The war itself was not an Islander war; Islanders were mobilised by exterior powers, but they were not passive recipients—their knowledge, labour, and shifting loyalties shaped the war's conduct.

## Consequence Analysis

The war produced profound and durable transformations:

- **Erosion of colonial legitimacy**: Islanders had "seen Europeans afraid, fleeing in panic," seen "Europeans steal and loot," and "witnessed the collapse of colonial authority." Many did not readily reaccept European authority because "they now knew how different things could be."
- **New political consciousness**: Contact with Americans who paid well, treated Islanders more equally, and criticised colonial conditions created expectations of change. Aituaki Islanders "became sharp critics of New Zealand," leading to post-war political protest under Albert Henry. Pouvanaa a Oopa emerged as a Polynesian nationalist leader partly inspired by his war experience.
- **Maasina Rule**: In the Solomons, wartime exposure to American generosity and equality crystallised into the Maasina Rule movement (1943–onward), the "most effective anti-colonial movement in the entire period of British rule."
- **Cargo and millenarian movements**: The war's display of vast wealth stimulated prophecy and cargo movements across Melanesia (produced: [[cargo-cults-pacific]]).
- **Fiji's ethnic fault-line**: The war "fortified Fijian tradition," rewarded Fijian chiefly loyalty with post-1944 constitutional reforms re-centralising chiefly control, and entrenched the cleavage between Fijians and Indo-Fijians that haunted post-war politics.
- **Trusteeship and decolonisation**: The war shifted international opinion so that "colonies were now seen not as permanent additions to the imperial realms but as territories set upon paths of development that would ultimately lead to self-government or independence." Trusteeship arrangements replaced League of Nations mandates (produced: united-nations-trusteeship-system).
- **Strategic Micronesia**: The Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands entered American trusteeship under a unique "strategic trust" provision permitting military experiments—the legal basis for nuclear testing (enabled: [[pacific-nuclear-testing]]).

## Historiography

The Pacific War's military history was researched almost immediately after the war for official publications—Australian volumes including McCarthy's *South-West Pacific Area: First Year*, Dexter's *The New Guinea Offensives*, and Long's *The Final Campaigns*; the US official naval history (Morison) covered naval operations. These works, as the text notes, "concentrate on Australian and United States citizens respectively, and throw only incidental light on the experience of Islanders."

Islander experience was researched much later. The most useful accounts, per the chapter's bibliographic essay, are Robinson's *Villagers at War*; Read's essay on the Markham Valley; Ravuvu's *Fijians at War*; Laracy's edited *Maasina Rule Movement*; and most comprehensively White and Lindstrom's *The Pacific Theater* (1989). The shift in historiographical attention from soldiers to Islanders represents a decolonisation of the war's historiography, though the Islander record remains incomplete—the history of Islander women in the war, for example, "can be reconstructed only from the most fragmentary evidence."

Key interpretive issues:
- **Loyalty**: The text argues Islanders were neither loyal nor disloyal in colonial terms but rational survival actors whose allegiance tracked military power. This challenges both Allied wartime narratives of the "faithful native" and Japanese propaganda claiming Pacific brotherhood.
- **'Fuzzy-Wuzzy Angels'**: The carriers' achievements were real, but the sentimentalised Australian representation obscured forced labour, corporal punishment, and the colonial power relations that compelled service.
- **Social transformation**: Debate continues over how much the war transformed colonial societies versus accelerating pre-existing tensions. The text treats the war as a catalyst rather than a sole cause.

## Collection Coverage Note

This chapter is drawn from [[denoon-cambridge-pacific-islanders-1997]], a multi-author Cambridge History providing strong regional coverage from an Islander-centred perspective. Coverage is strongest for Papua New Guinea, the Solomon Islands, Fiji, Micronesia, and the French Pacific territories. Coverage of the war's impact on Polynesian societies (Tonga, Samoa, Cook Islands) is lighter but present. The source draws on both official military records and Islander oral testimony, though it notes the fragmentary nature of evidence concerning Islander women and some outer-island communities.
